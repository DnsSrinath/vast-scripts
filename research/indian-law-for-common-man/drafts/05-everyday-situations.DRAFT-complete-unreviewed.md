# Law for the Common Man — 40 Everyday Situations and What the Law Says

*A plain-language guide for citizens of India. Current as of 5 September 2026.*

**How to read this guide:** India's core criminal laws changed on 1 July 2024. The **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS)** replaced the Indian Penal Code (IPC); the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS)** replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC); and the **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (BSA)** replaced the Indian Evidence Act. Throughout this guide, the **new law name and section is given first, with the old IPC/CrPC section in brackets**, because most lawyers, older judgments, and FIRs registered before July 2024 still refer to the old numbers.

**This is general information, not legal advice.** Laws, rules, and helpline numbers change; procedures can vary by state. Always confirm the current position with a lawyer, the relevant government portal, or a legal aid clinic (see Helplines table at the end) before acting on anything here.

## PART 1: HOME & FAMILY

### 1. Registering your marriage
**Situation:** You got married and need a legal marriage certificate for a passport, visa, bank account, or property purposes.
**Law that applies:** Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 (for Hindus, Sikhs, Jains, Buddhists) or the Special Marriage Act, 1954 (for inter-religious/civil marriages, and the only route for two people who don't want a religious ceremony). Muslim, Christian, and Parsi marriages have their own personal laws and registration Acts.
**What you can do:**
1. Both spouses apply to the Sub-Registrar/Marriage Registrar of the district where either lives, usually online via the state's e-registration portal.
2. Carry proof of age (18+ for the bride, 21+ for the groom), address proof, passport photos, and two witnesses with ID.
3. Under the Special Marriage Act, a 30-day public notice is issued before registration; under the Hindu Marriage Act, registration of an already-performed marriage is usually faster.
4. Pay the nominal registration fee (varies by state, typically ₹100–500).
5. Collect the marriage certificate — most states now issue it digitally.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** State marriage registration portals (e.g., Delhi's "Edistrict", Maharashtra's "Aaple Sarkar"); no fixed national deadline to register a marriage, but several states now require it within 30–90 days for certain benefits.
**Myth busted:** "An unregistered Hindu marriage is not legally valid." False — a Hindu marriage is valid once the religious ceremony (saptapadi etc.) is performed; registration is proof, not the source of validity. But without a certificate, you'll struggle with passports, visas, and bank nominations.

### 2. Getting a divorce — grounds and time
**Situation:** A marriage has broken down and one or both spouses want a legal divorce.
**Law that applies:** Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 (Sections 13, 13B) for Hindus; Special Marriage Act, 1954 (Section 27, 28) for civil marriages; Indian Divorce Act, 1869 for Christians; Muslim personal law (talaq, khula, and the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939) for Muslims — "triple talaq" (instant talaq-e-biddat) is criminalised under the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019.
**What you can do:**
1. **Mutual consent divorce**: both spouses file jointly, citing at least one year of separation; wait for two motions spaced roughly 6 months apart (this 6-month "cooling off" period can be waived by courts in genuine cases, per *Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur*, 2017).
2. **Contested divorce**: file on a specific ground — cruelty, desertion (2+ years), adultery, mental disorder, conversion, or irretrievable breakdown (courts increasingly accept this under Article 142 powers, though it isn't yet a statutory ground).
3. Engage a family court lawyer; many High Courts have mandatory mediation before litigation.
4. Settle ancillary issues — maintenance, child custody, and division of assets — often in the same proceeding.
5. A decree becomes final once passed; either party can appeal within the limitation period.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** Mutual consent minimum ~6 months (can be waived); contested divorces often take 2–5+ years. Family courts and District Legal Services Authorities (DLSA) offer free counsel to eligible litigants — helpline **15100/1800-XXX** (state-specific) or **National Legal Services Authority (NALSA) 1800-11-2255**.
**Myth busted:** "You must live separately for a fixed number of years before you can even file." Not true for mutual consent (only "living separately" as a fact, however brief, plus willingness to separate, is required); the 1-year/2-year rules apply to specific fault grounds, not universally.

### 3. Maintenance for wife, children, and parents (and live-in partners)
**Situation:** A wife, child, aged parent, or a woman in a long-term live-in relationship is not being financially supported by the person legally responsible for them.
**Law that applies:** **BNSS Section 144 (old CrPC Section 125)** — a fast, gender-neutral, cross-religion remedy. It covers a wife (including one divorced and not remarried), legitimate/illegitimate minor children, adult children with a disability, and parents unable to maintain themselves. Separately, the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 gives "monetary relief" to women in relationships "in the nature of marriage" — the Supreme Court (*Indra Sarma v. V.K.V. Sarma*, 2013 and later rulings) has extended this to long-term live-in partners meeting certain conditions (cohabitation resembling marriage, both of marriageable age, etc.).
**What you can do:**
1. File an application before the Judicial Magistrate (BNSS 144) or Family Court in the district where you or the respondent live/last lived together.
2. Show the respondent has "sufficient means" and has "neglected or refused" to maintain you.
3. The magistrate can order an interim maintenance amount pending final decision.
4. If maintenance already ordered isn't paid, you can seek recovery as if it were a fine, including attachment of salary or arrest.
5. You can also claim maintenance simultaneously under the Hindu Marriage Act, DV Act, or Special Marriage Act — courts adjust to avoid double payment.
6. Live-in partners should additionally register any DV Act complaint with the local Protection Officer.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** No time bar to file, but courts have directed such applications be disposed of speedily. Women's helpline **181**; NALSA free legal aid **1800-11-2255**.
**Myth busted:** "Only a wife who hasn't remarried can claim maintenance forever." A wife's maintenance stops on her remarriage, but a woman who proves adultery/cruelty against the husband can still claim; and a husband unable to earn (illness/disability) can claim maintenance from a wife who earns, since Section 144 is gender-neutral for spouses in that narrow sense.

### 4. Domestic violence and dowry harassment — filing a complaint
**Situation:** A woman is facing physical, verbal, economic, or sexual abuse at home, or her in-laws are demanding dowry.
**Law that applies:** Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 (civil remedies: protection order, residence order, monetary relief); **BNS Section 85 & 86 (old IPC Section 498A)** for cruelty by husband/relatives (criminal, cognizable, non-bailable); Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961 (giving/taking/demanding dowry is a criminal offence) alongside **BNS Section 80 (old IPC Section 304B)** for dowry death (presumption of guilt if death occurs within 7 years of marriage under unnatural circumstances following dowry harassment).
**What you can do:**
1. Approach the nearest **Protection Officer** (appointed in every district) or a registered service provider/NGO — they must prepare a "Domestic Incident Report."
2. File a complaint at any police station (Zero FIR allowed regardless of where the incident happened) for cruelty/dowry offences, or go directly to a Magistrate for civil relief under the DV Act.
3. Ask for interim protection and residence orders — you cannot be thrown out of the shared household while proceedings are pending.
4. Preserve evidence: messages, medical reports, witness statements, dowry lists/receipts.
5. The Magistrate must fix the first hearing within 3 days of receiving the DV Act application, and is expected to dispose of it within 60 days.
6. For 498A, note the Supreme Court's *Arnesh Kumar* guidelines — police must not automatically arrest and should follow a checklist before arresting in cases carrying up to 7 years' punishment.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** Women's helpline **181** (24x7), National Commission for Women, or **112**. No limitation period for a DV Act application while the domestic relationship subsists.
**Myth busted:** "Filing under 498A/85-86 automatically means the husband and all in-laws get arrested immediately." Courts have repeatedly cautioned against automatic/mechanical arrests in matrimonial cruelty cases to prevent misuse; arrest requires the police to record reasons per BNSS arrest safeguards.

### 5. Inheritance when someone dies without a will
**Situation:** A family member has died intestate (without a will) and you need to know who inherits the property.
**Law that applies:** Depends on religion. **Hindus, Sikhs, Jains, Buddhists**: Hindu Succession Act, 1956 (as amended in 2005). **Muslims**: uncodified personal law (Shariat) — Sunni (Hanafi) and Shia rules differ, with fixed shares for specified heirs. **Christians and Parsis**: Indian Succession Act, 1925.
**What you can do:**
1. For Hindu males dying intestate: property first goes equally to Class I heirs (widow, sons, daughters — sons and daughters equally, since 2005 — and mother); only if no Class I heir exists does it move to Class II heirs (father, siblings, etc.).
2. For Hindu females dying intestate: property generally goes first to her own children/husband, then to husband's heirs, then to her parents, in a specific statutory order.
3. For Muslims: shares are fixed by Shariat — e.g., a wife gets 1/8th (if there are children) or 1/4th (if none); sons generally get double the share of daughters as residuary heirs; consult a lawyer, as calculations are formula-driven and rules differ by sect.
4. For Christians: 1/3rd to the widow/widower and 2/3rds to lineal descendants (or reverse shares if no descendants), per the Indian Succession Act.
5. Apply for a **legal heir certificate** (Tehsildar/Revenue office) for pension/small transactions, or a **succession certificate**/letters of administration from a civil court for movable assets like bank accounts and shares, and mutate immovable property records at the local land/municipal office.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** No fixed deadline to claim inheritance, but delays complicate mutation and can invite adverse possession claims after 12 years (Limitation Act) — don't sit on it.
**Myth busted:** "The eldest son automatically inherits everything." Under Hindu law since 2005, sons and daughters have exactly equal coparcenary rights; there is no "eldest son" privilege in intestate succession.

### 6. Making your own will
**Situation:** You want to decide, in advance, who gets your property after your death.
**Law that applies:** Indian Succession Act, 1925 (Sections 57–191) applies to wills made by Hindus, Christians, Parsis and others (Muslims can also make a will, but generally limited to one-third of their estate under Shariat unless all heirs consent to more).
**What you can do:**
1. Write the will clearly, naming yourself, your assets, and the beneficiaries; you must be of sound mind and at least 18.
2. Sign it in the presence of at least **two witnesses**, who also sign — witnesses should ideally not be beneficiaries, to avoid disputes.
3. Registration is **not compulsory** but strongly advisable — register at the Sub-Registrar's office to prevent later challenges to authenticity.
4. Appoint an executor to carry out its terms.
5. Store the original safely and tell a trusted person/executor where it is; you can revise or revoke it any time before death.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** No deadline — a will can be made and changed anytime while you're alive and of sound mind.
**Myth busted:** "An unregistered will is not valid." False — a will only needs to satisfy the signing/witnessing requirements above; registration only adds evidentiary weight, it isn't mandatory for validity.

### 7. Adopting a child
**Situation:** A couple or individual wants to legally adopt a child.
**Law that applies:** Hindu Adoption and Maintenance Act, 1956 (for Hindus) or the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 read with CARA (Central Adoption Resource Authority) regulations — the JJ Act route is religion-neutral and is the only legal route for non-Hindus (Muslims, Christians, Parsis generally use "guardianship" under the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890, rather than adoption, unless they use the JJ Act route).
**What you can do:**
1. Register on CARA's **CARINGS** portal (cara.wcd.gov.in / cara.nic.in) as a Prospective Adoptive Parent.
2. Complete a home study report by a recognised specialised adoption agency.
3. Get matched with a child; you get a "referral" with the child's medical and background details.
4. Accept the referral, and file a petition before the designated civil/district court for the final adoption order.
5. Once the court order is passed, obtain a new birth certificate reflecting the adoptive parents.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** CARA helpline and portal at cara.wcd.gov.in; timelines vary by child's age/needs but CARA mandates specific reservation and waiting periods for different categories of prospective parents.
**Myth busted:** "You can adopt directly from an orphanage or through a private arrangement without going through CARA." Direct/private adoptions outside CARA's system are illegal and punishable under the JJ Act (child trafficking provisions can apply) — always use the CARINGS portal.

### 8. Senior citizens neglected by their children
**Situation:** Elderly parents are not being cared for or supported by their children, or fear losing their property to them.
**Law that applies:** Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007.
**What you can do:**
1. File an application before the district-level **Maintenance Tribunal** (headed by a Sub-Divisional Magistrate) — no lawyer is required; hearings are meant to be simple and quick.
2. Ask for a monthly maintenance amount from children/relatives who inherit or would inherit your property.
3. The Tribunal must dispose of the application within **90 days** (extendable by 30 days for recorded reasons).
4. If a senior citizen gifted/transferred property to a relative on the condition of being cared for, and that condition is breached, the transfer can be declared **void** and the property reclaimed through the Tribunal.
5. Non-payment of an awarded maintenance can lead to imprisonment of up to one month or until payment.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** Elder helpline **14567** (Monday–Sunday, roughly 8 AM–8 PM); state Social Justice/Welfare department tribunals.
**Myth busted:** "Only children can be made to pay; other relatives are safe." The Act also covers legal heirs and relatives in possession of the senior citizen's property, not just children.

## PART 2: MONEY & CONSUMER

### 9. Cheque bounce
**Situation:** A cheque given to you was dishonoured for insufficient funds or a closed account.
**Law that applies:** Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, Section 138 (criminal liability for dishonour of cheque).
**What you can do:**
1. Present the cheque again within its validity (3 months from the date on the cheque), if you wish, once.
2. On dishonour, send a **legal notice** demanding payment within **30 days** of receiving the bank's dishonour memo.
3. The drawer gets **15 days** from receiving your notice to pay.
4. If they don't pay, file a criminal complaint before the Magistrate within **30 days** after that 15-day period expires (i.e., roughly within 45 days of sending the notice).
5. The case is tried summarily; punishment can be up to 2 years' imprisonment and/or a fine up to twice the cheque amount.
6. The matter is compoundable — you can settle at any stage, including after conviction, on appeal.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** Strict deadlines above — missing them can permanently bar the complaint (though a fresh cheque presentation may reset some timelines). District/local courts; e-Courts portal for tracking.
**Myth busted:** "Cheque bounce cases get you your money back automatically." No — Section 138 is primarily a criminal deterrent; you must still separately pursue civil recovery (or ask the criminal court to order compensation) to actually recover the money.

### 10. Online / UPI fraud
**Situation:** Money has been fraudulently transferred out of your bank account or UPI app.
**Law that applies:** Information Technology Act, 2000 (Sections 66C, 66D and others) plus BNS cheating provisions (Sections 316, 318 — old IPC 420); RBI's 2017 circular on customer liability in unauthorised electronic transactions.
**What you can do:**
1. **Immediately** call your bank's helpline / use the app to block the card/UPI and freeze the account.
2. Report on the **National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal**, cybercrime.gov.in, or call **1930** — the faster you report, the higher the chance of the money being frozen before it's withdrawn.
3. File a written complaint with your bank within the RBI liability window.
4. Under RBI's rules: if you report within **3 working days** of the fraud and it wasn't your fault (e.g., bank system breach, or a third-party fraud with no negligence on your part), you have **zero liability** and get a full refund; reporting between **4–7 working days** caps your liability at a small fixed amount (e.g., ₹5,000–25,000 depending on account type) with the rest refunded; beyond **7 working days**, the bank decides liability per its board-approved policy, and there may be no refund. (Note: RBI has announced revised digital-fraud compensation rules to take effect from January 2027 — check the latest circular closer to that date.)
5. Also file a police FIR/e-FIR for cybercrime — it strengthens the bank claim and criminal case.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** **1930** (24x7 national cybercrime helpline) and cybercrime.gov.in; report to your bank within 3 days for zero liability.
**Myth busted:** "If I shared my OTP by mistake, the bank will never refund me." Not always true — banks are expected to look at the full facts, and speed of reporting matters more than the myth suggests; do not assume you have no recourse, report immediately regardless.

### 11. Defective product or bad service — consumer complaint
**Situation:** You bought a faulty product or received poor service and the seller won't fix it or refund you.
**Law that applies:** Consumer Protection Act, 2019, with pecuniary jurisdiction set by the **Consumer Protection (Jurisdiction) Rules, 2021**.
**What you can do:**
1. First send a written complaint/legal notice to the seller/service provider seeking replacement, repair, refund, or compensation.
2. If unresolved, register a complaint on the **National Consumer Helpline / INGRAM portal** (consumerhelpline.gov.in) for mediation-style resolution — many disputes settle here without going to court.
3. If still unresolved, file a case with the **District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission** for claims up to **₹50 lakh**, the **State Commission** for ₹50 lakh–₹2 crore, or the **National Commission** for claims above **₹2 crore** (these limits were revised downward from the earlier ₹1 crore/₹10 crore thresholds by the 2021 Rules).
4. E-filing is available via the e-Daakhil portal (edaakhil.nic.in).
5. No court fee for claims under a threshold set by rules; consumer commissions are meant to decide matters faster and more informally than regular civil courts.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** **1800-11-4000** or **1915** (National Consumer Helpline, also via WhatsApp on 8800001915); complaints must generally be filed within **2 years** of the cause of action.
**Myth busted:** "You need a lawyer to file a consumer complaint." False — consumer commissions are designed for complainants to represent themselves; a lawyer helps but isn't required.

### 12. Loan default, recovery agents, and harassment by loan apps
**Situation:** You've defaulted on a loan and recovery agents (or a loan app) are threatening or harassing you.
**Law that applies:** RBI's Fair Practices Code for lenders and recovery agents; RBI guidelines on digital lending (2022) for regulated loan apps; BNS provisions on criminal intimidation (Section 351 — old IPC 503) and defamation apply against abusive recovery tactics.
**What you can do:**
1. Recovery agents can only contact you between **7 AM and 7 PM**, must identify themselves, and cannot use threats, humiliation, or contact your family/employer to shame you.
2. If harassed, first complain in writing to the lender's grievance officer/nodal officer.
3. If unresolved within 30 days, escalate to the **RBI Banking Ombudsman/Integrated Ombudsman Scheme** (cms.rbi.org.in) — free of cost.
4. For unregulated or illegal instant-loan apps: they cannot legally recover money through threats — file a police complaint/cybercrime complaint (1930) and report the app to Google Play/Apple for takedown; RBI has directed app stores to only host RBI-regulated lending apps.
5. Genuine default remains a civil debt — non-payment alone is **not** a criminal offence unless there is fraud, cheating, or a bounced cheque involved.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** RBI Ombudsman via cms.rbi.org.in or **14448**; cybercrime **1930**.
**Myth busted:** "Recovery agents can seize your assets or send you to jail for defaulting." Only a court (after due process, e.g., through SARFAESI for secured loans or a civil decree) can authorise seizure of secured assets; agents cannot themselves seize property, detain you, or threaten arrest — simple default is not a crime.

### 13. Insurance claim rejected
**Situation:** Your life, health, or motor insurance claim has been denied or delayed unfairly.
**Law that applies:** Insurance Act, 1938; IRDAI (Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India) regulations and the Insurance Ombudsman Rules, 2017.
**What you can do:**
1. First get the rejection reason in writing from the insurer.
2. File a complaint through the insurer's Grievance Redressal Officer.
3. If unresolved within 30 days (or you're dissatisfied), escalate to IRDAI's **Bima Bharosa** portal (bimabharosa.irdai.gov.in) or call the **IRDAI Grievance Call Centre**.
4. For claims up to ₹50 lakh, approach the **Insurance Ombudsman** (free, informal, binding on the insurer if you accept the award) — there are ombudsman offices across major cities.
5. If still unresolved or for larger amounts, approach the Consumer Commission or civil court.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** IRDAI toll-free **155255** / **1800-4254-732**; Bima Bharosa portal; Ombudsman complaints generally must be filed within **1 year** of the insurer's final rejection.
**Myth busted:** "If my policy has a pre-existing disease clause, any rejection on that ground is final." Insurers must specifically prove non-disclosure was material and intentional; blanket or mechanical rejections are frequently overturned by Ombudsmen and courts.

### 14. Builder delays your flat — RERA
**Situation:** A builder has failed to hand over possession of your flat on the promised date.
**Law that applies:** Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016 (RERA).
**What you can do:**
1. Check that the project is **RERA-registered** on your state's RERA website (registration is mandatory for most projects).
2. Send a written notice to the builder citing the delay.
3. File a complaint with your **State Real Estate Regulatory Authority** — usually online, with a modest fee (often ₹1,000–5,000).
4. Under Section 18, you can either **exit the project and get a full refund plus interest** (often around SBI's MCLR + 2% per annum), or **stay in and get monthly interest** as compensation until possession.
5. If the builder doesn't comply with the RERA order, request **execution** — RERA authorities can recover dues as arrears of land revenue and even attach the builder's property.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** File within roughly **3 years** of the cause of action (delay); most authorities aim to decide within about 60 days, though in busier states cases commonly take several months to over a year.
**Myth busted:** "RERA only applies to under-construction flats bought directly from builders." RERA also covers resale-from-builder-inventory and ongoing projects registered under transitional provisions; it does not, however, typically cover fully completed projects with occupancy certificates already issued before RERA's commencement, or purely resale between two private individuals.

### 15. Income tax and GST basics
**Situation:** You want to know your income tax filing obligations and how GST affects daily purchases.
**Law that applies:** For income earned up to 31 March 2026: **Income-tax Act, 1961**. From **1 April 2026**, the **Income-tax Act, 2025** has replaced it (with largely the same tax rates and policy, but restructured into 536 sections instead of the old Act's 800+, for simplification) — the first returns under the new Act will be filed in 2027 for income earned in FY 2026–27. GST is governed by the CGST/SGST/IGST Acts, 2017, with rate slabs set by the GST Council.
**What you can do:**
1. Choose between the **new tax regime** (lower slab rates, fewer deductions) — the default regime unless you opt out — or the **old regime** (higher rates but exemptions like 80C, HRA, etc.).
2. File your Income Tax Return (ITR) on the incometax.gov.in e-filing portal; the usual due date for individuals without audit requirements is **31 July** of the assessment year (government can and does extend this).
3. Since the **56th GST Council meeting** reforms effective **22 September 2025 ("GST 2.0")**, most goods now fall into a simplified **5% and 18%** two-slab structure (from the earlier four slabs of 5/12/18/28%), with a new **40%** slab for luxury/sin goods; many daily essentials (packaged food, soaps, medicines) moved to nil or 5%.
4. Keep Form 16/26AS/AIS records; mismatches can trigger notices — reconcile before filing.
5. For grievances, use the **e-Nivaran** facility on the income tax portal, or the GST grievance redressal portal (grievance.cbic.gov.in).
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** Income Tax helpline **1800-103-0025** / **1800-419-0025**; GST helpdesk **1800-103-4786**; ITR deadline typically 31 July (verify each year, as extensions are common).
**Myth busted:** "The new Income-tax Act, 2025 changes how much tax I pay." No — the government has stated the new Act largely preserves existing tax rates and policies; it is primarily a simplification/restructuring exercise, not a rate change.

### 16. EPF withdrawal and gratuity on leaving a job
**Situation:** You're leaving a job and want to know what retirement dues you're entitled to.
**Law that applies:** Employees' Provident Fund and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1952; Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972 (both now folded into the Code on Social Security, 2020, which came into force alongside the other labour codes from **21 November 2025**, with detailed central rules notified through 2026).
**What you can do:**
1. **EPF**: your Universal Account Number (UAN) stays the same across jobs — get it activated/KYC-verified (Aadhaar, PAN, bank account linked) on the UAN portal.
2. You can withdraw the full EPF balance if unemployed for **2 months** or more, or transfer it to a new employer's EPF account when you join.
3. Withdrawal is tax-free if you've completed **5 continuous years of service**; if withdrawn earlier, TDS of 10% applies on amounts above ₹50,000 (higher without PAN).
4. **Gratuity**: payable after **5 years of continuous service** with the same employer (courts have allowed this at 4 years 240 days in the 5th year in some cases); calculated as (Last drawn salary × 15 × years of service) ÷ 26, capped currently at **₹20 lakh**. The 5-year rule does not apply if the employee dies or is permanently disabled.
5. File Form 19/10C (EPF) and Form I (gratuity) with your employer/EPFO; if the employer delays, complain to the regional EPFO Grievance portal (EPFiGMS).
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** EPFO helpline **1800-118-005**; EPFiGMS grievance portal (epfigms.gov.in).
**Myth busted:** "If I resign, I lose my gratuity." You only lose it if you haven't completed the minimum 5 years' service (except in cases of death/disability) or if terminated for proven misconduct causing loss to the employer — ordinary resignation after 5 years doesn't forfeit it.

## PART 3: WORK

### 17. Minimum wage and salary not paid
**Situation:** Your employer is paying below minimum wage, or hasn't paid your salary at all.
**Law that applies:** Payment of Wages Act, 1936 and Minimum Wages Act, 1948 — both now subsumed into the **Code on Wages, 2019**, part of the four Labour Codes notified into force from **21 November 2025** (with implementation rules being finalised through 2026).
**What you can do:**
1. Minimum wages are fixed separately by each state government for different job categories/skill levels — check your state labour department's notified rates.
2. First raise the non-payment in writing with your employer/HR.
3. File a complaint with the local **Labour Commissioner/Inspector** — this is a fast, largely free administrative remedy.
4. You can also approach the Labour Court for recovery of unpaid wages, plus a penalty/compensation for delay.
5. For an unpaid salary, you generally have up to **3 years** to claim (limitation periods are being reviewed under the new Wage Code, so check the latest rule).
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** State labour department helplines and Shram Suvidha portal (shramsuvidha.gov.in); central Ministry of Labour helpline **1800-11-6060 (Sankalp)**.
**Myth busted:** "If I signed an appointment letter accepting a lower wage, I can't claim minimum wage." False — minimum wage is a statutory floor; any private agreement to pay below it is void to that extent.

### 18. Fired without notice
**Situation:** You were terminated from your job without notice or without following proper procedure.
**Law that applies:** Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 (for "workmen") — now largely folded into the **Industrial Relations Code, 2020**; for others, your employment contract and the applicable Shops & Establishments Act.
**What you can do:**
1. Check whether you qualify as a "workman" under labour law (broadly, non-supervisory/non-managerial employees) — protections differ for managerial staff, who rely mainly on their contract.
2. Ask for the termination in writing with reasons.
3. If notice pay or retrenchment compensation (typically 15 days' average pay per year of service, for eligible employees with 1+ year of service) wasn't paid, raise a dispute with the Labour Commissioner/Conciliation Officer.
4. If conciliation fails, the dispute can be referred to a Labour Court/Industrial Tribunal.
5. For establishments with 100+ workmen (or the state-specific threshold, since some states have raised this), prior government permission is required before retrenchment/closure — check if this applied to you.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** Approach the Labour Commissioner promptly — undue delay can weaken your case, though there's no single universal limitation period across all remedies.
**Myth busted:** "An employer can fire anyone at will as long as they pay one month's salary." Not for "workmen" under labour law — proper procedure (notice, retrenchment compensation, "last come first go" principle in many cases, and government permission where applicable) must be followed, not just payment in lieu of notice.

### 19. Sexual harassment at the workplace (POSH)
**Situation:** An employee is being sexually harassed by a colleague, manager, or client at work.
**Law that applies:** Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013 (POSH Act).
**What you can do:**
1. Every workplace with **10 or more employees** must have an **Internal Committee (IC)**; smaller/unorganised workplaces are covered by a district-level **Local Committee (LC)**.
2. File a written complaint with the IC/LC within **3 months** of the incident (or the last incident, if a series) — the Committee has discretion to extend this by another **3 months** (so up to 6 months total) if satisfied there was good reason for the delay.
3. The IC must complete its inquiry within **90 days** and the employer must act on its recommendation within **60 days** of the report.
4. You can request interim relief — such as transfer of either party or leave — during the inquiry.
5. If unsatisfied with the outcome, you can appeal to a court/tribunal as specified under the Act, generally within 90 days of the recommendation.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** Women's helpline **181**; Ministry of Women and Child Development's **SHe-Box** portal (shebox.wcd.gov.in) for direct online complaints, including against Central Government entities.
**Myth busted:** "POSH only protects permanent female employees against male colleagues." It also covers women who are interns, contract workers, clients, or visitors at the workplace, and complaints can be against anyone regardless of their seniority — though the Act itself is specifically for the protection of women.

### 20. Maternity leave
**Situation:** A woman employee wants to know her maternity leave entitlement.
**Law that applies:** Maternity Benefit Act, 1961, as amended in 2017.
**What you can do:**
1. If you've worked at least **80 days in the 12 months before your expected delivery date**, you are entitled to paid maternity leave.
2. For the first two children: **26 weeks** of paid leave (up to 8 weeks can be taken before delivery, the rest after).
3. For the third child onward: **12 weeks**.
4. Adopting mothers (child under 3 months) and "commissioning mothers" (surrogacy) get **12 weeks** from the date of adoption/handing over of the child.
5. Employers with 50+ employees must provide a crèche facility and allow the mother 4 visits a day to it; "work from home" post-leave can be mutually agreed where the nature of work allows.
6. It is illegal to dismiss or vary the terms of employment of a woman because of pregnancy or maternity leave.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** Labour Commissioner's office for violations; Ministry of Labour helpline **1800-11-6060**.
**Myth busted:** "Maternity benefit only applies to permanent employees, not contract or probationary staff." The Act's protection applies to any woman employee meeting the 80-day threshold, regardless of permanent/contract/probation status, as long as the establishment is covered by the Act.

### 21. Gig workers, contract workers, and the new Labour Codes
**Situation:** You work for an app-based platform (delivery, cabs), as a contract/temporary worker, or want to know what's changing under India's new labour laws.
**Law that applies:** The **Code on Social Security, 2020** (gig and platform workers), **Industrial Relations Code, 2020**, **Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020**, and **Code on Wages, 2019** — collectively the **four Labour Codes**, brought into force from **21 November 2025**, with detailed Central Rules notified progressively through 2026 (check current status, as state rules are still being finalised in many states).
**What you can do:**
1. **Gig/platform workers**: register on the government's gig-worker portal once notified (this builds on e-Shram registration) to access welfare schemes — life/disability cover, accident insurance, health/maternity benefits — funded partly by a mandatory contribution (1–2% of turnover) from aggregator platforms.
2. **Contract workers**: are entitled to the same wages/conditions as regular workers doing similar work at the same establishment under the Code on Wages' "equal work" principle, and to statutory benefits like EPF/ESI if the contractor is covered.
3. **ESI (Employees' State Insurance)**: if you earn below the notified wage ceiling (periodically revised) and your establishment is covered, you and your employer contribute to ESI, entitling you to free/subsidised medical treatment, sickness benefit, maternity benefit, and disability/dependent benefits.
4. Working hours are generally capped around **8–9 hours a day / 48 hours a week**, with overtime to be paid at roughly **double the ordinary wage rate** for hours worked beyond the limit — exact thresholds are being finalised in the new Codes' rules, so confirm current limits with your state labour department.
5. If benefits are denied, complain to the Shram Suvidha portal or the ESIC regional office.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** Shram Suvidha portal (shramsuvidha.gov.in); ESIC helpline **1800-11-2526**; e-Shram helpline **14434**.
**Myth busted:** "Gig workers (Uber/Ola/Swiggy/Zomato-type) are 'employees' with full labour law protection like a company employee." Under the current Code on Social Security, gig/platform workers are treated as a distinct category with specific welfare-scheme benefits (insurance, etc.), not the full bundle of "employee" protections (like ESI, gratuity, or the Industrial Disputes framework) that apply to regular employees — this remains a contested and evolving area.

## PART 4: POLICE & CRIME

### 22. Filing an FIR / Zero FIR — and what if police refuse
**Situation:** You want to report a crime, but you're unsure of the process, or the police at your local station are refusing to register it.
**Law that applies:** **BNSS Section 173 (old CrPC Section 154)** for FIR registration; the **Zero FIR** concept (an FIR registered at any police station regardless of jurisdiction, later transferred to the right station) is now given statutory backing in BNSS; **BNSS Section 175(3) (old CrPC Section 156(3))** for magistrate-directed investigation when police refuse.
**What you can do:**
1. For a **cognizable offence** (serious crimes like theft, assault, rape), the police **must** register an FIR — registration is mandatory, not discretionary (*Lalita Kumari v. Govt. of UP*, 2013).
2. You can file an FIR at **any police station** (Zero FIR) if the crime occurred elsewhere — it must then be transferred to the appropriate station.
3. You can also file an **e-FIR** for certain categories (like vehicle theft or lost property) via your state police's online portal, in many states.
4. If police refuse to register: first send a written complaint to the **Superintendent of Police (SP)** — BNSS now requires this step be tried before approaching a Magistrate.
5. If that fails, move an application before the Judicial Magistrate under **BNSS 175(3)** to direct the police to register the FIR/investigate. Under BNSS, the Magistrate must first hear the concerned police officer's side before ordering this, but rejection of your application doesn't stop you from pursuing other remedies.
6. You always have the option to file a **private criminal complaint** directly before the Magistrate.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** Police emergency **112**/**100**; you should get a free copy of the FIR immediately after registration.
**Myth busted:** "Police can refuse to register an FIR saying 'it's a civil matter' or 'go get more evidence first.'" For cognizable offences, this refusal is illegal — the Supreme Court has made FIR registration mandatory in such cases; police can only conduct a brief preliminary inquiry (not investigation) in specific limited categories like matrimonial/commercial disputes before deciding to register.

### 23. Your rights if you are arrested
**Situation:** You or someone you know has been arrested by the police.
**Law that applies:** **BNSS Sections 35–58 (old CrPC Sections 41–60)**; Article 22 of the Constitution.
**What you can do:**
1. You have the right to know the **grounds of arrest** and, in cognizable non-bailable cases, satisfy yourself the arrest is genuinely necessary (police must record reasons, especially for offences punishable up to 7 years — following the *Arnesh Kumar* guidelines).
2. You must be informed you have a right to inform a friend/relative of your arrest and whereabouts (a mandatory duty on the arresting officer).
3. **Women cannot ordinarily be arrested after sunset or before sunrise** — an exception requires prior written permission from a Judicial Magistrate, and any search of a woman must be conducted by a female officer.
4. You must be produced before the nearest Magistrate within **24 hours** of arrest (excluding travel time) — this is an absolute constitutional right under Article 22(2)/BNSS Section 58.
5. You have the right to consult a lawyer of your choice, and legal aid free of cost if you cannot afford one.
6. Police **cannot arbitrarily seize your phone**; while investigating agencies can seize devices as evidence under due process (with a seizure memo, and — per emerging Supreme Court guidance — reasonable safeguards on searching digital devices), it must follow a documented, justified procedure, not an unrestrained "hand over your phone" demand.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** Police control room **112**; NALSA free legal aid **1800-11-2255**; note the police station and time of arrest, and the identity/badge number of officers if possible.
**Myth busted:** "Police can detain and question you for as long as they like before producing you in court." The 24-hour production rule is absolute — every day, including weekends and holidays; failure to comply makes further detention illegal.

### 24. Bail — bailable, non-bailable, anticipatory bail, and undertrial release
**Situation:** You or a family member is facing arrest or is already in custody, and you want to understand bail options.
**Law that applies:** **BNSS Section 478-484 (old CrPC Sections 436-450)** for regular bail; **BNSS Section 482 (old CrPC Section 438)** for anticipatory bail; **BNSS Section 479 (old CrPC Section 436A)** for undertrial release.
**What you can do:**
1. In a **bailable offence**, bail is a matter of right — the police or court must grant it on furnishing a bond/surety.
2. In a **non-bailable offence**, bail is at the court's discretion, considering the offence's severity, flight risk, and possibility of tampering with evidence.
3. **Anticipatory bail** (Section 482 BNSS) can be sought from the Sessions Court or High Court **before** arrest, if you apprehend arrest in a non-bailable case; BNSS has removed some earlier restrictive conditions, giving courts wider discretion than under the old Section 438.
4. **Undertrial release (Section 479 BNSS)**: a **first-time offender** who has served **one-third** of the maximum sentence for the offence (not applicable to death/life-sentence offences, or where multiple cases are pending) must be released on bond; for others (repeat offenders), the threshold remains **half** the maximum sentence. Jail superintendents have a statutory duty to move this application on your behalf.
5. If bail is granted but you can't afford the sureties/bond, ask the court to reduce the amount or accept a personal bond — courts are directed to avoid keeping poor undertrials in jail merely because they can't furnish sureties.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** NALSA free legal aid **1800-11-2255**; e-Courts portal to track case/bail status.
**Myth busted:** "Anticipatory bail means you can never be arrested for that case." Anticipatory bail is protection against arrest specifically for the apprehended offence, subject to conditions (like cooperating with investigation) — breach of those conditions, or a different/more serious charge, can change the position.

### 25. Hit-and-run and road accident compensation
**Situation:** You (or a family member) were injured, or a family member killed, in a road accident — either by an identified driver who fled, or in a general accident.
**Law that applies:** **BNS Section 106 (old IPC Section 304A)** — causing death by negligence generally carries up to 5 years; **BNS Section 106(2)** specifically penalises a driver who causes death by rash/negligent driving and **flees without reporting** to police/magistrate, with punishment up to **10 years**. Separately, the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 (as amended in 2019) governs compensation via Motor Accident Claims Tribunals (MACT), and the **Compensation to Victims of Hit and Run Motor Accidents Scheme, 2022** covers cases where the vehicle/driver cannot be traced.
**What you can do:**
1. Report the accident to the police immediately — an FIR is essential for both the criminal case and any compensation claim.
2. For accidents where the driver/vehicle is known (or insured), file a claim with the **Motor Accident Claims Tribunal (MACT)** in the relevant district for compensation — no need to prove fault for basic "no-fault liability" compensation in death/serious injury cases.
3. For **untraceable hit-and-run** vehicles, apply under the **2022 Hit and Run Scheme** through the District Magistrate/designated authority — compensation is a fixed **₹2,00,000 for death** and **₹50,000 for grievous injury**, and this is meant to be processed in a time-bound manner via the Motor Vehicle Accident Fund.
4. Preserve medical records, FIR copy, and treatment bills — needed for both the tribunal claim and hit-and-run scheme.
5. The Supreme Court has directed authorities to proactively inform victims/families about the compensation scheme's existence, since many remain unaware and never claim it.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** Police/ambulance **112**/**108**; MACT claims should generally be filed as soon as possible (courts have relaxed strict limitation given the compensatory nature of the claim, but don't delay).
**Myth busted:** "If the driver who hit me can't be found, I get nothing." The 2022 Hit and Run Scheme exists precisely for this — you can claim the fixed compensation even without identifying the driver.

### 26. Traffic challans and drunk driving
**Situation:** You've been issued a traffic challan, or stopped for suspected drunk driving.
**Law that applies:** Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, as amended by the Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 2019 — which significantly raised fines from 1 September 2019.
**What you can do:**
1. Know your rights: you can ask to see the traffic officer's ID; challans should generally be issued electronically wherever e-challan infrastructure exists, reducing scope for on-the-spot cash demands.
2. Pay e-challans online via the Parivahan portal (echallan.parivahan.gov.in) or state transport department app — avoid cash payments to officers where digital payment is available.
3. Common post-2019 fines include (indicative — states can vary some amounts): driving without a licence up to ₹5,000; overspeeding ₹1,000–2,000; no seatbelt ₹1,000; no helmet ₹1,000 plus 3-month licence disqualification; dangerous driving up to ₹5,000; drunk driving **₹10,000 and/or up to 6 months' imprisonment** for a first offence, **₹15,000 and/or up to 2 years** for a repeat offence within 3 years.
4. You can contest a challan in the designated traffic court (Virtual/e-Courts handle many minor challans now) instead of paying if you believe it's wrongly issued.
5. Vehicles can only be impounded/documents seized following proper procedure — not arbitrarily.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** Parivahan e-challan portal; traffic police helpline (varies by city, often **103** or a local number).
**Myth busted:** "Traffic police can demand you pay the fine in cash on the spot or they'll seize your vehicle." Except in specific situations, most challans should be issued electronically and can be paid online later; on-the-spot cash collection without a receipt is not standard procedure and you can insist on a proper challan.

### 27. Theft, snatching, and cyberstalking
**Situation:** Your phone/bag/vehicle was stolen or snatched, or you're being persistently followed/monitored online or offline.
**Law that applies:** **BNS Section 303 (old IPC Section 379)** for theft; **BNS Section 304 (old IPC Section 356)** covers snatching as a specific offence for the first time under BNS; **BNS Section 78 (old IPC Section 354D)** for stalking (following/contacting a woman despite her disinterest, or monitoring her internet/email/electronic communication).
**What you can do:**
1. File an FIR immediately — for theft/snatching, this is essential for insurance claims and to block/trace a stolen phone via the **CEIR (Central Equipment Identity Register)** on the Sanchar Saathi portal.
2. Use Sanchar Saathi (sancharsaathi.gov.in) to block a lost/stolen phone by its IMEI number so it can't be used on any network even with a new SIM.
3. For stalking, preserve all evidence — messages, call logs, screenshots — and report to the local police or the **Cyber Crime portal (cybercrime.gov.in)** for the online-monitoring component.
4. Stalking is a cognizable offence, punishable with up to 3 years for a first conviction and up to 5 years for repeat offences.
5. You can also seek a protection order from a Magistrate if the stalking amounts to harassment/threat to safety.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** Police **100/112**; Sanchar Saathi portal for lost phones; cybercrime **1930**/cybercrime.gov.in for the digital-stalking angle.
**Myth busted:** "Stalking laws only apply to strangers, not ex-partners or people you've met." The law applies regardless of any prior relationship — persistent unwanted contact/monitoring after clear disinterest is an offence either way.

### 28. Defamation, mob lynching, and false promise of marriage
**Situation:** Someone has published damaging false statements about you; or you're aware of a mob attack based on identity; or a woman was sexually exploited on a false promise of marriage.
**Law that applies:** **BNS Section 356 (old IPC Sections 499–502)** for criminal defamation; **BNS Section 103(2) (new provision, no direct IPC equivalent)** for mob lynching — murder by a group of 5 or more people motivated by race, caste, community, sex, place of birth, language, or similar grounds, punishable with **death or life imprisonment**; **BNS Section 69 (new provision)** criminalises sexual intercourse obtained by deceitful means, including a false promise of marriage, punishable with up to **10 years** imprisonment and a fine.
**What you can do:**
1. **Defamation**: you can pursue a criminal complaint (BNS 356 — a non-cognizable, bailable, compoundable offence, so you must approach a Magistrate directly, or the police need Magistrate permission to investigate) or a **civil defamation suit** for damages — these can run in parallel.
2. **Mob lynching**: report to police immediately (cognizable offence); the enhanced BNS 103(2) punishment specifically targets group violence motivated by identity-based hatred, distinct from ordinary murder/culpable homicide provisions.
3. **False promise of marriage (BNS 69)**: file an FIR — courts have cautioned this provision should be applied where the promise was false **from the very beginning** (fraudulent intent), not in cases where a genuine relationship later broke down for other reasons.
4. In all these cases, preserve evidence (screenshots, messages, videos, witness details) as early as possible.
5. Seek legal aid if needed — NALSA provides free counsel for many categories of victims.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** Police **100/112**; for defamation, limitation to file a criminal complaint is typically **3 years** from the act (general limitation for offences with this punishment range, per the Limitation Act principles applied to criminal complaints).
**Myth busted:** "Every failed relationship where there was a promise to marry counts as a criminal offence." Courts require proof the promise was **false from inception** with fraudulent/dishonest intent to induce sexual relations — a relationship that broke down later due to a genuine change of circumstances is not automatically a crime under BNS 69.

## PART 5: PROPERTY & HOUSING

### 29. Buying a property — checks before you pay
**Situation:** You're about to buy a house, flat, or land and want to avoid fraud or future disputes.
**Law that applies:** Transfer of Property Act, 1882; Registration Act, 1908; Indian Stamp Act, 1899 (and state stamp Acts); RERA, 2016 for under-construction properties.
**What you can do:**
1. Verify the **title** — get a lawyer to check the chain of ownership documents (sale deeds) going back at least 12–30 years depending on the state/type of property.
2. Get an **Encumbrance Certificate (EC)** from the Sub-Registrar's office to confirm there's no existing mortgage, lien, or pending litigation on the property.
3. Check **land records** online (Bhulekh/Bhu-Naksha or your state's equivalent portal) to confirm the seller is the recorded owner and the land use classification matches your purpose (e.g., agricultural vs. residential).
4. For apartments, check the project's **RERA registration** and the builder's track record on the state RERA website.
5. Pay the correct **stamp duty** (varies by state, often 3–8% of property value) and **registration fee** (usually ~1%), and register the sale deed at the Sub-Registrar's office — an unregistered sale of immovable property above a threshold value is not legally valid.
6. After registration, get the property **mutated** in your name in municipal/revenue records — this is separate from registration and is needed for tax and future sale purposes.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** State Sub-Registrar offices; land records portals (Bhulekh, DLR, DHARANI, etc., named differently per state); no fixed deadline but delaying mutation can cause complications.
**Myth busted:** "A registered sale deed alone proves clear ownership." Registration only records the transaction; it doesn't guarantee the seller had valid, undisputed title — always do independent title verification.

### 30. Tenant vs landlord rights
**Situation:** You're renting out or renting a property and there's a dispute over rent, eviction, or security deposit.
**Law that applies:** State-specific Rent Control Acts (most states still use older laws) and, where adopted, the **Model Tenancy Act, 2021** — a central model law that states can choose to adopt. As of now, only a handful of states/UTs (including Uttar Pradesh, Assam, and a couple of others) have formally ratified/notified it; most states continue under their own older rent laws, so **check your specific state's law**.
**What you can do:**
1. Always sign a **written rent agreement**; the common "11-month" agreement is a practice (not a legal mandate) to avoid the mandatory registration and higher stamp duty that apply to leases of 12 months or more in most states.
2. Under the Model Tenancy Act (where adopted), security deposit is capped at **2 months' rent for residential** and **6 months' for commercial** property, refundable within a set period after vacating.
3. Landlords generally cannot evict a tenant without due legal process — even where the lease has expired, forcible eviction (cutting power/water, changing locks) is illegal; the landlord must approach the Rent Court/Rent Authority or civil court.
4. Tenants must pay rent on time and cannot sublet without the landlord's written consent (unless the agreement allows it).
5. For disputes, approach the local **Rent Authority/Rent Controller** (where established) or civil court; Model-Tenancy states now have dedicated fast-track Rent Courts/Tribunals.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** State Rent Control/Housing department; no universal national deadline — check your state's specific eviction notice periods (commonly 1–3 months).
**Myth busted:** "The Model Tenancy Act, 2021 automatically applies across India." It does not — it is a model framework, and only takes legal effect in a state once that state formally adopts/notifies it; most of India is still governed by older, state-specific rent laws.

### 31. Ancestral property rights of daughters
**Situation:** A woman wants to know her rights to her father's/family's ancestral (Hindu joint family) property.
**Law that applies:** Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005, and the Supreme Court's clarification in ***Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma* (2020)**.
**What you can do:**
1. Since the 2005 amendment, a daughter is a **coparcener by birth**, exactly like a son, in Hindu Mitakshara joint family property — with equal rights to claim a share, seek partition, and inherit.
2. *Vineeta Sharma* (2020) clarified this applies **retrospectively**: a daughter has coparcenary rights **even if her father died before the 2005 amendment**, as long as the daughter was alive on 9 September 2005 (the amendment's commencement date) — the father need not have been alive on that date.
3. This resolved earlier conflicting judgments (*Prakash v. Phulavati* and *Danamma v. Amar*) that had wrongly required the father to be alive in 2005.
4. If denied your share, you can file a **partition suit** in civil court claiming your equal share as a coparcener.
5. Note: this rule specifically concerns Hindu ancestral/joint family property; self-acquired property of a parent who dies **with a will** goes as per that will, not automatically by coparcenary rules.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** No specific limitation for asserting coparcenary status, but a partition suit is subject to the general 12-year limitation for possession-related claims — don't delay if your share is being denied.
**Myth busted:** "A daughter only gets equal rights if her father was alive in 2005 or if she was married before/after a certain date." *Vineeta Sharma* squarely rejected both conditions — marital status and the father's date of death are irrelevant to a daughter's coparcenary right by birth.

### 32. Encroachment, land records, and society/RWA disputes
**Situation:** Someone has encroached on your land, you need to check/correct land records, or you're in a dispute with your housing society/Resident Welfare Association (RWA).
**Law that applies:** BNS provisions on criminal trespass (Section 329, old IPC 441/447); Transfer of Property Act; state Cooperative Societies Acts / Apartment Ownership Acts for RWAs; the **SVAMITVA Scheme** (2020) for rural property record digitisation.
**What you can do:**
1. For encroachment: first verify your boundaries via a government **survey/demarcation** (Patwari/Tehsildar for rural land; municipal survey for urban); if confirmed, send a legal notice, then file a civil suit for **injunction/possession**, and separately a **criminal complaint for trespass** if forceful/ongoing.
2. Use **Bhulekh** (or your state's equivalent land-records portal) to check/download your Record of Rights (RoR)/7-12 extract/Khatauni online, and flag discrepancies to the local revenue office.
3. Under **SVAMITVA**, rural residential property owners can get a legal **property card** using drone-mapping — check if your village has been surveyed, as this gives you documentary proof of ownership useful for loans and disputes.
4. For RWA/society disputes (maintenance charges, common area misuse, elections), first raise it in a general body meeting; unresolved disputes go to the **Cooperative Societies Registrar** or, for larger disputes, the state Cooperative Tribunal/Consumer Commission (RWAs providing services for a fee can sometimes be treated as "service providers" under consumer law).
5. Keep all communications in writing/email for evidence.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** State revenue department helplines; Cooperative Registrar's office; encroachment/possession disputes are subject to the 12-year adverse possession limitation — don't let an encroachment sit unchallenged.
**Myth busted:** "If someone occupies my land for a long time, I automatically lose it immediately." Only after **12 years of open, continuous, and hostile possession** (adverse possession) without your objection could a claim potentially arise — acting promptly (legal notice, police complaint, or civil suit) protects your title.

## PART 6: GOVERNMENT & DOCUMENTS

### 33. Filing an RTI application
**Situation:** You want information from a government department — file status, spending details, reasons for a decision, etc.
**Law that applies:** Right to Information Act, 2005.
**What you can do:**
1. Write a simple application addressed to the **Public Information Officer (PIO)** of the relevant department, stating clearly what information you want.
2. Pay the application fee — **₹10** for central government applications (state fees vary, e.g., ₹20 in Gujarat, ₹30 in some states) — fee is **waived for BPL applicants**.
3. File online via **rtionline.gov.in** (for central government bodies) or your state's RTI portal, or by post/in-person for state departments not yet online.
4. The PIO must respond within **30 days** (reduced to **48 hours** if it concerns life or liberty).
5. If you get no response or an unsatisfactory one, file a **first appeal** (free) with the department's Appellate Authority within **30 days** of the PIO's response (or of the 30-day deadline expiring).
6. If still unresolved, file a **second appeal** (also free) with the State/Central Information Commission within **90 days**.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** rtionline.gov.in helpdesk; State/Central Information Commissions; deadlines as above.
**Myth busted:** "RTI can be used to ask any question, including opinions or hypotheticals." RTI only compels disclosure of existing "information" (records, documents, data) held by a public authority — it cannot compel an official to answer opinion-based questions, create new information, or explain their reasoning if that reasoning isn't already recorded.

### 34. When Aadhaar, Voter ID, PAN, and Passport are mandatory — and grievance portals
**Situation:** You're unsure when Aadhaar can legally be demanded, or need help with voter ID/PAN/passport issues or a general government grievance.
**Law that applies:** Aadhaar (Targeted Delivery of Financial and Other Subsidies, Benefits and Services) Act, 2016, as interpreted in ***Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2018)**; various state Right to Public Services Acts; the Central Public Grievance Redress and Monitoring System (CPGRAMS).
**What you can do:**
1. Per the 2018 Puttaswamy judgment, Aadhaar is **mandatory** for filing income tax returns and obtaining a PAN, and for accessing **government welfare subsidies/benefits** funded from the Consolidated Fund of India.
2. Aadhaar is **not mandatory** (per that judgment) for opening a bank account or getting a mobile SIM card as a blanket private-sector requirement — though banks/telecom companies may still accept it as one valid ID/KYC option among others (e-KYC via Aadhaar is voluntary, not compulsory).
3. For voter ID, apply/correct details via the **Voter Helpline app** or **voters.eci.gov.in**; for passports, use **passportindia.gov.in**; for PAN, use the **NSDL/UTIITSL/incometax.gov.in** portals — most states also have **Right to Public Services Acts** guaranteeing specific documents within a fixed number of days (varies by state and service, e.g., often 7–21 days), with a penalty on the responsible officer for delay.
4. For a stuck grievance with any Central/State government department, file it on **CPGRAMS** (pgportal.gov.in), which is meant to be tracked to resolution.
5. For pension delays, use the department-specific pension grievance portal (e.g., **CPENGRAMS** for central government pensioners) alongside CPGRAMS.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** CPGRAMS (pgportal.gov.in); Voter Helpline **1950**; Passport Seva helpline **1800-258-1800**; PAN/Income tax **1800-103-0025**.
**Myth busted:** "A private shop, school, or company can lawfully refuse me service unless I show Aadhaar." Aadhaar cannot be made compulsory by private entities as a blanket condition for services, per the Puttaswamy judgment — you can offer any other valid government ID instead, in most such contexts.

### 35. Ration card, NFSA entitlements, and MGNREGA job cards
**Situation:** You want to access subsidised food grains or claim rural employment guarantee work.
**Law that applies:** National Food Security Act, 2013 (NFSA); Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act, 2005 (MGNREGA).
**What you can do:**
1. Apply for a ration card through your state's Public Distribution System (PDS)/Food & Civil Supplies department portal; NFSA guarantees subsidised foodgrains (typically 5 kg per person per month) to eligible "priority" households, and additional entitlements to "Antyodaya" (poorest) households.
2. Use "**One Nation One Ration Card**" portability to access your quota from any Fair Price Shop across India, not just your home state.
3. For MGNREGA: any adult member of a rural household can apply for a **job card** at the Gram Panchayat — it must be issued within **15 days**, and demanding work entitles you to it within another 15 days.
4. Every registered rural household is guaranteed **100 days of wage employment per year**; if work isn't provided within 15 days of demanding it, you're entitled to an **unemployment allowance** (roughly a quarter of the wage rate for the first 30 days, half thereafter, funded by the state government).
5. Track your job card, work demanded, and wages paid on the **nrega.nic.in** MIS portal — wages must be paid within a fixed period (typically 15 days) and delayed payment attracts compensation.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** NFSA/ration card grievance — state food department helplines/toll-free numbers (vary by state, generally listed as **1967** or **14445** on many state PDS portals); MGNREGA helpline **1800-11-0707**.
**Myth busted:** "MGNREGA only guarantees work if it's actually available near your village." The scheme legally **guarantees** work (or the unemployment allowance in lieu) — the government cannot simply say no work is available and leave it at that.

### 36. RTE admissions and birth/death registration
**Situation:** You want your child admitted under the Right to Education quota, or need to register a birth or death.
**Law that applies:** Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 (RTE); Registration of Births and Deaths Act, 1969, as amended by the **Registration of Births and Deaths (Amendment) Act, 2023** (in force since 1 October 2023).
**What you can do:**
1. Under RTE, private unaided schools must reserve **at least 25%** of entry-level seats for children from disadvantaged/weaker sections, with fees reimbursed by the government — apply through your state's RTE admission portal during the designated window (usually announced each academic year).
2. RTE guarantees free and compulsory education for children aged **6 to 14** in neighbourhood schools.
3. Register a birth or death within the local municipal/panchayat office (or hospital, if born there) — ideally within **21 days**, though late registration is possible (with an affidavit/additional process) up to a year, and beyond a year via a court/magistrate order in most states.
4. Since the 2023 amendment, the **birth certificate is now a single mandatory document** for admission to schools, issuing a driving licence, preparing voter lists, Aadhaar enrolment, marriage registration, and government job appointments — records are maintained through the digital **Civil Registration System (CRS)**.
5. Download digital birth/death certificates via your state's CRS portal (crsorgi.gov.in or state equivalent).
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** State education department RTE helpline (varies); CRS/municipal helpdesk for births/deaths; register a birth/death within 21 days to avoid extra paperwork.
**Myth busted:** "RTE's 25% quota is available in every private school, including elite unrecognised institutions." It applies specifically to private **unaided, recognised** schools (and some categories of aided schools) — unrecognised institutions and minority-run unaided schools have some specific exemptions/variations under RTE case law.

## PART 7: DIGITAL

### 37. Your rights under the Data Protection Act (DPDP)
**Situation:** A company or app is misusing your personal data, or you want to know your privacy rights online.
**Law that applies:** Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act) — its core operative provisions (data-processing obligations and individual rights, Sections 3–17) come into effect **18 months** after the notification of the DPDP Rules, 2025 (notified 13–14 November 2025), meaning the substantive rights are being phased in through 2027; the Data Protection Board of India (DPBI) itself and related provisions are already functional from November 2025.
**What you can do:**
1. As a "Data Principal," you will have the right to be informed clearly what personal data is collected and why (via a plain-language notice at the time of taking consent).
2. You can **withdraw consent** at any time — as easily as you gave it.
3. You can request **access** to your data, **correction** of inaccurate data, and **erasure** once the purpose for collecting it is served.
4. If a company (Data Fiduciary) doesn't honour your request, first complain to its designated **Grievance Officer**; if unresolved, escalate to the **Data Protection Board of India**, which can impose penalties on companies for violations (penalties under the Act can run into hundreds of crores for serious breaches).
5. Since these substantive rights are being phased in, check the current commencement status of Sections 3–17 closer to when you need to act — as of today, enforcement of the core rights provisions is not yet fully live even though the framework and Board exist.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** Data Protection Board of India (once fully operational); MeitY's grievance channels in the interim; check meity.gov.in for the latest notification on which sections are in force.
**Myth busted:** "The DPDP Act is already fully enforceable against every company today." Not entirely — while the Board and certain provisions are functional from November 2025, the core data-processing obligations and individual rights are on a staggered ~18-month rollout, so full enforcement is still being phased in as of late 2026/2027 — verify current status before assuming a right is enforceable today.

### 38. Cybercrime: hacking, deepfakes, SIM fraud, phishing, and takedowns
**Situation:** You've been hacked, targeted by a deepfake, had your SIM swapped/misused, been phished, or want objectionable content taken down.
**Law that applies:** Information Technology Act, 2000 — **Section 66** (computer-related offences/hacking), **66C** (identity theft), **66D** (cheating by impersonation), **66E** (violation of privacy, e.g., non-consensual intimate images, increasingly applied to deepfakes), **67/67A** (publishing obscene/sexually explicit material, with enhanced punishment for repeat offences and for content involving children under 67B); IT Rules, 2021 (as amended) for takedown/grievance obligations on platforms; **BNS** cheating and defamation provisions also apply to phishing and deepfakes as relevant.
**What you can do:**
1. Report any cybercrime — hacking, deepfakes, phishing, SIM fraud, or online harassment — at **cybercrime.gov.in** or by calling **1930**; there are specific categories for "cyberbullying," "hacking," "impersonation," and "sexually explicit content."
2. For **SIM card fraud/misuse**: use the **Sanchar Saathi** portal (sancharsaathi.gov.in) to check how many SIMs are registered in your name (TAFCOP), block a lost/stolen phone via IMEI (CEIR), and report fraudulent calls/SMS via the **Chakshu** facility.
3. For **deepfakes/non-consensual content**: report directly to the platform (most major platforms now have dedicated deepfake-reporting tools under IT Rules obligations) demanding takedown within the mandated timeframe (typically 24–72 hours depending on content category), and simultaneously file a police complaint citing Section 66E/67A of the IT Act.
4. For **phishing**: never click suspicious links; if compromised, immediately change passwords, enable two-factor authentication, and report to your bank and cybercrime.gov.in.
5. Platforms must appoint a **Grievance Officer** under the IT Rules — you can escalate content-removal requests to them, and then to the **Grievance Appellate Committee (GAC)** set up by the government if unsatisfied.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** **1930** and cybercrime.gov.in (24x7); Sanchar Saathi for SIM/device issues; content-takedown timelines under IT Rules are typically 24–72 hours once validly reported.
**Myth busted:** "There's no law against deepfakes in India, so nothing can be done." While India doesn't yet have a dedicated standalone "deepfake law," existing IT Act provisions (66E, 66D, 67/67A), BNS provisions (cheating, defamation, outraging modesty), and the 2023 IT Rules amendments on synthetic/AI-generated content together already provide real, usable remedies.

## PART 8: ENVIRONMENT & PUBLIC

### 39. Noise, garbage, stray dogs, and other civic complaints
**Situation:** You're dealing with excessive noise (loudspeakers, construction), uncollected garbage, stray dog issues, or illegal tree felling/pollution in your area.
**Law that applies:** Noise Pollution (Regulation and Control) Rules, 2000 (under the Environment Protection Act, 1986); Solid Waste Management Rules, 2016 (municipal duty to collect/manage waste); Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981; state Tree Preservation Acts (permission generally required before felling); and, for stray dogs, the **Animal Birth Control Rules, 2023** together with the **Supreme Court's 2025 orders** on stray dog management.
**What you can do:**
1. **Noise**: permissible limits are typically **55 dB (day) / 45 dB (night) in residential areas** and lower in "silence zones" (near hospitals/schools/courts) — complain to local police (noise pollution is a cognizable offence they can act on) or the State Pollution Control Board; loudspeakers generally need prior written permission and cannot be used late at night except with specific permits (e.g., during festivals, subject to local rules).
2. **Garbage**: municipal corporations have a statutory duty to collect, segregate, and dispose of waste — complain via your city's municipal helpline/app (most now have one) or the **Swachhata App**.
3. **Stray dogs**: following the Supreme Court's August 2025 order (as modified from an initial stricter August 11, 2025 order), municipal authorities must capture, sterilise, and vaccinate stray dogs and **release them back to their original territory** (not permanently confine them), except dogs that are rabid or aggressive. **Public feeding of strays is now restricted to designated, marked feeding areas** set up by municipal authorities in each ward — feeding outside these areas can attract legal consequences under the current directions; check for the latest pan-India policy, as the Court has extended proceedings to cover all states/UTs.
4. **Tree felling**: generally requires prior permission from the local Forest/Municipal Tree Officer — illegal felling can be reported to the Forest Department or via your state's forest helpline.
5. **Air/water pollution**: complain to the State Pollution Control Board (SPCB) or the **National Green Tribunal (NGT)** for serious/industrial violations — the NGT can order compensation and closure of polluting units.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** Local municipal helpline/app; SPCB regional office; police **112** for noise complaints at night; NGT (application generally within limitation, but urgent environmental harm can be raised promptly).
**Myth busted:** "Feeding stray dogs anywhere is now completely banned by the Supreme Court." Not an outright ban — feeding is meant to be **channelled into designated feeding spots** identified by municipal authorities, not eliminated altogether; check your municipality's notified feeding zones.

### 40. Public Interest Litigation (PIL) — how any citizen can approach the courts
**Situation:** You've noticed a public wrong — an unimplemented law, environmental harm, corruption, or a violation affecting many people — and want to approach a court, even though you're not personally the victim.
**Law that applies:** Article 32 (Supreme Court) and Article 226 (High Courts) of the Constitution — the basis for PIL, developed through judicial innovation since the late 1970s/80s (notably by Justices P.N. Bhagwati and V.R. Krishna Iyer) to let public-spirited citizens seek justice for those who cannot approach courts themselves.
**What you can do:**
1. A PIL can be filed by **any citizen or organisation**, even a **simple letter** to the Chief Justice (the "epistolary jurisdiction" tradition), though a properly drafted writ petition is more effective in practice today.
2. File directly with the **High Court** (under Article 226 — often faster and preferred for state/local issues) or the **Supreme Court** (Article 32 — reserved for violations of fundamental rights, or matters of pan-India importance).
3. Court fees for PILs are typically nominal; many High Courts and the Supreme Court have simplified filing (including e-filing portals).
4. Courts screen PILs to prevent misuse for personal/publicity/business rivalry ("busybody" litigation) — genuine public interest, not personal grievance, must be shown.
5. Courts can pass wide-ranging directions in a PIL — from ordering government compliance with existing law, to laying down interim guidelines (as seen in the stray dogs and other recent suo motu matters) until Parliament/the Executive legislates.
**Helpline/portal/time limit:** Supreme Court e-filing portal (sci.gov.in); respective High Court websites; District Legal Services Authority for guidance if you can't afford a lawyer (**NALSA 1800-11-2255**); no fixed limitation period for PILs generally, though courts expect reasonably prompt action once you become aware of the wrong.
**Myth busted:** "PILs are only for big NGOs, celebrities, or lawyers." Ordinary citizens have successfully filed PILs — courts have historically converted even postcards and newspaper reports into PIL proceedings when they revealed a genuine public wrong.
