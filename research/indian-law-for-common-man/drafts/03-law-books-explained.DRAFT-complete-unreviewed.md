# India's Big Law Books, Explained in Simple English

*A plain-language guide for ordinary citizens. Written 5 September 2026. All "current status" notes are checked against public sources listed at the end.*

---

## Before You Start: Five Words You Need

**Act.** A law passed by Parliament (for the whole country) or by a State Assembly (for one state). "Act" and "law" mean roughly the same thing here.

**Code / Sanhita / Adhiniyam.** These are just other names for a big Act. "Sanhita" and "Adhiniyam" are Hindi words the government started using for the new criminal laws in 2023. A "Code" usually means one big book that pulls many scattered rules into one place.

**Section.** A numbered paragraph inside an Act. When someone says "he was booked under Section 103", they mean paragraph number 103 of that law book. Sections are grouped into **Chapters**.

**Bare Act.** The plain text of the law, with nothing added. No opinions, no explanations. Just the law as written. You can buy one for Rs 50 to Rs 300, or read it free online.

**Came into force.** The date a law actually starts working. A law can be passed in one year and start working in a later year. Passing is not the same as starting.

**A caution about numbers.** Section counts change every time Parliament amends a law. The counts below are the commonly cited figures. Treat them as "about this many", not as gospel.

**A caution about advice.** This guide tells you what laws are *for*. It is not legal advice for your own case. For a real problem, talk to a lawyer, a legal aid clinic, or your District Legal Services Authority (free help, every district has one).

# PART 1 — CRIMINAL LAW

Criminal law is about acts the State treats as offences against society: theft, assault, murder, cheating, drugs, and so on. The police investigate, a government prosecutor argues the case, and a court decides. The punishment is jail, fine, or both.

India replaced its three main criminal law books on **1 July 2024**. This was the biggest change to Indian criminal law since the British era.

---

## 1. Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS)

**(a) Full name and year.** The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023. The name means roughly "Indian Justice Code". Act No. 45 of 2023.

**(b) What it replaced.** The Indian Penal Code, 1860 (IPC) — a law written by the British 164 years earlier, which had 511 sections in 23 chapters.

**(c) What it covers, in plain words.** This is the book that lists **what counts as a crime and what the punishment is**. If you want to know whether something is an offence in India, and how many years of jail it carries, this is the book. It covers crimes against the body (murder, hurt, kidnapping), crimes against women and children (rape, stalking, sexual harassment), crimes against property (theft, robbery, cheating, criminal breach of trust), crimes against the State (terrorism, waging war), and crimes against public peace (rioting, unlawful assembly). Most of the old IPC rules were kept, but the section numbers all changed and some new offences were added.

**(d) Five things a common person should know.**

1. **The section numbers you grew up with are gone.** Murder was IPC 302; it is now **BNS 103**. Cheating was IPC 420; it is now **BNS 318**. Rape was IPC 376; it is now **BNS 64**. If an old FIR or an old news report mentions "302", it means murder under the old law. Police now write "103 BNS".

2. **Mob lynching is now a named crime.** If a group of five or more people kills someone because of their caste, race, community, sex, language, place of birth or personal belief, every person in that group can be punished — up to life imprisonment or death (BNS Section 103(2)). Earlier this had to be squeezed into the general murder section.

3. **Community service is now a punishment.** For the first time in Indian criminal law, a court can order you to do unpaid public work instead of jail. It applies to small offences — for example, a first-time petty theft of property worth under Rs 5,000 where the property is returned, attempting suicide to obstruct a public servant, public drunkenness causing annoyance, and non-appearance after a public servant's summons. So a young person caught in a minor offence may sweep a hospital rather than sit in jail.

4. **Hit-and-run has a heavier punishment.** Causing death by rash or negligent driving carries up to 5 years. But if the driver **runs away without reporting** to the police or a magistrate, the punishment goes up to **10 years** (BNS Section 106(2)). Important: this harsher sub-section was held back after truckers protested, and as of 2026 it had not been brought into force separately — the 5-year rule applies. If you are ever in an accident, stopping and reporting is both the right thing and the legally safer thing.

5. **"Sedition" is gone, but something similar took its place.** The old sedition law (IPC 124A) punished "disaffection towards the Government". BNS drops that word. But **Section 152** creates a new offence: acts endangering the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India — exciting secession, armed rebellion, or subversive activities, including through spoken or written words or electronic communication. Punishment: up to life imprisonment. Critics say it is sedition under a new name; the government says it targets threats to the nation, not criticism of the government. Either way, forwarding certain content on WhatsApp can attract it, so think before you forward.

6. **Two other new offences worth knowing.** *Organised crime* (Section 111) and *petty organised crime* (Section 112) — covering gangs, kidnapping-for-ransom, contract killing, land grabbing, and also pickpocket gangs and exam-paper leak rings. And *sexual intercourse by deceitful means* (Section 69) — where a man has sex with a woman on a **false promise of marriage**, or by hiding his identity, punishable up to 10 years.

**(e) Size.** 358 sections in 20 chapters (down from IPC's 511 sections in 23 chapters).

**(f) Status as of September 2026.** In force since **1 July 2024**, all over India. The exception noted above — Section 106(2), the 10-year hit-and-run provision — was deliberately not notified. Two years in, government reviews report better timeline compliance by police, but training and forensic laboratory capacity remain the weak points.

**Important transition rule:** crimes committed **before** 1 July 2024 are still tried under the old IPC. So the IPC has not vanished — it will keep running in courts for years for old cases. Both books matter for now.

## 2. Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS)

**(a) Full name and year.** The Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 — roughly "Indian Citizen Protection Code". Act No. 46 of 2023.

**(b) What it replaced.** The Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 (CrPC), which itself replaced an 1898 law. The CrPC had 484 sections.

**(c) What it covers, in plain words.** If BNS says *what* is a crime, BNSS says *how the system handles it* — step by step. How an FIR is registered. When police can arrest you and what your rights are. How bail works. How a search or seizure is done. How a trial runs, who gives evidence when, and how a judgment is delivered and appealed. It is the rulebook for police, prosecutors, magistrates and judges. For a citizen, it is the book of **your procedural rights** when you are a victim, a witness, or an accused.

**(d) Five things a common person should know.**

1. **Zero FIR — no police station can refuse you on "not our area".** If a crime happened anywhere in India, you can walk into *any* police station and they must register your complaint. It is registered with number "0", then transferred to the station that actually has jurisdiction. Earlier, victims were often sent away with "this is not our area". Now that refusal is illegal.

2. **e-FIR — you can report electronically.** You can send information about an offence by electronic means, without physically going to the station. Catch: you must sign the FIR within **three days** for it to be taken on record. Also, you have a right to a **free copy of the FIR**.

3. **Video recording of searches and seizures is mandatory.** When police search a place or seize property during an investigation, the process must be recorded on audio-video — a mobile phone is allowed — and the recording sent to the magistrate. This protects you against planted evidence and protects honest police against false allegations. Insist on it and note whether it was done.

4. **There are now hard timelines.** The system used to drift for years. BNSS puts clocks on it: police must inform the victim or complainant of investigation progress within **90 days**; in serious cases (punishable with 10 years or more, life, or death) the chargesheet must generally be filed within **90 days**; a magistrate must decide on taking a case forward within a fixed window; and a **judgment must be pronounced within 30 days of arguments ending, extendable to 45 days** with reasons recorded. Roughly 145 timelines run through the law.

5. **Trial in absence, and a rule about jail time already served.** A declared absconder can now be tried and convicted even when he is not present in court — so fugitives cannot stall a case forever. Separately, an undertrial (someone in jail waiting for trial) who has served **one-third** of the maximum sentence, if it is a first offence, is generally entitled to bail. Earlier the bar was one-half. This matters enormously for poor undertrials.

6. **Forensics is compulsory for serious crimes.** For offences punishable with 7 years or more, a forensic expert must visit the crime scene and collect evidence, with videography. This is being rolled out state by state as laboratory capacity builds.

**(e) Size.** 531 sections in 39 chapters (CrPC had 484 sections).

**(f) Status as of September 2026.** In force since **1 July 2024**. It runs alongside the Interoperable Criminal Justice System (ICJS), which links police, courts, prisons, forensics and prosecution, plus apps like e-Sakshya (evidence), e-Summons and Nyaya Shruti. Government reviews after two years report 90-day chargesheet compliance rising from about 40% to about 60%. Old cases begun before 1 July 2024 continue under the old CrPC procedure.

## 3. Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (BSA)

**(a) Full name and year.** The Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 — "Indian Evidence Act". Act No. 47 of 2023.

**(b) What it replaced.** The Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (167 sections).

**(c) What it covers, in plain words.** This is the book of **what a court is allowed to believe**. Not every statement is evidence. This law decides which documents count, which witnesses count, when hearsay ("someone told me") is allowed, who has to prove what, and what happens when there is no direct proof. It is short and technical, but it decides most cases in practice — many cases are lost not because the truth was different but because the proof was not admissible.

**(d) Four things a common person should know.**

1. **Electronic records are now full-fledged documents.** WhatsApp messages, emails, CCTV footage, server logs, call records, information on a phone, laptop or cloud account — these are treated as **primary evidence** in their own right, not as a second-class copy. This is the single biggest change.

2. **You still need a certificate for electronic evidence.** Under **Section 63** (which replaced the famous Section 65B of the old Act), an electronic record produced in court normally needs a signed certificate — from the person in charge of the device and from an expert — saying where it came from and that it was not tampered with. The certificate format is in a Schedule to the Act. Practical lesson: a screenshot alone is often not enough. Keep the original device, and get the certificate.

3. **Burden of proof stays where it always was.** The person who asserts something must prove it. In a criminal case, the prosecution must prove guilt **beyond reasonable doubt** — the accused does not have to prove innocence. In a civil case the standard is lower: "on the balance of probabilities", meaning more likely than not.

4. **Confessions to police are still not evidence.** A confession made to a police officer cannot be used to convict you. A confession before a magistrate, recorded properly, can be. This old protection was kept.

**(e) Size.** 170 sections, 12 chapters, arranged in 4 parts (the old Act had 167 sections).

**(f) Status as of September 2026.** In force since **1 July 2024**. Courts have been actively interpreting Section 63 certificates, and this is an area where the law is still settling.

## Other Criminal Laws Everyone Should Know (one paragraph each)

These are **special laws**. They sit alongside BNS and override it where they conflict.

**POCSO Act, 2012 — Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012.** Protects everyone under 18, boys and girls equally, from sexual assault, sexual harassment and pornography. It is gender-neutral about victims. Key points: **reporting is compulsory** — if you know a child has been sexually abused and you do not report it, you can be punished (up to 6 months). The child's identity must never be revealed. Trials are held in Special Courts, in a child-friendly way, ideally within one year. The accused is presumed guilty unless he proves otherwise on certain points — a reversal of the normal rule, because child victims can rarely produce proof. About 46 sections in 9 chapters. In force since 14 November 2012; amended in 2019 to add the death penalty for aggravated penetrative sexual assault.

**NDPS Act, 1985 — Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1985.** The drugs law. It punishes production, possession, sale, transport and consumption of banned drugs. Punishment depends on **quantity**: small quantity (up to 1 year), more than small but less than commercial (up to 10 years), and commercial quantity (10 to 20 years plus a heavy fine, and bail is very hard to get). What most people do not know: this law also covers medicines misused as drugs, and being caught with a commercial quantity puts the burden on **you** to prove you are innocent. About 83 sections. In force since 14 November 1985; amended in 1989, 2001, 2014 and 2021.

**Arms Act, 1959.** Controls guns and ammunition. You need a licence to own or carry a firearm, and the licence names what you may keep and where. Carrying a licensed gun outside the permitted area, or lending it, is an offence. Illegal possession of a prohibited weapon can bring 7 years to life. Also relevant to ordinary people: it covers air guns above certain power and some knives notified by state governments. About 46 sections; supported by the Arms Rules, 2016. In force since 1 October 1962.

**Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988.** The anti-bribery law for public servants. It punishes taking a bribe (called "undue advantage"), and since the **2018 amendment it also punishes the person who gives the bribe** — up to 7 years. There is a safety valve: if you were forced to pay a bribe and you report it to a law enforcement agency within **seven days**, you are protected. It also covers disproportionate assets — a government servant whose wealth cannot be explained by his known income can be prosecuted. Prior government approval is now needed before investigating a serving public servant. About 31 sections. In force since 9 September 1988; heavily amended in 2018.

**Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 (PWDVA).** A **civil** law, not a criminal one — its purpose is protection and relief, not primarily jail. Any woman in a domestic relationship (wife, mother, sister, daughter, live-in partner) can approach a Magistrate. "Domestic violence" is defined widely: physical, sexual, verbal, emotional and **economic** abuse, including dowry harassment. What she can get: a **protection order** (stop the abuse, stay away), a **residence order** (a woman cannot be thrown out of the shared household even if she does not own it), a **monetary relief order** (maintenance, medical costs), a **custody order** for children, and compensation. Every district has a **Protection Officer** to help file the application. Breaking a protection order **is** a crime — up to 1 year. 37 sections in 5 chapters. In force since 26 October 2006.

**Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961.** Makes giving, taking or **demanding** dowry an offence — minimum 5 years jail and a fine of Rs 15,000 or the value of the dowry, whichever is more, for giving or taking; 6 months to 2 years for demanding. Advertising a dowry demand is also an offence. Important detail: gifts given voluntarily at a wedding without demand are allowed, but they must be **listed in a signed list** maintained under the rules. Related criminal provisions on cruelty by husband and in-laws (old IPC 498A) are now **BNS Section 85**, and dowry death (old IPC 304B) is now **BNS Section 80**. 10 sections. In force since 1 July 1961.

**SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989.** Protects Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes from caste-based humiliation and violence. It lists specific atrocities — forcing someone to eat inhuman substances, parading, denying access to a water source or public place, sexual exploitation, false criminal cases, social or economic boycott, and using caste names as abuse in public view. Cases go to Special Courts; anticipatory bail is generally barred; and victims are entitled to **compensation and relief** under the rules. The 2018 amendment reversed a Supreme Court ruling to restore the bar on anticipatory bail and remove the need for prior approval to arrest. About 23 sections. In force since 30 January 1990.

**Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015.** Deals with two groups: **children in conflict with law** (under-18s who commit offences) and **children in need of care and protection** (abandoned, trafficked, working, orphaned children). Its core idea is reform, not punishment. Children go before a **Juvenile Justice Board**, not a regular court, and cannot be sentenced to death or life imprisonment. The controversial change after the 2012 Delhi case: a **16 to 18 year old accused of a heinous offence** (7 years or more) can, after a preliminary assessment of maturity, be tried as an adult. This Act also lays down India's legal adoption process through CARA — any adoption outside this process is not valid. 112 sections in 10 chapters. In force since 15 January 2016; amended in 2021 to give District Magistrates the power to issue adoption orders.

**UAPA, 1967 — Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967.** India's main anti-terror law. It lets the government ban organisations as "unlawful" or "terrorist", and, since the **2019 amendment, designate an individual person as a terrorist**. Its practical bite is procedural: police get **180 days** (instead of the usual 90) to file a chargesheet, and Section 43D(5) makes bail extremely difficult — a court must deny bail if the accusation appears prima facie true. This is why UAPA cases often mean years in jail before trial. About 53 sections. In force since 30 December 1967; amended in 2004, 2008, 2012 and 2019.

**PMLA, 2002 — Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002.** Targets "black money" — the act of hiding the origin of money earned from crime. The Enforcement Directorate (ED) can **attach** (freeze) property it believes is proceeds of crime, even before conviction. It also puts obligations on ordinary institutions: banks, insurers, mutual funds and even some jewellers must verify your identity (**KYC**) and report suspicious and large cash transactions. Bail conditions are strict. Note: PMLA is not a standalone offence — it needs a "predicate offence" from a listed schedule (corruption, drugs, cheating and so on). About 75 sections in 10 chapters. In force since 1 July 2005; amended several times including 2019.

# PART 2 — CIVIL AND EVERYDAY LAW

Civil law is about disputes between people or companies — money, property, contracts, services. Nobody goes to jail. The court orders someone to pay, to hand over property, or to do (or stop doing) something. **You** file the case yourself; the government does not do it for you.

---

## 4. Indian Contract Act, 1872

**(a)** The Indian Contract Act, 1872. Act No. 9 of 1872.

**(b)** It replaced scattered English common-law rules applied by courts in British India. It has itself been trimmed twice: its sale-of-goods portion was taken out in 1930 and its partnership portion in 1932, each becoming a separate Act.

**(c) What it covers.** This is the law of **promises**. It says when a promise becomes a legally binding agreement that a court will enforce, and what happens when someone breaks it. A contract needs: an offer, acceptance of that offer, "consideration" (each side gives something — money, goods, work), free consent, lawful purpose, and parties competent to contract. It also covers agency (acting on someone's behalf), guarantee and surety (standing security for someone's loan), bailment (handing over goods for safekeeping), and pledge.

**(d) Four things a common person should know.**

1. **A contract does not have to be on stamp paper, and often does not have to be written at all.** An oral agreement is legally valid for most everyday deals. Buying vegetables is a contract. The problem with oral contracts is not validity — it is **proof**. Get it in writing anyway.
2. **A minor's contract is void.** A person under 18 cannot make a binding contract. If you lend money to a 17-year-old, you cannot enforce repayment.
3. **Consent obtained by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation or mistake is not free consent.** A contract signed under threat, or after being lied to about a material fact, is voidable — the wronged party can cancel it.
4. **An agreement in restraint of trade or marriage is void.** A clause saying "you can never work in this line of business again" is generally unenforceable in India (Section 27), unlike in some other countries. So is an agreement not to marry at all. Also void: **wagering agreements** (bets), which is why gambling debts are not recoverable in court.
5. **You can claim compensation, not punishment.** For breach of contract, you get damages for actual loss reasonably foreseeable — not a windfall.

**(e) Size.** 266 sections as originally enacted; about 238 remain in force after the 1930 and 1932 removals. 11 chapters.

**(f) Status.** In force since **1 September 1872**. Still fully operational, unamended in substance for most of its life. One of the oldest laws still running your daily life.

## 5. Property Trio: Transfer of Property Act 1882 + Registration Act 1908 + Indian Stamp Act 1899

These three always work together. If you buy a flat or land, all three apply to the same transaction.

### Transfer of Property Act, 1882

**(a)** The Transfer of Property Act, 1882. **(b)** Replaced uncertain English-law rules applied case by case in India.

**(c) What it covers.** It says **how property moves from one living person to another** — by sale, mortgage, lease, exchange, gift, or "actionable claim". (Property moving after death, by will or inheritance, is a different law — the Succession Acts.) It applies mainly to immovable property: land, houses, flats.

**(d) Key things to know.**
1. **A sale of immovable property worth Rs 100 or more must be by a registered document.** No exceptions. A sale agreement, a General Power of Attorney (GPA), or a possession letter is **not** a transfer of ownership. The Supreme Court has said so clearly. "GPA sale" property is not properly yours.
2. **There are six kinds of mortgage** (Section 58), including the common **simple mortgage** and the **mortgage by deposit of title deeds** — the one banks use for home loans, where you simply hand over your original property papers.
3. **A lease of immovable property for more than one year must be registered.** Most landlords write 11-month agreements precisely to avoid this.
4. **Lis pendens (Section 52).** If a property is already in a court case, anyone who buys it takes it subject to the court's final decision. Always check for pending litigation.
5. **A gift of immovable property needs a registered gift deed** signed by the donor and attested by two witnesses, and must be accepted while the donor is alive.

**(e)** 137 sections, 8 chapters. **(f)** In force since **1 July 1882**; amended notably in 1929 and 2002.

### Registration Act, 1908

**(c) What it covers.** It says **which documents must be registered** with the Sub-Registrar, and what happens if you do not register. Registration means the document is copied into a permanent public record, so anyone can later verify it.

**(d) Key things to know.**
1. **Compulsorily registrable (Section 17):** sale deeds, gift deeds of immovable property, leases over one year, and any non-testamentary document creating a right in immovable property worth Rs 100 or more.
2. **An unregistered document that should have been registered cannot be used as evidence of the transaction.** You lose your ability to prove ownership.
3. **You normally have 4 months from signing** to present a document for registration; a further 4 months is possible with a penalty.
4. **A will does NOT have to be registered** — but registering it makes it much harder to challenge later.
5. **Registration proves the transaction, not the seller's title.** The Sub-Registrar does not verify whether the seller actually owned the property. That is why you still need an independent title search and an encumbrance certificate.

**(e)** 91 sections, 15 parts. **(f)** In force since **1 January 1909**. States have their own amendments and now run online registration portals.

### Indian Stamp Act, 1899

**(c) What it covers.** **Stamp duty** — a tax you pay to the state government on documents. It is not a fee for a service; it is a tax, and it is often the biggest single cost when you buy property.

**(d) Key things to know.**
1. **Stamp duty rates are set by each state**, not the Centre, and vary widely (roughly 3% to 8% of the property value). Many states give a **1% to 2% discount if the buyer is a woman**.
2. **Duty is calculated on the higher of the actual price or the government "circle rate" / ready reckoner value.** Under-declaring the price to save duty is a false declaration and can be penalised.
3. **An under-stamped document is not admissible as evidence** until the deficit duty plus penalty (which can be up to 10 times the deficit) is paid.
4. It is not only for property: stamp duty applies to affidavits, power of attorney, share transfers, bonds, insurance policies and lease deeds.

**(e)** 78 sections plus Schedule I listing instruments and rates. **(f)** In force since **1 July 1899**; heavily amended by each state, and amended centrally in 2019 to centralise duty on securities transactions.

## 6. Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (CPC)

**(a)** The Code of Civil Procedure, 1908. Act No. 5 of 1908. **(b)** Replaced the Civil Procedure Codes of 1859, 1877 and 1882.

**(c) What it covers.** The **step-by-step rulebook for a civil court case**. Which court can hear your case (jurisdiction), how to write and file a plaint (the document that starts a suit), how the other side files a written statement, how documents and witnesses come in, how a judgment is written, how you appeal, and — the part people forget — how you actually **collect** what the court awarded you (execution).

**(d) Five things a common person should know.**

1. **The CPC has two halves.** The numbered **Sections** (1 to 158) are the skeleton and can only be changed by Parliament. The **First Schedule** contains 51 **Orders** with **Rules** — the day-to-day details — and High Courts can amend those. That is why lawyers say "Order 7 Rule 11" or "Order 39".
2. **Order 39 — injunctions.** This is how you get a court to say "stop" immediately: stop the demolition, stop the sale, stop the construction, until the case is decided. This is the most practically useful thing in the CPC for an ordinary person.
3. **Section 89 — settle out of court.** The court can and should refer your case to mediation, conciliation, arbitration or Lok Adalat. A Lok Adalat settlement is final, binding, has no appeal, and **your court fee is refunded**. For small money disputes this is usually far better than a full trial.
4. **Order 33 — suing as an indigent person.** If you genuinely cannot afford court fees, you can apply to sue without paying them. Poverty is not supposed to bar you from court.
5. **Winning is not getting.** A decree in your favour means nothing until you file **execution proceedings** (Order 21) to attach and sell the loser's property or attach his salary. Many people win and never collect.
6. **Commercial disputes have a faster track.** The Commercial Courts Act, 2015 amended the CPC for commercial disputes above a threshold, adding compulsory pre-litigation mediation and strict timelines.

**(e)** 158 sections plus the First Schedule with 51 Orders. **(f)** In force since **1 January 1909**; major amendments in 1976, 1999 and 2002 aimed at speeding up trials, and 2015 for commercial courts.

## 7. Specific Relief Act, 1963 and Limitation Act, 1963

### Specific Relief Act, 1963

**(a)** The Specific Relief Act, 1963. **(b)** Replaced the Specific Relief Act, 1877.

**(c) What it covers.** Sometimes money is not enough. If a seller refuses to hand over the flat you paid for, you do not want your advance back — you want **the flat**. This Act lets a court order a person to actually **perform** the promise ("specific performance"), or to hand back property, or to stop doing something (injunction), or to cancel or rectify a document.

**(d) Key things to know.**
1. **The 2018 amendment changed the game.** Earlier, specific performance was a discretionary favour a court might grant. Since 2018, **specific performance is the rule**, not the exception. If you have a valid agreement to sell and you were always ready and willing to pay, the court should order the sale to be completed.
2. **Section 6 — recovery of possession within 6 months.** If someone throws you out of immovable property without your consent and without due process of law, you can file a simple suit within **6 months** and get possession back **without having to prove you own it**. This is a powerful, fast remedy against forcible eviction, and most people have never heard of it.
3. **You cannot get specific performance of a personal-service contract.** A court will not force a singer to sing or an employee to work. It will award damages instead.
4. **Substituted performance (Section 20, added 2018).** If the other side breaks the contract, you can get the work done by a third party and recover the cost from the defaulter.

**(e)** 44 sections, 3 parts. **(f)** In force since **1 March 1964**; substantially amended by the Specific Relief (Amendment) Act, 2018, in force from 1 October 2018.

### Limitation Act, 1963

**(c) What it covers.** **Deadlines.** Every kind of civil case has a time limit. Miss it and the court will refuse to hear you even if you are completely right. The idea: evidence goes stale and people should not live forever under threat of an old claim.

**(d) Key things to know — memorise these numbers.**
1. **3 years** — to recover money lent, to recover the price of goods sold, for most contract breaches, and for compensation for most wrongs.
2. **12 years** — to recover possession of immovable property; **30 years** if the property is government-owned.
3. **1 year** — for a suit for wrongful dismissal from service, and for many defamation claims (1 year).
4. **The clock can restart.** A written and signed acknowledgement of the debt, or a part payment, before the period expires, restarts the 3 years (Sections 18 and 19). This is why lenders ask for a signed confirmation letter.
5. **Section 5 — condonation of delay.** For appeals and applications (not for fresh suits), a court can excuse a delay if you show "sufficient cause". Do not rely on it.
6. **Adverse possession.** If someone openly occupies your land as their own for 12 years and you do nothing, you can lose the right to evict them. Check on your empty land.

**(e)** 32 sections plus a Schedule with 137 Articles setting the time limits. **(f)** In force since **1 January 1964**.

## 8. Consumer Protection Act, 2019

**(a)** The Consumer Protection Act, 2019. Act No. 35 of 2019. **(b)** Replaced the Consumer Protection Act, 1986.

**(c) What it covers.** Your rights when you **buy a product or pay for a service** and get cheated — defective goods, bad service, overcharging, misleading advertisements, unfair contract terms. It gives you special, cheap, fast consumer courts (formally "Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions") instead of ordinary civil courts. The 2019 version added online shopping, product liability, and a regulator with teeth.

**(d) Five things a common person should know.**

1. **You can file where you live.** Under the old law you had to go to the seller's city. Now you can file in the commission where **you** reside or work. You can file **online** at edaakhil.nic.in, and for small claims you can argue it yourself — no lawyer needed.
2. **Three levels, by value of the claim.** **District Commission** up to Rs 50 lakh, **State Commission** above Rs 50 lakh up to Rs 2 crore, **National Commission (NCDRC)** above Rs 2 crore. (These limits were revised in 2021 — check the current figures before filing.) The deadline to file is **2 years** from when the problem arose.
3. **The CCPA has real power.** The Central Consumer Protection Authority can order a **product recall**, order refunds, stop misleading advertisements, and fine the manufacturer, the advertiser **and the celebrity endorser** — up to Rs 10 lakh for a first offence and Rs 50 lakh for repeat offences, plus a ban on endorsing for up to 1 to 3 years.
4. **Product liability (a new chapter).** If a defective product injures you, you can sue the manufacturer, the seller **and** the service provider — and against a manufacturer you do not have to prove negligence.
5. **Online shopping is covered.** E-commerce platforms must display the seller's details, country of origin, return and refund policy, and grievance officer contact. "Dark patterns" — misleading design tricks that push you into buying or subscribing — have been separately targeted by CCPA guidelines. **Unfair contract terms** (like a one-sided builder agreement) can now be struck down.
6. **Mediation is built in.** A commission can refer your case to mediation, which often ends it in weeks rather than years.

**(e)** 107 sections in 8 chapters. **(f)** Came into force in stages from **20 July 2020** (most provisions) and 24 July 2020 (product liability and e-commerce related). Supporting rules include the Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020. Fully operational as of 2026.

## 9. Sale of Goods Act, 1930 and Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881

### Sale of Goods Act, 1930

**(a)** The Sale of Goods Act, 1930. **(b)** Carved out of Chapter VII of the Indian Contract Act, 1872.

**(c) What it covers.** Buying and selling **movable goods** — a car, a phone, machinery, grain. It answers: when exactly does ownership pass from seller to buyer? Who bears the loss if the goods are destroyed in transit? What quality can the buyer demand? What can an unpaid seller do?

**(d) Key things to know.**
1. **Risk follows ownership, not possession.** If ownership has passed to you, the loss is yours even if the goods are still in the seller's godown. Check the terms of your purchase order.
2. **Conditions and warranties.** A **condition** is essential — break it and the buyer can reject the goods and cancel. A **warranty** is minor — the buyer can only claim damages. Read which is which in your contract.
3. **Implied conditions protect you automatically.** Goods must match their description, be of merchantable quality, and — if you told the seller your purpose and relied on his skill — be fit for that purpose. These apply even if the contract is silent.
4. **"Caveat emptor" (buyer beware) is the old baseline**, but the implied conditions above and the Consumer Protection Act have cut it down a lot for ordinary consumers.
5. **An unpaid seller has a lien** on the goods and a right to stop them in transit if the buyer becomes insolvent.

**(e)** 66 sections, 7 chapters. **(f)** In force since **1 July 1930**.

### Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881

**(a)** The Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881. **(b)** Codified English mercantile law on paper instruments for India.

**(c) What it covers.** Three instruments: the **promissory note** (an IOU), the **bill of exchange**, and the **cheque**. It says how they are made, transferred by endorsement, presented for payment, and what happens when they are dishonoured. For ordinary Indians, the whole Act boils down to one section.

**(d) Section 138 — cheque bounce. What you must know.**

1. **A bounced cheque is a criminal offence.** If a cheque is returned unpaid for insufficient funds or because the amount exceeds the arrangement with the bank, the drawer (the person who wrote it) can face imprisonment up to **2 years**, or a fine up to **twice the cheque amount**, or both.
2. **There is a strict three-step clock, and missing any step kills the case.** (i) You must present the cheque to the bank within **3 months** of its date. (ii) Within **30 days** of receiving the bank's dishonour memo, you must send a written **demand notice** to the drawer. (iii) He gets **15 days** to pay. If he does not, you must file the complaint in court within the next **30 days**.
3. **Interim compensation.** Under **Section 143A** (added 2018), the court can order the drawer to pay you up to **20% of the cheque amount** as interim compensation even while the trial is going on. Under Section 148, on appeal against conviction, the appellate court can order deposit of at least 20% of the fine or compensation.
4. **Cases can be settled.** Section 138 cases are compoundable — if the drawer pays, the case ends. Most are settled in Lok Adalats.
5. **Practical safety tips.** Never sign a blank cheque. Cross your cheques ("A/c Payee only"). Keep the dishonour memo — it is the starting gun for the whole timeline. Post-dated cheques given as loan security do fall under Section 138.

**(e)** Originally 141 sections; now 148 sections in 17 chapters, after the addition of the cheque-bounce chapter (1988) and later amendments. **(f)** In force since **1 March 1882**. Section 138 was added with effect from **1 April 1989**; important amendments in 2002, 2015 (fixing which court has jurisdiction — the payee's bank branch location) and 2018 (interim compensation).

## 10. Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016 (RERA)

**(a)** The Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016. Act No. 16 of 2016. **(b)** Nothing — before RERA there was no dedicated law for homebuyers. Buyers had to use the Consumer Protection Act or ordinary civil suits.

**(c) What it covers.** It regulates **builders and real estate agents** and protects **homebuyers**. Every project above a threshold size must be registered with the state RERA authority before a single flat can be advertised or sold. The builder must publish the project details, approvals, layout, and completion date on a public website, and must stick to them. There is a fast dispute forum for buyers.

**(d) Five things a common person should know.**

1. **Never buy in an unregistered project.** Projects on land above 500 square metres or with more than 8 apartments must be registered. Check your state RERA website for the registration number before paying anything. Advertising an unregistered project is itself an offence.
2. **70% of your money is locked in an escrow account.** The builder must deposit 70% of the money collected from buyers in a separate bank account, usable only for that project's construction and land cost. This is meant to stop builders taking money from Project A to fund Project B — the practice that stranded lakhs of buyers.
3. **"Carpet area" is now the only legal measure.** Builders must quote and sell on **carpet area** — the actual usable floor area within your walls — not "super built-up area", which included lifts, lobbies and gardens. This alone changed the effective price per square foot people were paying.
4. **Delay means interest, and you can walk away.** If the builder misses the promised possession date, you can either withdraw and get a full refund with interest, or stay in the project and claim **monthly interest for every month of delay**. The rate of interest must be the **same** for the builder and the buyer — earlier contracts charged buyers 18% and paid buyers 2%.
5. **Five-year defect liability.** If a structural defect or a quality problem appears within **5 years** of possession, the builder must fix it free within 30 days. Also: a builder cannot take more than **10% of the cost as advance** before signing a registered agreement for sale.
6. **Where to complain.** File with your **State RERA Authority**; appeal to the **Real Estate Appellate Tribunal**. It is designed to be faster and cheaper than a civil court.

**(e)** 92 sections in 10 chapters. **(f)** Came into force in stages: 59 sections on **1 May 2016**, the remaining sections on **1 May 2017**. It is a central law but implemented through **state rules and state authorities** — so the details, and the strictness, vary a lot by state. As of 2026 all major states have functioning authorities, though ongoing projects that started before RERA remain a grey area in some states.

## 11. Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 (as amended in 2019)

**(a)** The Motor Vehicles Act, 1988. **(b)** Replaced the Motor Vehicles Act, 1939.

**(c) What it covers.** Everything about vehicles and roads: driving licences, vehicle registration, permits for commercial vehicles, insurance, traffic offences and penalties, and compensation for road accident victims. The **Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 2019** sharply raised penalties and added new ideas like the Good Samaritan protection and a compulsory motor accident fund.

**(d) Five things a common person should know.**

1. **Third-party insurance is compulsory, and driving without it is an offence.** "Third party" means the insurer pays the person you injure or whose property you damage. Driving without it carries up to 3 months jail or a Rs 2,000 fine (first offence) — and, far worse, you personally pay the accident compensation, which can run into tens of lakhs. Own-damage cover is optional; third-party is not.
2. **Penalties went up several times over in 2019.** Indicative central figures: driving without a licence around Rs 5,000; drunk driving Rs 10,000 and/or 6 months (Rs 15,000 and/or 2 years for a repeat); over-speeding Rs 1,000 to Rs 2,000; not wearing a helmet Rs 1,000 plus 3-month licence disqualification; not wearing a seat belt Rs 1,000; using a phone while driving Rs 5,000; dangerous driving up to Rs 5,000; racing Rs 5,000; **juvenile driving — the guardian or vehicle owner is held liable**, with up to 3 years jail and Rs 25,000 fine, and the vehicle's registration cancelled. **States may set different amounts**, and several have reduced them, so check your state's notification.
3. **The Good Samaritan is protected (Section 134A).** If you stop and take an injured person to hospital, you **cannot** be harassed — you are not liable civilly or criminally, you need not give your name, and the hospital must treat the victim immediately. Fear of police trouble kills people on Indian roads; this section exists to remove that fear.
4. **Hit-and-run and no-fault compensation.** The Act provides compensation from a government fund for hit-and-run victims — raised in 2019 to **Rs 2 lakh for death** and Rs 50,000 for grievous hurt. Separately, a claim can be made before the **Motor Accidents Claims Tribunal (MACT)**, and after the 2019 amendment there is no longer an outer time limit bar in the same rigid form — but file within 6 months to be safe.
5. **Licence and documents are now digital.** A licence or registration certificate shown on **DigiLocker or mParivahan** is legally valid — you do not have to carry the physical card. Learner's licence, driving test and many services are online through Parivahan Sewa.
6. **Vehicle recall and a Registered Vehicle Scrappage Policy.** The government can order a manufacturer to recall defective vehicles, and old vehicles must pass fitness tests or be scrapped, under rules made from 2021 onward.

**(e)** 217 sections in 14 chapters. **(f)** In force since **1 July 1989**. The 2019 amendment came into force in stages from **1 September 2019**. Enforcement of penalty amounts varies by state.

## 12. Information Technology Act, 2000 and Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023

### Information Technology Act, 2000

**(a)** The Information Technology Act, 2000. Act No. 21 of 2000. **(b)** Nothing — it was India's first law for the internet.

**(c) What it covers.** It gives legal recognition to **electronic records and digital signatures**, so an email or an e-contract counts in law. It creates **cybercrimes** and punishments. And it sets the rules for "intermediaries" — the platforms that carry other people's content, like Facebook, X, YouTube, WhatsApp and your internet provider.

**(d) Things a common person should know.**
1. **Common cyber offences.** Identity theft (Section 66C), cheating by personation using a computer — the classic OTP or fake-customer-care fraud (Section 66D), **violation of privacy** by capturing or publishing images of a private area without consent (Section 66E), and publishing obscene or sexually explicit material electronically (Sections 67, 67A, 67B — the last one covering child sexual abuse material, which carries very heavy punishment).
2. **Section 43A and 72A — your data.** A company that is careless with your sensitive personal data and causes you loss must compensate you. Disclosing your information in breach of a lawful contract is punishable.
3. **Section 66A is dead.** The section that let police arrest people for "offensive" online messages was struck down as unconstitutional by the Supreme Court in *Shreya Singhal* (2015). If any police officer cites Section 66A, it does not exist. Sadly, cases were still being registered under it years later.
4. **Section 79 and the IT Rules, 2021.** Platforms get "safe harbour" — they are not liable for what users post — **only if** they follow due diligence and take down unlawful content when properly notified. The Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021 require every significant platform to appoint an Indian Grievance Officer and respond to your complaint within fixed timelines. If a platform ignores your complaint about, say, an obscene image of you, you can escalate.
5. **Where to complain.** **cybercrime.gov.in** — the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal — and the helpline **1930** for financial fraud. Report a fraudulent transaction within the "golden hour" (ideally within a few hours) to have any real chance of freezing the money.

**(e)** 94 sections in 13 chapters (after the substantial 2008 amendment). **(f)** In force since **17 October 2000**; major amendment in force from **27 October 2009**. Still the main cyber law. Note: a proposal to replace it with a "Digital India Act" has been discussed for years but, as of September 2026, no such Act has been enacted.

### Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP)

**(a)** The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023. Act No. 22 of 2023, passed August 2023.

**(b)** It does not replace a whole Act, but it **amends** Section 43A of the IT Act and, importantly, **amends Section 8(1)(j) of the RTI Act, 2005** (see below).

**(c) What it covers.** Your **personal data in digital form**. It gives you rights over information about you that companies and government bodies collect, and puts duties on whoever collects it (called a "Data Fiduciary"). You are the "Data Principal". The core idea: your data can be processed only with your **free, specific, informed and unambiguous consent**, given for a stated purpose, after a clear notice — or for certain "legitimate uses" listed in the Act.

**(d) Five things a common person should know.**

1. **Your rights.** The right to **know** what data of yours is held and who it has been shared with; the right to **correct or update** it; the right to **erase** it when the purpose is over; the right to **withdraw consent** as easily as you gave it; the right to a **grievance redressal** process; and the right to nominate someone to exercise your rights if you die or become incapacitated.
2. **Notice must be in plain language and in your language.** Every consent notice must be available in English and in any of the **22 languages in the Eighth Schedule** of the Constitution.
3. **Children get special protection.** For anyone under 18, a Data Fiduciary must get **verifiable parental consent**, and must not do behavioural advertising targeted at children or tracking that harms them.
4. **Penalties are large.** Up to **Rs 250 crore** for failing to take reasonable security safeguards leading to a data breach, and up to Rs 200 crore for failing in duties towards children. Note there is **no compensation to you personally** under this Act — the money is a penalty paid to the government. You can also be fined up to Rs 10,000 for filing a frivolous complaint.
5. **The RTI trade-off is real and controversial.** Section 44(3) of the DPDP Act amended **Section 8(1)(j) of the RTI Act** to exempt **all** "personal information" from disclosure. Earlier, personal information could be disclosed if there was a larger **public interest**. That public-interest override has been removed from that clause. Transparency activists and many MPs say this weakens RTI badly; the government says Section 8(2) of the RTI Act still allows disclosure in the public interest. Challenges have been raised before the Supreme Court. **This is unsettled law — watch it.**

**(e)** 44 sections in 9 chapters, plus a Schedule of penalties.

**(f) Status as of September 2026 (verified).** The Act was passed in August 2023 but sat unused for over two years. Then:
- **13–14 November 2025:** the Ministry of Electronics and IT (MeitY) issued notifications bringing the Act into force **in stages**, notified the **Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025**, and established the **Data Protection Board of India** in the National Capital Region with four members.
- **14 November 2025:** first tranche in force — definitions and the setting up of the Board.
- **14 November 2026:** the **Consent Manager** framework kicks in (a Consent Manager is a registered platform through which you can give, manage and withdraw consent across many companies from one place). This is roughly two months away as of this writing.
- **14 May 2027:** all remaining substantive obligations — notice, consent, security safeguards, breach reporting, your rights, and penalties — become enforceable. This is the deadline companies are working towards.

So: **the law exists and is partly in force, but most of your enforceable rights switch on in May 2027.** Until then, complaints mostly rest on the IT Act and its rules.

## 13. Right to Information Act, 2005 (RTI)

**(a)** The Right to Information Act, 2005. Act No. 22 of 2005. **(b)** Replaced the Freedom of Information Act, 2002, which was passed but never brought into force.

**(c) What it covers.** It gives every citizen of India the right to **ask a government body for information and get an answer**. This flows from Article 19(1)(a) of the Constitution — free speech includes the right to know. It covers all central, state and local government bodies, and bodies substantially financed by government. It also requires departments to **proactively publish** a lot of information without being asked (Section 4).

**(d) Five things a common person should know.**

1. **It costs Rs 10 and one page.** A central RTI application costs Rs 10 (free if you are Below Poverty Line — attach the BPL card copy). You write to the **Public Information Officer (PIO)** of the department. No format is required. You do **not** have to give any reason for wanting the information. You do not have to be affected by the matter.
2. **The deadlines.** **30 days** for a normal reply. **48 hours** if the information concerns the **life or liberty** of a person. **35 days** if you route it through an Assistant PIO. If the PIO does not reply in time, it is a "deemed refusal" and you can appeal for free.
3. **Two appeals, then the Commission.** First Appeal to the departmental First Appellate Officer within 30 days. Second Appeal to the **Central or State Information Commission** within 90 days. The Commission can **fine the PIO Rs 250 per day, up to Rs 25,000**, for wrongly refusing or delaying — this penalty is what makes the law work.
4. **Real-life uses.** Why is my ration card pending? Where has the road-repair money for my ward gone? What are the marks and answer sheets in a public exam (you can get your own evaluated answer sheet)? What is the status of my pension file? Copies of muster rolls under MGNREGA. Inspection of works and records.
5. **What is exempt (Section 8).** National security, foreign relations, trade secrets, information held in a fiduciary relationship, Cabinet papers before a decision, and — now widely — **personal information**. Note the changed position: since **Section 44(3) of the DPDP Act, 2023** amended **Section 8(1)(j)**, personal information is exempt without the earlier public-interest override in that clause. As of 2026 this is being contested and is the single biggest live question about the future of RTI in India. Section 8(2) — allowing disclosure where the public interest outweighs the harm — remains on the books.
6. **File it online.** For central bodies: **rtionline.gov.in**. Many states have their own portals.

**(e)** 31 sections in 6 chapters, plus 2 Schedules. **(f)** Some provisions in force from **15 June 2005**; the operative right to seek information from **12 October 2005**. Amended in 2019 (giving the Centre power to fix the tenure and salary of Information Commissioners — criticised as weakening their independence) and in 2023 through the DPDP Act as described above. As of September 2026 the Act is in force, but vacancies in Information Commissions and the Section 8(1)(j) change are the two big practical problems.

# PART 3 — FAMILY AND PERSONAL LAW

**One thing to understand first.** India does not have one family law. Marriage, divorce, inheritance, adoption and maintenance depend on **which religion you belong to** — unless you deliberately choose a religion-neutral law like the Special Marriage Act. This is called "personal law". A Hindu, a Muslim, a Christian and a Parsi are each governed by different rules for the same life event.

The word "Hindu" in these Acts is defined widely: it includes **Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs**, and anyone who is not a Muslim, Christian, Parsi or Jew by religion.

---

## 14. The Four Hindu Acts of 1955–56

In the 1950s, Parliament codified Hindu personal law into four Acts, often called the "Hindu Code Bills". They apply to Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs.

### Hindu Marriage Act, 1955

**(b) What it replaced.** Uncodified Hindu customary and scriptural law on marriage, plus earlier partial laws.

**(c) What it covers.** Who may marry whom, what makes a Hindu marriage valid, registration, restitution of conjugal rights, judicial separation, and divorce.

**(d) Key things to know.**
1. **Conditions for a valid marriage (Section 5).** Neither party has a living spouse (**monogamy is compulsory** — a second marriage is void and is also an offence, BNS Section 82); the bride is at least **18** and the groom at least **21**; neither is incapable of consent due to unsoundness of mind; and they are not within "prohibited degrees" of relationship or "sapindas" of each other, unless a custom allows it.
2. **Grounds for divorce (Section 13).** Adultery, cruelty, desertion for 2 years or more, conversion to another religion, unsoundness of mind, virulent leprosy (removed by a 2019 amendment), venereal disease, renunciation of the world, and not being heard of for 7 years. A wife has **extra grounds** (Section 13(2)), including that the husband has been guilty of rape, sodomy or bestiality, and the option of repudiating a marriage that took place before she turned 15.
3. **Mutual consent divorce (Section 13B).** Both spouses can jointly petition after living apart for **one year**. There is then a **6-month cooling-off period** before the second motion. The Supreme Court has held this 6 months can be **waived** where the marriage has clearly broken down, and in 2023 held it can even dissolve such a marriage directly under Article 142.
4. **Section 9 — restitution of conjugal rights.** A spouse who has left without reason can be ordered by the court to return. This section is controversial and its constitutionality has been questioned.
5. **Maintenance and custody.** Section 24 gives **maintenance during the case** (interim), and Section 25 gives **permanent alimony**, to either husband or wife. Section 26 covers custody of children. Separately, maintenance can be claimed under **BNSS Section 144** (the old CrPC 125), which applies to everyone regardless of religion and is usually faster.
6. **Registration.** Section 8 allows state governments to make registration compulsory. Many states have. **Register your marriage** — it is your proof for passports, visas, insurance, pensions and inheritance.

**(e)** 29 sections in 6 chapters. **(f)** In force since **18 May 1955**. Amended in 1976 (adding mutual consent divorce) and 2019 (removing leprosy as a ground).

### Hindu Succession Act, 1956

**(c) What it covers.** Who inherits a Hindu person's property when they die **without a will** (called dying "intestate"). It replaced two different traditional schools — Mitakshara and Dayabhaga — with one statutory scheme.

**(d) Key things to know.**
1. **The 2005 amendment is the most important change in Indian inheritance law.** Before 2005, only sons were **coparceners** — members by birth of the joint family property (a coparcener has a birthright to ancestral property and can demand partition). The Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005 made a **daughter a coparcener by birth, with exactly the same rights and liabilities as a son**. She has this right whether or not she is married.
2. **The Supreme Court settled the confusion in 2020.** In *Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma* (11 August 2020), the Court held that the daughter's right is by **birth**, so it does **not** matter whether her father was alive on 9 September 2005 (the date the amendment took effect). Earlier conflicting judgments were overruled. Practical effect: a daughter can claim her share in ancestral property even if her father died before 2005 — subject to partitions already legally completed before 20 December 2004.
3. **Class I heirs.** When a Hindu man dies intestate, his property goes first, in equal shares, to his **Class I heirs**: widow, mother, sons and daughters (and children of predeceased children). A father is only a Class II heir. If there is no Class I heir, it goes to Class II, then agnates, then cognates.
4. **A woman's own property (Section 14).** Any property a Hindu woman possesses is her **absolute property**, which she can sell or will away as she likes. This abolished the old "limited estate" that a widow held only for her lifetime.
5. **A will overrides all of this.** These rules apply only when there is no valid will. Making a will is the single most effective way to prevent a family property dispute.

**(e)** 30 sections in 3 chapters plus a Schedule listing heirs. **(f)** In force since **17 June 1956**; the daughters' amendment in force from **9 September 2005**.

### Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956

**(c) What it covers.** Who is the legal guardian of a Hindu child (under 18) and of the child's property.

**(d) Key things to know.**
1. **The father is named as the natural guardian first, then the mother.** But the Supreme Court in *Githa Hariharan v. RBI* (1999) read the word "after" to mean "in the absence of", so the **mother can act as natural guardian** when the father is absent or indifferent. For a child below **5 years**, custody ordinarily goes to the mother.
2. **An illegitimate child's natural guardian is the mother**, then the father.
3. **A guardian cannot sell or mortgage the minor's immovable property without prior permission of the court** (Section 8). A sale without permission is voidable by the child. If you are buying property where a minor has a share, insist on seeing the court order.
4. **Guardianship of a married minor girl** vests in her husband under this Act — a provision widely criticised and in tension with child marriage law.
5. **In every dispute, the "welfare of the minor" is the paramount consideration** — courts say this overrides the statutory order of guardians.

**(e)** 13 sections. **(f)** In force since **25 August 1956**. It works alongside the older Guardians and Wards Act, 1890, which applies to all communities.

### Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956

**(c) What it covers.** Two separate topics: valid adoption by Hindus, and the duty to maintain dependants.

**(d) Key things to know.**
1. **An adopted child is treated as a natural-born child** from the date of adoption, with full inheritance rights in the adoptive family, and loses ties to the birth family (except that certain relationships for marriage purposes remain barred).
2. **A married person needs the spouse's consent to adopt** (unless the spouse has renounced the world, converted, or been declared of unsound mind). Since a 2010 amendment, a **married woman can also adopt** in her own right in the circumstances allowed.
3. **Age gap rule.** If a man adopts a girl, or a woman adopts a boy, there must be at least a **21-year age difference**. You cannot adopt a second child of the same gender if you already have a living child of that gender (son or son's son, or daughter, as the case may be).
4. **Two routes exist.** This Act is one route (for Hindus adopting, usually within the family). The **Juvenile Justice Act, 2015** through **CARA** (cara.wcd.gov.in) is the other, and is open to everyone regardless of religion, and is the only legal route for adopting an orphaned, abandoned or surrendered child. Anything outside these two routes — "taking in" a child informally — gives the child no legal rights.
5. **Maintenance.** A Hindu is bound to maintain a wife, minor children, unmarried daughter and **aged or infirm parents** — including a mother-in-law who is a widow with no other means. A wife may live separately and still claim maintenance if the husband is cruel, keeps a concubine, is guilty of desertion, or has converted.

**(e)** 30 sections in 4 chapters. **(f)** In force since **21 December 1956**; amended 2010.

## 15. Muslim Personal Law

### Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937

**(a)** The Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937. **(b)** It displaced custom and usage that had crept in and were being applied to Muslims by British Indian courts instead of Islamic law.

**(c) What it covers.** It is a very short Act. It does **not** write down Muslim law. It simply says that in a list of matters — **intestate succession, women's special property, marriage, dissolution of marriage (including talaq, ila, zihar, lian, khula and mubaraat), maintenance, dower (mahr), guardianship, gifts, trusts and waqf** — where the parties are Muslims, the rule to apply is **Muslim Personal Law (Shariat)**, not custom. So the actual rules live in Islamic jurisprudence as interpreted by Indian courts, not in a statute.

**(d) Things a common person should know.**
1. **Mahr (dower) is a debt.** It is a sum the husband is obliged to pay the wife. It is not a gift and not optional. An unpaid mahr is recoverable like any other debt from his estate.
2. **A Muslim marriage (nikah) is a civil contract**, not a sacrament, and it requires offer, acceptance and witnesses. The nikahnama can carry conditions, including delegating the right of divorce to the wife (talaq-e-tafweez).
3. **Agricultural land is excluded** from the Act's coverage in the original text, leaving that to state laws.
4. **Adoption is not recognised** in classical Muslim law in the sense of creating an inheriting child; guardianship (kafala) is. But the Supreme Court in *Shabnam Hashmi* (2014) held that any person, regardless of religion, may adopt under the Juvenile Justice Act.
5. **Two other statutes complete the picture:** the **Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939**, which lists the grounds on which a Muslim **woman** can obtain a court decree of divorce (husband missing 4 years, failure to maintain for 2 years, imprisonment for 7 years, cruelty, impotence, and others); and the **Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986**, passed after the *Shah Bano* case, on maintenance after divorce — which the Supreme Court has interpreted generously, and in **2024 (Mohd Abdul Samad)** confirmed that a divorced Muslim woman can **also** claim maintenance under the general criminal-law provision (now BNSS Section 144).

**(e)** 6 sections. **(f)** In force since **7 October 1937**.

### Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019 — the "triple talaq" law

**(a)** The Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019. Act No. 20 of 2019.

**(b)** It followed the Supreme Court's judgment in *Shayara Bano v. Union of India* (22 August 2017), which declared instant triple talaq unconstitutional.

**(c) What it covers.** It deals only with **talaq-e-biddat** — "instant triple talaq", where a husband pronounces talaq three times in one sitting, by any means including in writing, by phone, by SMS or on WhatsApp, and claims the marriage is instantly and irrevocably over. The Act declares such a pronouncement **void and illegal** and makes it a **criminal offence**.

**(d) Things a common person should know.**
1. **Punishment: up to 3 years imprisonment and a fine.** The offence is cognizable (police can arrest without a warrant), but **only if** the FIR is filed by the wife or a person related to her by blood or marriage — not by a stranger or a neighbour.
2. **The marriage does not end.** Because the talaq is void, the woman remains legally married. She is entitled to **subsistence allowance** for herself and her dependent children, and to **custody of her minor children**.
3. **It is compoundable.** The Magistrate may allow the parties to settle at the wife's instance. Bail may be granted by the Magistrate after hearing the wife.
4. **Other forms of talaq are untouched.** Talaq-e-ahsan and talaq-e-hasan — the revocable forms spread over three months — remain lawful. Khula (divorce at the wife's instance) and mubaraat (mutual) also remain.
5. **The criticism.** Many ask how jailing the husband helps a woman who now has no income. That debate continues.

**(e)** 8 sections in 4 chapters. **(f)** In force **retrospectively from 19 September 2018** (the date of the ordinance it replaced); the Act received assent on 31 July 2019.

### Waqf Act, 1995, as amended by the Waqf (Amendment) Act, 2025 ("UMEED Act")

**(a)** The Waqf Act, 1995, as amended by the Waqf (Amendment) Act, 2025. The amending Act renamed the parent Act the **Unified Waqf Management, Empowerment, Efficiency and Development Act, 1995** — hence "UMEED", which also means "hope".

**(b)** The 1995 Act replaced the Waqf Act, 1954.

**(c) What it covers.** A **waqf** is a permanent dedication of property by a Muslim for a religious, pious or charitable purpose — a mosque, a graveyard, a school, a hospital, land whose rent funds charity. Once dedicated, it cannot be sold or inherited. This Act governs how waqfs are surveyed, registered and managed: it creates **State Waqf Boards**, the **Central Waqf Council**, a **Waqf Tribunal** for disputes, and the office of the **mutawalli** (manager). Waqf boards collectively hold one of the largest property portfolios in India, and mismanagement and encroachment have been long-standing complaints — which is what the 2025 amendment claims to address.

**(d) What the 2025 amendment changed.**
1. **"Waqf by user" was removed.** Previously, a property used as a waqf for a long time (say a graveyard used for generations) could be treated as waqf even without a deed. The amendment requires a formal declaration, protecting existing registered waqf-by-user properties but barring new ones — and this was the most contested change.
2. **Only a practising Muslim of at least five years' standing, who owns the property, may create a waqf.** The five-year requirement was new.
3. **Non-Muslim members** may now be included in the Central Waqf Council and State Waqf Boards.
4. **Government property is not waqf.** Where a dispute arises over whether a property is government land, a senior government officer (originally the District Collector) decides, rather than the Waqf Tribunal.
5. **The Limitation Act, 1963 now applies** to waqf property claims — meaning the earlier protection against time-barred claims on encroached waqf land is gone.
6. **Compulsory registration on the UMEED portal** — a central online database — within six months, with mandatory maintenance of records. Waqf boards must also ensure inheritance rights of women are not defeated by a waqf.

**(e)** Around 113 sections in the parent Act as it stood, restructured by the 2025 amendment.

**(f) Status as of September 2026 — this one is genuinely unsettled, so read carefully.**
- The Waqf (Amendment) Act, 2025 received assent in April 2025 and was **notified with effect from 8 April 2025**.
- Around **65 petitions** challenged it in the Supreme Court, arguing violation of Articles 14, 15, 25, 26, 29, 30 and 300A.
- On **15 September 2025**, a bench headed by **CJI B. R. Gavai** delivered an **interim order**. It refused to stay the whole Act (a law passed by Parliament carries a presumption of constitutionality), but it **stayed or read down several provisions**, including: the requirement that a person show **five years of practising Islam** (stayed until the state frames rules and a mechanism); the power of a designated officer to change revenue records while an inquiry is pending (a waqf property is not to lose its character until a Tribunal decides); and it **capped the number of non-Muslim members** on the Central Waqf Council and State Waqf Boards (broadly, not more than 4 of 22 and 3 of 11 respectively), while requiring the CEO to preferably be a Muslim.
- The Court **upheld** compulsory registration through the **UMEED portal**. It later (around December 2025) **refused a blanket extension** of the six-month registration deadline, saying individual waqfs seeking more time must go to the **Waqf Tribunal** under Section 3B.
- **The final decision on constitutional validity was still pending as of September 2026.** Do not treat the interim order as the last word. If you are a mutawalli, trustee or beneficiary of a waqf, take current legal advice — this is a moving target.

## 16. Christian, Parsi and Religion-Neutral Laws

### Indian Christian Marriage Act, 1872

**(c) What it covers.** How a marriage where at least one party is a Christian is solemnised in India: by a licensed minister of religion, a clergyman of the Church of England/Scotland/Rome, or a **Marriage Registrar**. It sets out notice requirements, the hours and place of the ceremony (traditionally between 6 a.m. and 7 p.m. in a church, with exceptions), the need for two credible witnesses, and registration.

**(d) Note.** Minimum age is 21 for the man and 18 for the woman, with parental consent needed below 21. Marriage without proper notice or by an unauthorised person can be void. The Act does not apply in some states, notably the erstwhile Travancore-Cochin and Manipur areas, where separate laws apply.

**(e)** 88 sections in 8 parts. **(f)** In force since **18 July 1872**. Law Commission reports have recommended replacing it; no replacement has been enacted.

### Indian Divorce Act, 1869 (Divorce Act, 1869)

**(c) What it covers.** Divorce, nullity and judicial separation where at least one party is a Christian.

**(d) Key things to know.** The original Act was blatantly unequal — a husband could divorce for adultery alone, while a wife had to prove adultery **plus** cruelty or desertion. The **2001 amendment** fixed this: the grounds are now the **same for both spouses** (adultery, conversion, unsoundness of mind, leprosy, venereal disease, not heard of for 7 years, desertion for 2 years, cruelty, and for a wife also rape/sodomy/bestiality). The 2001 amendment also introduced **divorce by mutual consent (Section 10A)** after living apart for 2 years, and removed the requirement that every High Court confirm a district court's divorce decree. Note: the separation period for mutual consent under this Act is **2 years**, longer than the 1 year under the Hindu Marriage Act and Special Marriage Act.

**(e)** 62 sections in 14 parts. **(f)** In force since **1 April 1869**; substantially amended in **2001**.

### Parsi Marriage and Divorce Act, 1936

**(c) What it covers.** Marriage and divorce among Parsi Zoroastrians. Marriage must be solemnised by the **Ashirvad** ceremony performed by a priest in the presence of two Parsi witnesses.

**(d) Key things to know.** This Act is unique in India for using a **jury system**: matrimonial cases go to special **Parsi Matrimonial Courts** with a panel of **delegates** (lay jurors) who decide the facts. The 1988 amendment made grounds for divorce equal for husband and wife and added mutual consent divorce after living apart for one year. A long-criticised feature: a **Parsi woman who marries a non-Parsi** has historically faced loss of community rights, though the Supreme Court in *Goolrokh Gupta* (2018) protected her right to attend her parents' last rites.

**(e)** 54 sections in 7 chapters. **(f)** In force since **22 April 1936**; amended in 1988 and 1991.

### Special Marriage Act, 1954 — the religion-neutral marriage law

**(a)** The Special Marriage Act, 1954. **(b)** Replaced the Special Marriage Act, 1872.

**(c) What it covers.** **Anyone in India can marry anyone else under this Act, regardless of religion, without either person converting.** It is the law for inter-faith and inter-caste marriages, for people who want a purely civil marriage, and for Indians marrying abroad through Indian consulates. It also provides for **registering** a marriage already solemnised under a religious ceremony.

**(d) Five things a common person should know.**
1. **The process.** Give written **notice** to the Marriage Officer of a district where either party has lived for at least 30 days. The notice is **published for 30 days** for objections. If nobody objects (or objections fail), the marriage is solemnised before the Officer and **three witnesses**, and a certificate is issued.
2. **The 30-day public notice is the problem.** It has been widely misused to harass inter-faith couples — notices are pinned on public boards and photographed. The **Allahabad High Court in Safiya Sultana (2021)** held that publication of the notice is **optional**, not compulsory: the couple can ask the Officer not to publish it, and the Officer must not insist. If you face a demand to publish, cite this judgment. A challenge to the 30-day provision is pending before the Supreme Court.
3. **Conditions.** Groom 21, bride 18; neither has a living spouse; both capable of consent; not within prohibited degrees of relationship (unless custom allows).
4. **Succession changes for some.** If two Hindus (or Buddhists, Jains, Sikhs) marry under this Act, their succession is governed by the Hindu Succession Act; but in other cases the **Indian Succession Act, 1925** applies instead of religious personal law. This matters for inheritance planning.
5. **Divorce is under this same Act**, with grounds broadly similar to the Hindu Marriage Act, including mutual consent after one year's separation.

**(e)** 51 sections in 8 chapters. **(f)** In force since **9 October 1954**.

### Indian Succession Act, 1925

**(c) What it covers.** Two things. First, **wills** — how to make one, how to prove it, executors, probate, letters of administration. The rules on wills in this Act apply to **almost everyone in India except Muslims** (for whom Islamic rules on bequests apply, generally limiting a bequest to one-third of the estate without heirs' consent). Second, **intestate succession** (dying without a will) for Christians, Parsis, Jews, and those married under the Special Marriage Act.

**(d) Five things a common person should know.**
1. **How to make a valid will.** It must be in writing, signed by the testator, and **attested by at least two witnesses** who saw the testator sign. That is all. It does **not** need stamp paper, and does **not** need registration — though registration makes it far harder to challenge. **A witness should not be a beneficiary** under the will.
2. **You can change your will any time.** The latest valid will overrides earlier ones. Write the date clearly.
3. **Probate.** In the presidency towns of Kolkata, Chennai and Mumbai (and for immovable property there), probate of a will is compulsory for Hindus, Buddhists, Jains, Sikhs and others; elsewhere it is generally optional but often demanded by banks and registrars.
4. **Intestate succession for Christians (Sections 31 to 49).** The widow takes one-third and the children two-thirds; if there are no children, the widow takes half and the father or other kindred the rest. Note that the shares differ substantially from Hindu law — many Christian families are unaware of this.
5. **Succession certificate (Part X).** For **movable** assets — bank deposits, shares, debts owed to the deceased — heirs usually need a succession certificate from a civil court. This is the document banks ask for when there is no nomination and no will.

**(e)** 391 sections in 11 parts. **(f)** In force since **30 September 1925**.

## 17. Uniform Civil Code (UCC) — a live, changing area

**What it means.** Article 44 of the Constitution (a Directive Principle, not enforceable in court) says the State "shall endeavour to secure for the citizens a uniform civil code throughout the territory of India". A UCC would replace the separate religion-based personal laws described above with **one set of rules on marriage, divorce, maintenance, inheritance and adoption for everyone**. There is no national UCC. But personal law is in the **Concurrent List**, so **states can legislate** — and since 2024 three have moved.

**Status as of September 2026 (verified):**

**Uttarakhand — first, and in force.** The **Uniform Civil Code of Uttarakhand Act, 2024** was passed in February 2024 and **came into force on 27 January 2025**, making Uttarakhand the first state in independent India with a UCC. **Scheduled Tribes are exempt.** Key features:
- One set of rules on marriage, divorce, succession and inheritance for all religions in the state.
- **Compulsory registration of marriages**, and — the most discussed provision — **compulsory registration of live-in relationships**, with penalties for not registering. A child born of a registered live-in relationship is legitimate, and the woman can claim maintenance if deserted.
- Polygamy, halala, iddat and triple talaq are prohibited; equal inheritance rights for sons and daughters; uniform grounds for divorce.
- After a year of operation, the state issued the **Uttarakhand Uniform Civil Code (Amendment) Ordinance, 2026**, which came into force on **27 January 2026** with roughly 15 to 18 changes: registration of marriages and live-in relationships was **eased and made more voluntary in several respects**, the requirement of Aadhaar and community certificates for registration was removed, live-in couples were **freed from police investigation or surveillance** (complaints now need a valid legal reason), and penal provisions against coercion and fraud in marriage and live-in relationships were strengthened.

**Gujarat — second, passed but check assent.** The **Gujarat Uniform Civil Code Bill, 2026** was passed by the Gujarat Legislative Assembly on **24–25 March 2026** after a long debate, making Gujarat the second state to adopt a UCC. It broadly follows the Uttarakhand model. **As of the sources available, the Bill had been passed by the Assembly but final assent and the commencement date were not clearly confirmed.** Verify the current position on the Gujarat government gazette before relying on it.

**Assam — third, passed by the Assembly.** The **Uniform Civil Code, Assam, 2026 Bill** was passed by the Assam Legislative Assembly on **27 May 2026**, again following the Uttarakhand model and again **exempting Scheduled Tribes**. The Chief Minister stated the Bill would go to the Governor and then to the **President** for assent. **As of September 2026, confirm whether Presidential assent has been received and whether it has been brought into force.**

**Everywhere else.** No UCC. Your religion's personal law continues to apply. Goa is a special case: it has had a **common civil code inherited from Portuguese law (the Portuguese Civil Code of 1867)** since long before independence, which applies to all Goans — often cited as India's only pre-existing "uniform" civil code.

## 18. Three Laws Protecting the Vulnerable

### Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007

**(a)** The Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007. **(b)** Nothing directly — it supplements the general maintenance provision (now BNSS Section 144) with a far faster route.

**(c) What it covers.** It gives parents and senior citizens a **cheap, fast legal right to be maintained by their children or relatives**, without going to a regular court.

**(d) Five things every family should know.**
1. **Apply to a Maintenance Tribunal, not a court.** Every subdivision has one, presided over by a revenue officer. **A lawyer is not allowed to appear** — that is deliberate, to keep it simple and cheap. The Tribunal is meant to decide within **90 days**.
2. **Who can claim.** A **parent** of any age (including a mother-in-law or father-in-law, and adoptive parents) can claim from children or grandchildren. A **senior citizen (60+) without children** can claim from a relative who is in possession of, or would inherit, his property.
3. **Maintenance amount.** Capped at **Rs 10,000 per month** under the central Act (states may vary; an amendment bill proposing removal of the cap has been pending for years).
4. **The strongest provision — Section 23: you can take your property back.** If a senior citizen transferred property to a child on the condition that the child would look after him, and the child fails, the **transfer can be declared void** by the Tribunal and the property returned. Many High Courts and the Supreme Court have used this to get elderly parents' houses back and to evict abusive children. This is the provision most people do not know exists.
5. **Abandoning a senior citizen is a crime** — up to 3 months imprisonment, or a fine, or both. The Act also obliges states to set up old age homes and provide medical facilities.

**(e)** 32 sections in 7 chapters. **(f)** In force from **December 2007** onwards as notified by each state (Himachal Pradesh, for example, from 2008); in force across India today.

### Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 (POCSO)

Covered in Part 1 above. The three points to repeat here for families: **anyone under 18 is a child**, regardless of consent; **failure to report** known child sexual abuse is itself an offence; and the child's identity must never be published. Every school must have a policy, and the **CHILDLINE helpline is 1098**.

### Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006

**(a)** The Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006. **(b)** Replaced the Child Marriage Restraint Act, 1929 (the "Sarda Act"), which only "restrained" child marriage rather than allowing it to be undone.

**(c) What it covers.** It sets the legal marriage age at **18 for women and 21 for men**, punishes those who perform, conduct, direct or abet a child marriage, and lets a child married below those ages get out of the marriage.

**(d) Five things a common person should know.**
1. **A child marriage is "voidable", not automatically void.** The person who was a child at the time can apply to a District Court to **annul** it — up to **2 years after attaining majority**, i.e. until age 20 for a girl and 23 for a boy. It becomes **void** outright in cases of kidnapping, trafficking, or where the child was sold or forced.
2. **Punishment.** A **male adult above 18** who marries a child: up to 2 years rigorous imprisonment and/or a fine up to Rs 1 lakh. The same for anyone who **performs, conducts, directs or abets** the marriage — which includes the priest, the band, the caterer and the parents. Parents or guardians who fail to prevent it are punishable, though a woman cannot be sentenced to imprisonment under that section.
3. **Child Marriage Prohibition Officers.** Every state must appoint them. They can seek an injunction from a court to **stop a marriage before it happens**. A court can issue such an injunction even on information from a citizen or an NGO — and a marriage performed in breach of an injunction is **void**.
4. **Maintenance and residence.** A girl whose child marriage is annulled is entitled to maintenance until she remarries, and to residence. Children born of the marriage are **legitimate**, and courts decide their custody on welfare grounds.
5. **POCSO overrides.** Sexual intercourse with a wife under 18 is an offence — the Supreme Court in *Independent Thought v. Union of India* (2017) read down the marital-rape exception so that sex with a wife below 18 is rape. A proposal to raise women's marriage age to 21 (the Prohibition of Child Marriage (Amendment) Bill, 2021) was referred to a Parliamentary Standing Committee and, as of September 2026, **has not been enacted**.

**(e)** 21 sections. **(f)** In force since **1 November 2007**.

## 19. The Four Labour Codes — India's biggest workplace change in 70 years

**(a) Full names and years.**
1. **The Code on Wages, 2019**
2. **The Industrial Relations Code, 2020**
3. **The Code on Social Security, 2020**
4. **The Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020**

**(b) What they replaced.** Together they consolidate and replace **29 central labour laws**, split as follows:

- **Code on Wages, 2019** replaces **4** laws: Payment of Wages Act 1936, Minimum Wages Act 1948, Payment of Bonus Act 1965, Equal Remuneration Act 1976.
- **Industrial Relations Code, 2020** replaces **3** laws: Trade Unions Act 1926, Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act 1946, Industrial Disputes Act 1947.
- **Code on Social Security, 2020** replaces **9** laws: Employees' Compensation Act 1923, Employees' State Insurance Act 1948, Employees' Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act 1952, Employment Exchanges (Compulsory Notification of Vacancies) Act 1959, Maternity Benefit Act 1961, Payment of Gratuity Act 1972, Cine Workers Welfare Fund Act 1981, Building and Other Construction Workers Welfare Cess Act 1996, Unorganised Workers' Social Security Act 2008.
- **Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020** replaces **13** laws: Factories Act 1948, Mines Act 1952, Dock Workers Act 1986, Building and Other Construction Workers Act 1996, Plantations Labour Act 1951, Contract Labour (Regulation and Abolition) Act 1970, Inter-State Migrant Workmen Act 1979, Working Journalists and other Newspaper Employees Act 1955, Working Journalists (Fixation of Rates of Wages) Act 1958, Motor Transport Workers Act 1961, Sales Promotion Employees Act 1976, Beedi and Cigar Workers Act 1966, Cine Workers and Cinema Theatre Workers Act 1981.

**(c) What they cover, in plain words.** **Wages** — how your salary is defined, when it must be paid, minimum wages, bonus, equal pay. **Industrial Relations** — trade unions, standing orders, layoffs, retrenchment, closure, strikes and dispute resolution. **Social Security** — provident fund, pension, ESI health cover, gratuity, maternity benefit, and, for the first time, **gig and platform workers**. **Occupational Safety** — safety at the workplace, working hours, leave, welfare facilities, contract labour, migrant workers, and licensing of factories.

**(d) Six things a working person should know.**

1. **You must be given a written appointment letter.** This is now mandatory for **every** worker. For crores of Indians who work with no document at all, this is the single most important change — no letter means no proof of employment, no PF, no way to fight unfair dismissal.
2. **Your take-home pay may change, and your PF and gratuity should go up.** The Codes define **"wages"** uniformly, and require that **allowances excluded from "wages" cannot exceed 50%** of total remuneration. Employers who kept basic pay low and allowances high (to reduce PF and gratuity liability) must now restructure. Effect: higher PF contribution and higher gratuity, but possibly lower monthly cash in hand.
3. **Gig and platform workers get social security for the first time.** Delivery riders, cab drivers and similar workers are defined in law, and **aggregators must contribute 1% to 2% of turnover (capped at 5% of the amount paid to such workers)** to a social security fund. Registration is on the **e-Shram** portal.
4. **Minimum wage now covers everyone, plus a national floor wage.** Earlier, minimum wages applied only to "scheduled employments". Now a statutory minimum wage applies to **all** employees in both organised and unorganised sectors, and the Centre can fix a **national floor wage** below which no state may go.
5. **Gratuity for fixed-term employees after one year.** Previously you needed **5 years** of continuous service to get gratuity. A **fixed-term employee** now earns pro-rata gratuity after **one year**. Fixed-term employees also get the same wages, hours and benefits as permanent employees doing the same work.
6. **Some rules got easier for employers, which is the contested part.** The threshold for needing **government permission before layoff, retrenchment or closure** rose from 100 workers to **300 workers**. A **60-day notice is now compulsory before any strike** in all industrial establishments (earlier only in public utilities). Trade unions have opposed both. Also new: **women may work at night and in all types of work**, with consent and safety measures.
7. **Other practical points.** Working hours remain 8 per day / 48 per week, but daily hours can go up to 12 with a corresponding shorter week (the "4-day week" possibility), with overtime at **twice** the wage. Annual leave eligibility eased from 240 to **180 days** worked. **Annual free health check-ups** for workers above a notified age. Wages must be paid within fixed timelines depending on the wage period, and **digitally** in many cases.

**(e) Size.** Code on Wages: 69 sections, 9 chapters. Industrial Relations Code: 104 sections, 14 chapters. Code on Social Security: 164 sections, 14 chapters. OSH Code: 143 sections, 14 chapters.

**(f) Status as of September 2026 — verified, and this is recent news.**
- The four Codes were passed in **2019 and 2020** but sat unimplemented for five years, because labour is a Concurrent subject and both the Centre and every state had to frame rules.
- **21 November 2025:** the Government of India **brought all four Labour Codes into force across the country**. This is the date to remember.
- **30 December 2025:** draft Central Rules were published for public consultation (30 days for the IR Code, 45 days for the other three).
- **8 May 2026:** the **final Central Rules were notified** — the Code on Wages (Central) Rules 2026, Social Security (Central) Rules 2026, OSH (Central) Rules 2026 and Industrial Relations (Central) Rules 2026.
- **Central Rules apply where the Central Government is the "appropriate government"** — banking, insurance, telecom, mines, oilfields, major ports, air transport, railways and central public sector undertakings. **For most private employers, your own state's rules apply**, and states are at different stages of notifying theirs. So the practical answer to "which rules apply to me?" is: **check your state**.
- The Ministry of Labour has been publishing **FAQs** (a set dated 16 March 2026 is public) as employers work through the transition.

### The older laws being replaced — still worth knowing, because they governed your past service

**EPF & MP Act, 1952 (Employees' Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act).** Created the **EPFO**. Applies to establishments with 20 or more employees. Employee contributes **12% of basic + DA**; the employer matches it, but the employer's share splits — 8.33% to the **Employees' Pension Scheme (EPS)** and 3.67% to PF. Your **UAN** (Universal Account Number) stays with you for life across jobs. You can withdraw partially for a house, marriage, education or illness, and fully after 2 months of unemployment. 23 sections. In force since 1952; now subsumed into the Code on Social Security.

**ESI Act, 1948 (Employees' State Insurance Act).** Medical care and cash benefits for workers earning up to **Rs 21,000 a month** (Rs 25,000 for persons with disability). Employee pays **0.75%** of wages, employer **3.25%**. Benefits: full medical care for you and your family at ESI hospitals and dispensaries, sickness benefit (70% of wages up to 91 days a year), maternity benefit, disablement benefit, dependants' benefit on death, and funeral expenses. About 100 sections. In force since 1948.

**Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972.** A lump sum thank-you payment for long service. Payable after **5 years of continuous service** (not required if service ends due to death or disablement) at the rate of **15 days' wages for each completed year**. Ceiling raised to **Rs 20 lakh** (tax-free up to that limit for non-government employees). 15 sections. In force since 16 September 1972.

**Minimum Wages Act, 1948.** Central and state governments fix minimum wages for scheduled employments, revised usually every 5 years with a dearness allowance component revised more often. Paying below the minimum wage is an offence, and an agreement by the worker to accept less is void. 31 sections.

**Maternity Benefit Act, 1961.** As amended in **2017**: **26 weeks** of paid maternity leave for the first two children (12 weeks for the third and beyond), 12 weeks for a **commissioning mother** or a mother adopting a child under 3 months, work-from-home where the nature of work permits, and a **crèche facility** compulsory in establishments with **50 or more employees**, with four visits a day allowed. Dismissal during maternity leave is illegal. About 30 sections.

**POSH Act, 2013 — Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013.** Passed after the *Vishaka* guidelines. Every workplace with **10 or more employees** must set up an **Internal Committee (IC)**, chaired by a senior woman, with at least half the members women and one external member from an NGO or legal background. Districts must have a **Local Committee** for smaller workplaces and the unorganised sector, including domestic workers. A complaint must be filed within **3 months** (extendable by 3 more), the inquiry completed within **90 days**, and action taken within 60 days of the report. Conciliation is possible but **no monetary settlement** can be the basis of conciliation. It covers clients, customers, interns and visitors on the premises, and includes an implied promise of preferential treatment or threat about employment status as harassment. Not constituting an IC attracts a fine up to Rs 50,000, and repeat failure can mean cancellation of licence. **Note: the POSH Act is NOT subsumed into the labour codes — it stands alone and continues.** 30 sections in 8 chapters. In force since **9 December 2013**. The Supreme Court has repeatedly criticised poor implementation and, in 2023 and again since, directed states to constitute committees properly and set up the **SHe-Box** portal.

## 20. Money and Tax Laws

### Income-tax Act, 2025

**(a)** The Income-tax Act, 2025. **(b)** Replaces the **Income-tax Act, 1961**.

**(c) What it covers.** How much tax you pay on what you earn — salary, house property, business or profession, capital gains, and other sources. It covers deductions, exemptions, TDS and TCS (tax deducted or collected at source), filing returns, assessment by the department, appeals, and penalties. The 2025 Act is a **rewrite for simplicity, not a change in tax policy** — rates and the basic structure carry over. Its declared aim was to cut the volume of text roughly in half, replace long provisos and explanations with tables and formulas, and use plain language.

**(d) Five things a taxpayer should know.**
1. **"Previous year" and "assessment year" are gone.** The new Act uses a single concept: **"tax year"**. This alone removes the most common source of confusion for ordinary filers.
2. **The Act is shorter and organised into tables.** 536 sections against the 1961 Act's 800-plus (as amended over 60 years), in **23 chapters** with **16 schedules**. Section numbers are completely renumbered — the famous "Section 80C" and "Section 139" of the old Act now sit at different numbers, so old advice citing section numbers needs mapping.
3. **Tax policy did not change with the new Act.** Slabs, the new-regime-versus-old-regime choice, capital gains rules and TDS rates continue as they were, subject to whatever the annual Finance Act does.
4. **The old Act still governs old years.** Income for years up to FY 2025-26 is assessed under the **1961 Act**. The 2025 Act governs income from **FY 2026-27** onwards. Both will run side by side in appeals and assessments for years.
5. **New Rules too.** The **Income-tax Rules, 2026** replaced the Income-tax Rules, 1962 with effect from **1 April 2026**.

**(e)** 536 sections, 23 chapters, 16 schedules.

**(f) Status as of September 2026 — verified.** Passed by Parliament on **12 August 2025**, received Presidential assent on **21 August 2025**, and **came into force on 1 April 2026**. It is the law in force today for the current tax year.

### GST — CGST, SGST, IGST Acts, 2017, and "GST 2.0"

**(a)** The **Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017**; the **Integrated Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017**; the **Union Territory GST Act, 2017**; the **GST (Compensation to States) Act, 2017**; and a **State GST Act** passed by each state.

**(b) What they replaced.** Around 17 central and state indirect taxes and 13 cesses — central excise duty, service tax, VAT, central sales tax, entry tax, octroi, luxury tax, entertainment tax and others. Enabled by the **101st Constitutional Amendment**.

**(c) What it covers.** One tax on the **supply of goods and services** across India. It is a **destination-based** tax (the state where you consume pays) collected in stages, with **input tax credit** so businesses pay tax only on the value they add. Within a state you pay **CGST + SGST**; across states you pay **IGST**. Decisions are made by the **GST Council**, a body of the Union Finance Minister and all state finance ministers.

**(d) Five things to know.**
1. **You pay GST on almost everything you buy**, and it is printed on your bill. If a shop charges GST but is not registered, that is a fraud — you can check any **GSTIN** on the GST portal.
2. **Registration threshold.** Broadly Rs 40 lakh annual turnover for goods and Rs 20 lakh for services (Rs 20 lakh / Rs 10 lakh in special category states). A **Composition Scheme** lets small businesses up to Rs 1.5 crore pay a flat low rate with minimal paperwork but no input tax credit.
3. **GST 2.0 — the rate overhaul of September 2025.** The GST Council decided on **3 September 2025**, effective **22 September 2025**, to collapse the four-slab structure. The **12% and 28% slabs were abolished**. The main rates are now **5% and 18%**, with **0%** for essentials and a new **40%** slab for luxury and sin goods (aerated drinks, luxury cars, motorcycles above 350cc, tobacco products, casinos and betting). About **99% of items** in the old 12% slab moved down to 5%, and about **90%** of the old 28% slab moved down to 18%. Compensation cess was scrapped on most items except certain tobacco products.
4. **What got cheaper for households.** Everyday items, many medicines and medical devices, and a broad range of goods that were at 12% or 28% saw rate cuts from 22 September 2025.
5. **E-invoicing and returns.** Businesses above notified turnover thresholds must generate e-invoices; regular filers file GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B monthly or quarterly. Late filing attracts interest and late fees.

**(e)** CGST Act: 174 sections in 21 chapters, plus 3 schedules. IGST Act: 25 sections. **(f)** GST came into force on **1 July 2017**. The GST 2.0 rate structure has applied since **22 September 2025**.

### Companies Act, 2013

**(a)** The Companies Act, 2013. **(b)** Replaced the Companies Act, 1956.

**(c) What it covers.** How a company is born, run, and wound up. Incorporation, share capital, directors and their duties, board and shareholder meetings, accounts and audit, related party transactions, mergers, investor protection, and penalties. It created the **NCLT** (National Company Law Tribunal), the **SFIO** (Serious Fraud Investigation Office) and India's **CSR** regime.

**(d) Five things to know.**
1. **A company is a separate legal person.** It can own property and be sued in its own name. Its shareholders' liability is **limited** to what they agreed to pay for their shares — this is the whole point of a limited company.
2. **One Person Company (OPC).** For the first time, a single individual can form a company with limited liability, instead of being a sole proprietor with unlimited personal liability. Very useful for small entrepreneurs.
3. **CSR is mandatory (Section 135).** Companies crossing thresholds — net worth Rs 500 crore, or turnover Rs 1,000 crore, or net profit Rs 5 crore — must spend at least **2% of average net profits of the last three years** on corporate social responsibility.
4. **Independent directors and women directors.** Listed and certain large companies must appoint independent directors, and **at least one woman director**. Directors have codified duties, and a **DIN** (Director Identification Number) is required.
5. **Directors can be personally liable for fraud.** The corporate veil is lifted for fraud, and "fraud" under Section 447 carries 6 months to 10 years imprisonment. Also relevant to everyday people: a company that accepts deposits from the public in breach of the rules is committing an offence — this is what many chit-fund and Ponzi collapses were prosecuted under.

**(e)** 470 sections in 29 chapters with 7 schedules. **(f)** Came into force in stages from **12 September 2013** and **1 April 2014**; amended repeatedly (2015, 2017, 2019, 2020) largely to decriminalise minor procedural defaults and ease compliance for small companies.

### Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC)

**(a)** The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016. **(b)** Replaced or overrode a scattered mess: the Sick Industrial Companies Act 1985 (SICA), the winding-up provisions of the Companies Act, the Presidency Towns Insolvency Act 1909 and the Provincial Insolvency Act 1920, and parts of the Recovery of Debts Act 1993.

**(c) What it covers.** What happens when a company or a person **cannot pay its debts**. The idea is a **time-bound** process: either the business is rescued through a resolution plan, or it is sold off (liquidated) and the money distributed in a fixed order. The key shift is from "debtor in possession" to **"creditor in control"** — once the process starts, an Insolvency Professional runs the company under a Committee of Creditors, not the old management.

**(d) Five things to know.**
1. **The timeline.** The Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) must finish in **180 days, extendable by 90**, with an outer limit of **330 days** including litigation. In practice it often overruns.
2. **Moratorium.** Once a case is admitted, all recovery suits and enforcement against the company **freeze**. This gives breathing space.
3. **The waterfall (Section 53).** When money is distributed, the order is: insolvency costs, then **workmen's dues for 24 months and secured creditors**, then employee dues for 12 months, then unsecured financial creditors, then government dues and remaining secured creditors, then other creditors, then preference shareholders, then equity shareholders last.
4. **Homebuyers are financial creditors.** A 2018 amendment made **homebuyers financial creditors**, so they sit on the Committee of Creditors when a builder goes insolvent. This mattered enormously in the Amrapali and Jaypee cases.
5. **Section 29A bars defaulting promoters.** The person who ran the company into the ground cannot buy it back cheap at the resolution stage.

**(e)** 255 sections in 7 parts, plus schedules. **(f)** In force in stages from **28 May 2016**; corporate insolvency provisions from December 2016. **Recent change verified:** the **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Amendment) Act, 2026** was passed by the Lok Sabha on **30 March 2026** and the Rajya Sabha on **1 April 2026**, received assent on **6 April 2026** and was **brought into force on 6 April 2026**. Its headline change is the **Creditor-Initiated Insolvency Resolution Process (CIIRP)** — notified classes of financial creditors holding at least **51% of the debt by value** can start an insolvency process **out of court**, with the debtor's management staying in control initially, and the process convertible into a full CIRP by the Committee of Creditors. (Note: this began as the IBC (Amendment) Bill, 2025 and was enacted in 2026.)

### SARFAESI Act, 2002

**(a)** The Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002.

**(c) What it covers.** It lets **banks and financial institutions seize and sell mortgaged property without going to court** when a borrower defaults. Before this, a bank had to file a civil suit and wait a decade.

**(d) Five things a borrower should know — this one affects home-loan holders directly.**
1. **The trigger.** Your loan account must be classified a **Non-Performing Asset (NPA)** — broadly, no payment for **90 days**.
2. **Section 13(2) notice — 60 days.** The bank must serve you a written demand notice giving you **60 days** to pay. This is your window to negotiate, restructure or repay.
3. **Section 13(4) — possession.** If you do not pay, the bank can take possession of the secured asset, take over management, or appoint a manager — and then sell it, usually by auction, after a further **30 days' sale notice**.
4. **You can object, and you can appeal.** Under **Section 13(3A)** you may make a written representation to the bank within the 60 days, and the bank must reply with reasons within 15 days. If possession is taken, you can appeal to the **Debts Recovery Tribunal (DRT)** under **Section 17 within 45 days** — and you do **not** have to deposit money to file this appeal (a deposit of 50% is only required at the second appeal stage before DRAT, reducible to 25%).
5. **Limits that protect you.** SARFAESI does **not** apply to **agricultural land**. It does not apply to unsecured loans (credit cards, personal loans) — for those the bank must sue. And bank recovery agents cannot use force, threats or public humiliation; RBI's Fair Practices Code and Supreme Court rulings prohibit it. Complain to the bank, then the **RBI Ombudsman**.

**(e)** 42 sections in 6 chapters. **(f)** In force since **21 June 2002**; amended in 2004, 2013 and 2016 (the 2016 amendment shortened timelines and brought in a central registry, CERSAI).

### RBI Act, 1934 and Banking Regulation Act, 1949

**(c) What they cover.** The **Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934** creates the RBI, gives it the **sole right to issue currency notes**, makes it banker to the government, and gives it charge of monetary policy — now through a **Monetary Policy Committee** with an inflation target (4% with a band of plus or minus 2%), added by the 2016 amendment. It also regulates NBFCs (non-banking financial companies) under Chapter IIIB. The **Banking Regulation Act, 1949** governs banks themselves: licensing, what business a bank may do, capital requirements, appointment of directors and CEOs, inspection, and the RBI's power to give directions, impose moratorium, or amalgamate a failing bank.

**(d) Things an ordinary depositor should know.**
1. **Your bank deposits are insured up to Rs 5 lakh per depositor per bank** by the DICGC (a subsidiary of RBI) — raised from Rs 1 lakh in 2020. This covers savings, current, fixed and recurring deposits added together, per bank.
2. **Co-operative banks came under RBI's full supervision in 2020**, after the PMC Bank crisis, by amending the Banking Regulation Act.
3. **The RBI Ombudsman scheme is free.** For a complaint about a bank, NBFC or payment system — wrong charges, failed transaction, mis-selling, harassment by recovery agents — first complain to the bank, and if unresolved in 30 days, go to **cms.rbi.org.in**.
4. **Note the rupee promise.** The RBI Governor's signature on a note carries the promise "I promise to pay the bearer the sum of..." — a legacy of the RBI Act's currency provisions.
5. **Nomination matters.** Add a nominee to every bank account, locker and deposit. Without it, your family needs a succession certificate.

**(e)** RBI Act: 61 sections. Banking Regulation Act: about 56 sections in 5 parts. **(f)** RBI Act in force since **1 April 1935** (when the RBI began operations); Banking Regulation Act since **16 March 1949**. Both heavily amended, most recently in 2020 (co-operative banks) and by the Finance Acts.

### SEBI Act, 1992

**(a)** The Securities and Exchange Board of India Act, 1992.

**(c) What it covers.** It makes **SEBI** a statutory regulator with three jobs: **protect investors**, develop the securities market, and regulate it. SEBI has powers to inspect, investigate, summon, search and seize, impose penalties, and pass orders barring people from the market. It regulates stock exchanges, brokers, mutual funds, merchant bankers, rating agencies and investment advisers, and polices insider trading and fraudulent and unfair trade practices.

**(d) Five things a small investor should know.**
1. **Check registration before you invest.** Every broker, mutual fund distributor and Registered Investment Adviser has a SEBI registration number. Unregistered "advisers" promising assured returns on Telegram or WhatsApp are almost always illegal. SEBI has been acting against "finfluencers".
2. **SCORES is your complaint platform.** File grievances against listed companies and market intermediaries free at **scores.sebi.gov.in**; there is also the **SMART ODR** portal for online dispute resolution.
3. **Insider trading is a serious offence.** Trading on unpublished price-sensitive information can bring a penalty of Rs 10 lakh or **three times the profit made, whichever is higher**, up to Rs 25 crore, plus imprisonment up to 10 years under Section 24.
4. **No scheme may promise guaranteed returns in the market.** Collective Investment Schemes and deposit schemes must be registered; unregistered ones are how most chit-fund frauds operate.
5. **Nomination and KYC.** SEBI requires nomination or an explicit opt-out for demat and mutual fund accounts, and periodic KYC updation. Keep them current so your family can claim.

**(e)** 35 sections in 7 chapters. **(f)** SEBI was set up in 1988 and given statutory powers by this Act, in force **retrospectively from 30 January 1992**. Amended in 1995, 1999, 2002 and 2014 (the last significantly widening SEBI's search, seizure and recovery powers).

# WHICH BARE ACTS SHOULD A HOUSEHOLD ACTUALLY OWN?

You do not need a library. Most families will only ever touch a handful of laws. Here is a realistic list, in order of how likely you are to need it.

**The essential five — buy these, they cost a few hundred rupees together:**

1. **The Constitution of India.** Not an Act, but the source of everything. Read at least Part III (Fundamental Rights, Articles 12 to 35) and Part IVA (Fundamental Duties). Everything else in this guide must obey it.
2. **Consumer Protection Act, 2019.** You will use this more than any other law in your life — for a bad phone, a delayed flight, an insurance claim wrongly rejected, a builder who did not deliver.
3. **Right to Information Act, 2005.** Ten rupees and a page of paper is the most power an ordinary Indian has over the government.
4. **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 + Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023.** Not to memorise offences, but so you know your rights at a police station: the right to a Zero FIR, a free copy of the FIR, to be told the grounds of arrest, to inform a relative, to a lawyer, and to be produced before a magistrate within 24 hours.
5. **The personal law that applies to you** — Hindu Marriage Act and Hindu Succession Act, or the Shariat Act and Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, or the Christian/Parsi Acts, or the Special Marriage Act if you married under it. Plus the **Indian Succession Act, 1925** if you plan to write a will.

**Add these depending on your life situation:**

- **Buying a home:** RERA 2016, Transfer of Property Act 1882, Registration Act 1908, your state's Stamp Act, and SARFAESI 2002 if you have a home loan.
- **Salaried or working:** the four Labour Codes (or, for older service periods, the EPF Act, ESI Act and Gratuity Act), the POSH Act 2013, and the Maternity Benefit Act 1961.
- **Running a small business:** Indian Contract Act 1872, Sale of Goods Act 1930, Negotiable Instruments Act 1881 (for cheque bounce), CGST Act 2017, and the Companies Act 2013 or the LLP Act if incorporated.
- **Driving:** Motor Vehicles Act 1988 — at least the penalty schedule and Section 134A on Good Samaritans.
- **Elderly parents in the family:** Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act 2007. Read Section 23.
- **Children in the family:** POCSO Act 2012, Juvenile Justice Act 2015, Prohibition of Child Marriage Act 2006.
- **Facing violence at home:** Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act 2005 and Dowry Prohibition Act 1961.
- **Online life:** IT Act 2000 and the DPDP Act 2023.

**A tip on reading a bare act.** Do not start at Section 1. Start at the **Preamble** (it tells you the purpose), then read the **definitions section** (usually Section 2 — half of all confusion comes from not knowing the defined meaning of a word), then jump to the chapter that concerns you. Ignore the rest.

**Useful numbers to save.** Police 112. Women's helpline 181. Child helpline 1098. Senior citizens 14567. Cyber fraud 1930. National Legal Services Authority (free legal aid) 15100.

# WHERE TO READ THESE LAWS FOR FREE

All of these are official and free. Beware of unofficial websites that host outdated versions — always check the amendment date.

**Central laws (the main source):**
- **India Code — indiacode.nic.in.** The official repository of all central Acts, maintained by the Legislative Department. It gives you the bare Act, the amendments incorporated, and the rules made under it. This is the single best free source. It also has a "Repealed Acts" section.
- **Legislative Department — legislative.gov.in.** The parent department. Hosts the Constitution, lists of Acts by year, and the official English and Hindi texts.
- **e-Gazette — egazette.gov.in.** The official Gazette of India. This is where a law legally becomes real — every Act, notification, rule and commencement order is published here first. If you need to prove a law came into force on a date, this is where the proof is.
- **PRS Legislative Research — prsindia.org.** Not official, but free, neutral and outstanding: plain-English summaries of every Bill, comparisons of old and new law, and legislative history. The best starting point if the bare Act is hard going.
- **Supreme Court of India — sci.gov.in** and the **eSCR** free judgment database at **digiscr.sci.gov.in**. Judgments in full, free.
- **eCourts — ecourts.gov.in** and the district court services portal, to check case status by CNR number.

**Reading laws in Indian languages:**
- **India Code** carries Hindi versions of many central Acts.
- **Bhashini** and the government's translation initiatives have been extending regional-language access; the Supreme Court publishes translated judgments in several languages on the eSCR portal.
- **Nyaya Setu / Nyaya Shruti** and the National Judicial Data Grid are part of the ongoing digitisation of justice services.

**State laws:**
- Each state has its own law department portal, e.g. **India Code's state section (indiacode.nic.in/state-wise-acts)** which aggregates state Acts.
- For property and registration, use your state's registration department portal (e.g. IGRS in several states) for circle rates, encumbrance certificates and online registration.
- **State RERA websites** for real estate projects — search the project's registration number before paying.

**Subject-specific official portals:**
- **Income tax:** incometax.gov.in (the Act, rules, forms, and e-filing).
- **GST:** gst.gov.in and cbic-gst.gov.in.
- **Labour:** labour.gov.in (the Codes, rules and FAQs) and eshram.gov.in for unorganised and gig workers.
- **Consumer:** consumerhelpline.gov.in and edaakhil.nic.in to file a case online; the **NCH helpline is 1915**.
- **RTI:** rtionline.gov.in.
- **Cybercrime:** cybercrime.gov.in, helpline **1930**.
- **Company law:** mca.gov.in.
- **Data protection:** meity.gov.in for the DPDP Act and Rules.
- **Free legal aid:** nalsa.gov.in — if your annual income is below the prescribed limit, or you are a woman, child, SC/ST, disabled person, industrial workman, or victim of trafficking or disaster, you are entitled to a **free lawyer**. Every district has a District Legal Services Authority. Most people who need this never claim it.

# A FINAL WORD

Three habits will protect you more than knowing any section number:

1. **Get it in writing, and register what the law says must be registered.** Marriage, sale deed, will, rent agreement, appointment letter.
2. **Watch the clock.** Almost every right in Indian law has a deadline — 3 years for a money suit, 2 years for a consumer complaint, 30 days for a cheque-bounce notice, 60 days for a SARFAESI notice, 3 months for a POSH complaint, 30 days for an RTI reply. A right you sleep on is a right you lose.
3. **Ask for the free help first.** Lok Adalat, mediation, District Legal Services Authority, the RBI or Insurance Ombudsman, the consumer helpline. They are faster, cheaper and often better than a court case.

*This guide describes the law as publicly reported up to 5 September 2026. Laws change. Before acting on anything here, check the current text on India Code and, for anything serious, consult a lawyer.*
