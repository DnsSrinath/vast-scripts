## TASK A — What does "EPCO" most plausibly mean?

The user's phrase was: *"Indian EPCO rules — how many total laws we have"*, in a conversation about *"law for the common man"*. "EPCO" is not a standard Indian legal acronym, so it is almost certainly a mistyping or mishearing of something else. Below are the candidates, each with a short factual note, followed by a recommendation.

### Candidate 1 — IPC (Indian Penal Code, 1860) — typo/transposition

- The **Indian Penal Code, 1860** was India's general criminal code for 164 years. It contained **511 sections**.
- Repealed and replaced by the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS)**, which came into force on **1 July 2024** and has **358 sections** — a consolidation from 511 to 358.
- BNS is one of three replacement statutes:
  - **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023** replaces the Indian Penal Code, 1860
  - **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS)** replaces the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973
  - **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (BSA)** replaces the Indian Evidence Act, 1872
- Drafting changes include merging offences against women and children into a single Chapter V, and replacing colonial-era terms ("minor" → "child", "insane" → "person of unsound mind").
- **Why this fits:** "EPCO" is one key-slip from "IPC" if typed on a phone; and a person asking "how many total laws we have" about criminal law would be asking about sections/offences. However, IPC is a *code*, not "rules", and the phrasing "EPCO **rules**" fits a regulator better.

### Candidate 2 — EPFO (Employees' Provident Fund Organisation) — phonetically closest

This is phonetically the nearest real Indian acronym ("E-P-F-O" vs "E-P-C-O") and is the one ordinary workers most often ask about by acronym. **Full treatment below in section A.2.**

### Candidate 3 — EPCO = Environmental Planning and Coordination Organisation (Madhya Pradesh)

- **EPCO** is a real, correctly-spelled Indian body: the **Environmental Planning & Co-ordination Organisation**, established **1981** under the Housing and Environment Department, **Government of Madhya Pradesh**.
- Located at **Paryavaran Parisar, E-5 Arera Colony, Bhopal 462016**; foundation stone laid by Smt. Indira Gandhi on a 50-acre campus.
- It is an **autonomous "think-tank"** organisation, not a regulator. It advises on state environmental policy, runs public awareness (including the MP Eco Clubs programme), and offers environmental consultancy.
- It is the **State Nodal Agency for Climate Change** in Madhya Pradesh and nodal agency for the state's Management Action Plan; it runs the MP Climate Change Knowledge Portal.
- **Crucially, EPCO makes no "rules" that bind citizens.** It has no statutory rule-making power, so "EPCO rules — how many total laws we have" does not sensibly attach to it.

### Candidate 4 — EPCA / EPA 1986 (environmental law)

Two distinct things are often confused here:

- **EPCA = Environment Pollution (Prevention and Control) Authority** — a Supreme Court-appointed body for the NCR, created in **1998** under s.3 of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986. It was **dissolved in 2020-21** and replaced by the **Commission for Air Quality Management in the National Capital Region and Adjoining Areas (CAQM)**, created first by ordinance (Oct 2020, lapsed 12 March 2021) and then by the **CAQM Act, 2021** (passed 12 August 2021). Government's stated reason: EPCA had no penal powers; CAQM does.
- **EPA = Environment (Protection) Act, 1986** — the umbrella statute under which most of India's environmental *rules* are made. **Main rules a citizen or small business actually meets:**
  - Environment (Protection) Rules, 1986
  - **EIA Notification, 2006** (prior environmental clearance for mining, thermal power, river valley, infrastructure, large construction)
  - **Solid Waste Management Rules, 2016**
  - **Plastic Waste Management Rules, 2016** (as amended; single-use plastic ban, EPR)
  - **Bio-Medical Waste Management Rules, 2016**
  - **Construction & Demolition Waste Management Rules, 2016**
  - **Hazardous and Other Wastes (Management and Transboundary Movement) Rules, 2016**
  - **E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2022**
  - **Battery Waste Management Rules, 2022**
  - **Noise Pollution (Regulation and Control) Rules, 2000**
  - **Coastal Regulation Zone (CRZ) Notification, 2019** (G.S.R. 37(E))
  - Ozone Depleting Substances (Regulation and Control) Rules, 2000; Wetlands (Conservation and Management) Rules, 2017
- Alongside EPA 1986 sit the **Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974**, the **Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981**, the **Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980** (renamed **Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam** by 2023 amendment), the **Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972** and the **National Green Tribunal Act, 2010**.
- **Why this could fit:** a person typing "EPCO rules" may have meant "EPA rules" or "EPCA rules" — and environmental law *is* a body of rules with a countable list.

### Candidate 5 — EPC / EPCs = Export Promotion Councils

- Sector bodies recognised by the Department of Commerce under the Foreign Trade Policy (e.g. FIEO, EPCES for EOUs/SEZs, CHEMEXCIL, CAPEXIL, Pharmexcil, Council for Leather Exports).
- **Count is contested in open sources**: figures of 14 (Department of Commerce-affiliated sector councils), 26+, and 38 (all EPCs and commodity boards) all circulate. `[UNVERIFIED — no single authoritative 2026 count located; the Ministry of Commerce EPC list should be treated as the authority.]`
- EPCs issue **Registration-cum-Membership Certificates (RCMCs)**; they do not make law. Poor fit for "how many total laws we have".

### Candidate 6 — other plausible expansions (lower probability)

| Expansion | What it is | Fit |
|---|---|---|
| **EPC (Engineering, Procurement & Construction)** | Contract form used in infrastructure; "EPC contract rules" is common industry usage (NHAI/MoRTH model EPC agreements) | Low — not "laws" |
| **EPC (European Patent Convention)** | Not Indian law; India is not an EPC member | Very low |
| **EPCG (Export Promotion Capital Goods) Scheme** | Real DGFT scheme with detailed rules under the Foreign Trade Policy | Moderate if the user is a trader |
| **ECO / e-commerce operator** | Statutory term under GST and Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020 | Low |
| **EPFO 3.0** | EPFO's current digital-reform programme (see below) | Merges into Candidate 2 |
| **EPC = Energy Performance Certificate** | UK concept; no Indian equivalent | Very low |

### RECOMMENDATION

**The most likely intended meaning is EPFO — the Employees' Provident Fund Organisation and its scheme rules.**

Reasoning:
1. **Phonetics and typing.** "EPCO" is one letter from "EPFO"; "F" and "C" are adjacent on a QWERTY row shift and are commonly confused in dictation. No other candidate is that close.
2. **The word "rules".** EPFO is popularly discussed exactly as "EPFO rules" / "PF rules" — withdrawal rules, contribution rules, pension rules. "IPC rules" and "EPCO rules" are not idiomatic; "PF rules" is.
3. **"Law for the common man."** EPF is the single body of Indian law that touches the largest number of ordinary salaried people directly and financially — roughly every formal-sector employee. Environmental rules and export councils do not.
4. **"How many total laws we have."** This is a general-astonishment question about the size of the statute book, typically prompted by someone hitting one confusing rule (a PF withdrawal rejection is a classic trigger) and then asking "how many of these are there?"
5. **Secondary reading:** if the user is in Madhya Pradesh or works in environment/CSR, EPCO (Bhopal) is a genuine correctly-spelled hit — but it makes no rules, so the question would still not parse.

**Suggested clarifying reply:** *"Did you mean EPFO (provident fund) rules, or the Indian Penal Code / BNS, or environmental (EPA 1986) rules? Also — India has roughly 800-900 central Acts in force plus several thousand state Acts."*

**Answer to the literal question, "how many total laws do we have":**
- **Central Acts in force:** the India Code portal listed **836 Central Acts** as at 28 August 2026; the Legislative Department has separately cited **859 Central Acts in force** (845 live + 14 spent), apart from Finance Acts. The gap is a counting/updating difference. `[Treat ~830-860 as the honest range; there is no single canonical number.]`
- **Including State Acts:** commonly cited as **over 5,000**.
- **Including rules, regulations, notifications and subordinate legislation:** **well over 10,000 instruments**, and by DPIIT's compliance-mapping work, **tens of thousands of distinct compliance obligations** (see Part B).

## A.2 — EPFO: the rules a worker should actually know (as at September 2026)

### The statutory architecture — and the big 2025-26 change

**This is the single most important verified finding for this section: the EPF & MP Act, 1952 has now been subsumed under the Code on Social Security, 2020, and the Code IS in force.**

Timeline:

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 2020 | **Code on Social Security, 2020** enacted (assent 28 Sept 2020), subsuming 9 social-security laws including the **EPF & MP Act, 1952** and the ESI Act, 1948 — but **not brought into force** for five years |
| **21 November 2025** | Government brings **all four Labour Codes into force** — Code on Wages 2019, Industrial Relations Code 2020, **Code on Social Security 2020**, and OSH & Working Conditions Code 2020 — rationalising **29 existing central labour laws into 4 Codes** |
| **29 June 2026** | Ministry of Labour & Employment **notifies the EPF Scheme 2026, EPS 2026 and EDLI Scheme 2026** under the Social Security Code, replacing the 1952 / 1995 / 1976 schemes |
| **1 July 2026** | The 2026 Schemes come into force (gazetted 1 July 2026) |

So the correct answer to "is EPF subsumed under the Code on Social Security 2020?" as of September 2026 is: **Yes — and unlike the position in 2020-2025, the Code is now actually operative, and the old EPF Scheme 1952 has been replaced by the EPF Scheme 2026.**

Caveat: **central rules and several state-specific rules under the Codes were still not fully in force** immediately after 21 November 2025, so a transition period applies and practitioners were advised to track state notifications. `[Status of every state's rules as at Sept 2026 not individually verified.]`

### The three schemes

| Scheme | Old instrument | New instrument (2026) | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Provident Fund | EPF Scheme, **1952** | **EPF Scheme, 2026** | Lump-sum retirement corpus + interest |
| Pension | Employees' Pension Scheme, **1995** | **EPS, 2026** | Monthly pension after 10 years' service |
| Insurance | EDLI Scheme, **1976** | **EDLI Scheme, 2026** | Death-in-service life cover, max **₹7,00,000** |

### Contributions — what actually comes out of a payslip

- **Employee: 12%** of basic wages + DA (+ retaining allowance), all of it into EPF.
- **Employer: 12%**, but split:
  - **8.33%** → **Employees' Pension Scheme (EPS)**, capped at the statutory wage ceiling
  - **3.67%** → EPF
- **Plus employer-only charges:** EDLI **0.50%**, and EPFO administrative charges. (The employer, not the worker, bears these.)
- **Reduced rate of 10%** applies to certain classes of establishment (e.g. fewer than 20 employees, sick units, specified industries).
- **Coverage threshold:** establishments with **20 or more employees**; voluntary coverage possible below that.

### The wage ceiling — ₹15,000, and the 2025-26 revision fight

- The statutory wage ceiling has been **₹15,000/month since 1 September 2014** (raised then from ₹6,500). **It has not moved for ~12 years.**
- **The EPF Scheme, 2026 kept the ceiling at ₹15,000.** Contributions on wages above ₹15,000 remain **voluntary** (employer + employee may agree to contribute on full wages).
- **Revision pipeline (all still proposals as of Sept 2026):**
  - The **Supreme Court directed the Centre and EPFO (reported January 2026) to decide within four months** on revising the wage ceiling, noting it had been static for 11 years.
  - Figures floated in policy discussion: **₹21,000**, **₹25,000**, up to **₹30,000**. **₹25,000 has the most traction.**
  - Press reports of **early August 2026** say the **Finance Ministry has approved ₹25,000**; **Union Cabinet approval is still awaited**.
  - A widely-reported **1 April 2027 effective date is journalistic expectation, not notified policy.**
  - `[UNVERIFIED — no gazette notification of a revised ceiling located as at 5 Sept 2026. Treat ₹15,000 as the operative figure and ₹25,000 as pending.]`

### Withdrawal rules — the October 2025 liberalisation, now embedded in EPF Scheme 2026

The **238th meeting of the EPFO Central Board of Trustees on 13 October 2025** approved the largest simplification of withdrawal rules in decades. Key elements, now carried into the EPF Scheme 2026:

1. **13 separate partial-withdrawal provisions collapsed into 3 categories:**
   - **Essential needs** — illness, education, marriage
   - **Housing needs** — purchase, construction, home-loan repayment, renovation
   - **Special circumstances** — notified contingencies (natural calamity, establishment closure, etc.)
2. **Up to 100% of the *eligible* balance** can be withdrawn (employee + employer share), where previously full withdrawal in service was barred.
3. **Minimum balance rule: 25% of the corpus must be retained**, so **75% is the accessible pool**. This is the price of the liberalisation and is the point most workers miss.
4. **Uniform minimum service reduced to 12 months** for all partial withdrawals (previously 5-7 years for several heads).
5. **Frequency raised:** education withdrawals up to **10 times**; marriage up to **5 times**.
6. **Full/final settlement on unemployment tightened:** under the EPF Scheme 2026, full withdrawal on unemployment is permitted only after **12 months** of unemployment — **up from the old 2-month rule**. This is a *restriction*, not a liberalisation, and is the single most consequential change for job-switchers.
7. **EPFO 3.0** — the digital programme: upgraded UAN member portal, target of **"zero-document", 100% auto-settled claims**, and (announced) ATM/UPI-based access to PF withdrawals.

### UAN — Universal Account Number

- A permanent **12-digit ID** issued to every member; links all PF accounts across all employers.
- Makes the balance **portable** — the member transfers the account rather than withdrawing on each job change.
- Aadhaar-seeding and UAN activation are now the gateway to online claims and to auto-settlement under EPFO 3.0.

### Pension (EPS) — eligibility

- **10 years of pensionable service** = entitlement to a monthly pension.
- Payable from **age 58**; **early pension from age 50** at a reduced rate.
- Under 10 years: withdrawal benefit / Scheme Certificate instead of pension.

### The "higher pension" saga

- **Supreme Court judgment of 4 November 2022** (*EPFO v. Sunil Kumar B.* line of cases) allowed eligible members to opt for pension on **actual salary** rather than on the capped wage.
- **15,24,365 applications** were forwarded by employers to EPFO up to **31 January 2025**.
- **1,65,621 demand notices** issued (as at 28 Jan 2025) requiring members to deposit additional past contributions.
- As at **March 2026** EPFO reported **over 99.2% of applications disposed of**; as at **5 August 2026**, **11,595 cases remained pending** (highest pendency: Punjab & Himachal Pradesh 3,004; Delhi/Uttarakhand/Jammu 1,389; Maharashtra ex-Mumbai 1,380).
- Practical effect: for most *retired* claimants the process is largely closed; the position of members **still in service** has been slower to settle. `[Detailed current status for in-service members not independently verified.]`

### VISHWAS 2026 and AMNESTY 2026

Launched with the new Schemes, both effective **29 June 2026, open for six months**:

- **VISHWAS, 2026** — one-time settlement of **damages and penalties** disputes under the EPF & MP Act 1952 / Social Security Code. Damages for defaults **before 14 June 2024** recalculated at reduced rates on payment of statutory interest. Fraud cases excluded. Fully online, time-bound.
- **AMNESTY, 2026** — one-time **regularisation for establishments running recognised PF Trusts without formal exemption**, with retrospective regularisation and relief from certain compliance requirements.
- An **Employees' Enrolment Campaign (EEC)** runs alongside, to bring unenrolled workers on the books.

### Summary card — "the ten EPF things a worker should know"

1. You contribute 12%; your employer contributes 12%, of which 8.33% goes to your **pension**, not your PF.
2. The wage ceiling is **₹15,000** (a hike to ₹25,000 is approved by the Finance Ministry but **not yet notified**).
3. Your **UAN** is permanent — **transfer**, don't withdraw, when you change jobs.
4. You can now access up to **75%** of your corpus for essential/housing/special needs; **25% must stay**.
5. Minimum service for any partial withdrawal is now just **12 months**.
6. Full withdrawal on unemployment now needs **12 months** out of work (was 2 months).
7. **10 years** of service = lifelong pension from 58 (or reduced from 50).
8. **EDLI** gives your family up to **₹7 lakh** if you die in service — free to you.
9. The **EPF Act 1952 is gone**; you are now under the **Code on Social Security, 2020** and the **EPF Scheme, 2026** (from 1 July 2026).
10. Interest is credited annually; check it on the UAN passbook — EPFO 3.0 is moving to document-free auto-settlement.

# TASK B — Law reform and simplification in India: making law understandable to ordinary citizens

## B.1 Repeal of obsolete laws

### The baseline problem

The **Ramanujam Committee**, set up by the Prime Minister in **2014** to identify obsolete laws within three months, found:

- **2,781 Central Acts** in existence as at **15 October 2014**
- Recommended repeal of **1,741 Central Acts**
- Of those, **340** were Central Acts on **State subjects**, repealable only by state legislatures

In parallel, the **Law Commission of India** ran its study **"Legal Enactments: Simplification and Streamlining" (LESS)** and produced four interim reports on obsolete laws:

| Report | Date | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| **248th** | 12 Sept 2014 | **72 laws** for immediate repeal |
| **249th** | 13 Oct 2014 | **88 laws** for wholesale repeal + **25** for partial repeal |
| **250th** | 2014-15 | **73 laws** for complete repeal |
| **251st** | 2015 | 54 laws studied, **30** recommended for complete repeal |

Cumulative across Reports 248-250: **258 archaic laws** recommended for repeal; **~288 with Report 251**.

### What Parliament actually repealed

| Instrument | Acts repealed |
|---|---|
| Repealing and Amending Act, 2015 | 35 |
| Repealing and Amending (Second) Act, 2015 | 90 |
| Repealing and Amending (Third) Bill, 2015 | 295 (proposed) |
| Appropriation Acts (Repeal) Act, 2016 | 758 |
| Repealing and Amending Act, 2016 | (further tranche) |
| Repealing and Amending Act, 2017 | 104 |
| Repealing and Amending Act, 2019 | 58 |
| **Repealing and Amending Act, 2023** | **76** (+ minor amendment to the Factoring Regulation Act, 2011) |
| **Repealing and Amending Act, 2025** | **71** |

**Latest total (verified):** Union Law Minister **Arjun Ram Meghwal** told Parliament in **December 2025**, replying on the Repealing and Amending Bill 2025, that since May 2014 **1,577 laws have been repealed** — of which **1,562 were completely repealed and 15 re-enacted**. The commonly quoted "~1,486" figure is an earlier vintage; **1,562 / 1,577 is the current number.**

### The Repealing and Amending Act, 2025 (the newest one)

- Passed by **Lok Sabha 16 December 2025**, **Rajya Sabha 17 December 2025**; **Presidential assent 20 December 2025**.
- **Repeals 71 obsolete laws spanning 1886-2023.**
- Also **amends four live statutes**:
  - **General Clauses Act, 1897** — updates terminology for registered post
  - **Code of Civil Procedure, 1908** — same modernisation of service-by-post terminology
  - **Indian Succession Act, 1925** — removes the requirement to obtain court validation of wills in certain cases
  - **Disaster Management Act, 2005** — corrects a drafting error

### States

State repeal has historically lagged badly (the Centre for Civil Society's long-running critique: "The old order changeth not"). Recent movement:

- **Maharashtra Repealing Bill, 2026** — passed by the Maharashtra Legislative Assembly **March 2026**, scrapping **80 obsolete laws**.
- Vidhi Centre for Legal Policy has run a state-by-state repeal research programme (e.g. *Repealing Obsolete Laws in Maharashtra*, Parts I and II covering pre- and post-independence statutes).
- NITI Aayog published a case study, *"Old is Not Always Gold — A Case Study on Repealing Statutes"*.
- `[UNVERIFIED — no consolidated all-India count of state Acts repealed since 2014 exists in open sources.]`

### The current institutional driver

The **23rd Law Commission of India** was constituted for a three-year term, **1 September 2024 to 31 August 2027**, with an explicit mandate to **identify obsolete laws for repeal**, **audit laws affecting the poor**, and identify laws needing amendment for current economic needs. Composition: full-time chairperson, four full-time members, the Secretaries of the Legal Affairs and Legislative Departments ex-officio, and up to five part-time members. `[The named chairperson reported in one secondary source appeared unreliable and is not repeated here — verify against the Law Commission gazette notification.]`

## B.2 Decriminalisation — the Jan Vishwas track

### Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act, 2023

- Notified **11 August 2023**.
- **Decriminalised 183 provisions** across **42 Central Acts** administered by **19 Ministries/Departments**.
- Method: replace imprisonment with **monetary penalties** and administrative/adjudicatory enforcement; introduce periodic revision of penalty amounts.

### Jan Vishwas 2.0 — the 2025 Bill that did *not* become law

- Union Cabinet approval **12 August 2025**; introduced in **Lok Sabha 18 August 2025**.
- Proposed amendments to **355 provisions** across **16 Central Acts** administered by **10 Ministries/Departments** — of which **288 to be decriminalised** and **67 amended for "ease of living"**.
- **Referred to a Select Committee of the Lok Sabha chaired by Shri Tejasvi Surya**, which held **49 sittings** and reported on **13 March 2026**.
- **The 2025 Bill was WITHDRAWN on 17 March 2026.** (This corrects the premise in the task brief: the "~355 provisions across 16 acts" Bill was never enacted.)

### Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act, 2026 — the one that passed

- Introduced in **Lok Sabha 27 March 2026** by MoS Commerce & Industry **Shri Jitin Prasada**.
- Scope massively expanded on the Select Committee's advice:
  - **784 provisions** amended
  - across **79 Central Acts**
  - administered by **23 Ministries**
  - of which **717 provisions decriminalised** and **67** amended for ease of living
- **Passed Lok Sabha 1 April 2026; passed Rajya Sabha 2 April 2026.**
- This is now **by a wide margin the largest single decriminalisation statute in Indian history** — roughly **4x** the 2023 Act on Acts covered and **~4x** on provisions decriminalised.
- `[UNVERIFIED — exact date of Presidential assent and commencement notification not located; treat as "passed by both Houses April 2026".]`

### State Jan Vishwas laws

A coordinated state-level wave followed, reported (BusinessToday, 21 November 2025) as **seven NDA states** pushing Jan Vishwas-style reform simultaneously:

| State | Scope |
|---|---|
| **Gujarat** | Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Bill — **516 provisions across 11 laws**, 6 departments (the largest state effort) |
| **Haryana** | Jan Vishwas Ordinance 2025 — **164 provisions across 42 Acts**, **17 departments** (broadest cross-departmental spread) |
| **Chhattisgarh** | Passed July 2025 — jail terms removed from **163 provisions across 8 Acts**, moving wholly to monetary penalties |
| **Madhya Pradesh** | MP Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act, 2024 (Act 1 of 2025) — **144 sections across 24 Acts** |
| Others | Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan, Uttarakhand, Assam reported in the same wave `[UNVERIFIED — individual counts not confirmed]` |

### The "compliance burden" evidence base

- **Economic Survey 2024-25** (tabled 31 January 2025) made **deregulation its central theme**, calling for **"Ease of Doing Business 2.0"**. Key arguments:
  - **MSMEs bear disproportionately high compliance costs** in both time and money.
  - Regulatory burden **holds back formalisation and labour productivity, limits employment growth, chokes innovation and depresses growth**.
  - **Unrealistic regulatory standards imported from high-state-capacity nations** burden the system without effective enforcement.
  - Recommended a shift from "regulate everything" to **trust-based, risk-proportionate regulation**.
- A **Task Force on Compliance Reduction and Deregulation** was constituted in **January 2025**; a **Deregulation Commission** was announced to nudge **states** to cut compliance burden (Business Today, Feb 2025).
- **DPIIT cumulative scorecard (PIB, 2026): more than 47,000 compliances reduced**, comprising:
  - **16,108** simplified
  - **22,287** digitised
  - **4,458** decriminalised
  - **4,270** identified as redundant and removed
- **Business Reforms Action Plan (BRAP) 2024** extends the exercise to states and UTs.

**Reading for the "common man" question:** this entire track is framed as **ease of doing *business***, not ease of *living*. Only **67 of 784** provisions in the 2026 Act are labelled ease-of-living. The citizen-facing benefit is real but incidental.

## B.3 Consolidation into Codes

The dominant reform technique of the last five years: replace many statutes with one code, and shrink the section count.

### Labour: 29 → 4

| Code | Year | Subsumes |
|---|---|---|
| **Code on Wages** | 2019 | Minimum Wages Act 1948, Payment of Wages Act 1936, Payment of Bonus Act 1965, Equal Remuneration Act 1976 |
| **Industrial Relations Code** | 2020 | Trade Unions Act 1926, Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act 1946, Industrial Disputes Act 1947 |
| **Code on Social Security** | 2020 | EPF & MP Act 1952, ESI Act 1948, Maternity Benefit Act 1961, Payment of Gratuity Act 1972, and others (9 laws) |
| **OSH & Working Conditions Code** | 2020 | Factories Act 1948, Mines Act 1952, Contract Labour Act 1970, Inter-State Migrant Workmen Act 1979, and others (13 laws) |

- **All four brought into force 21 November 2025**, rationalising **29 central labour laws into 4**.
- Headline citizen-facing entitlements announced with commencement: **mandatory appointment letters** for all workers; **social security coverage extended to gig and platform workers**; **statutory minimum wage for all workers**; **women permitted to work at night and in all categories of work** with consent and safety conditions.
- **Caveat:** central rules and many state rules were **not simultaneously in force**; implementation remains staged into 2026.

### Criminal law: 3 → 3 (renamed and restructured)

- **BNS 2023** (511 → **358 sections**), **BNSS 2023**, **BSA 2023**, all in force **1 July 2024**.
- Genuine consolidation gains: offences against women and children in one chapter; colonial vocabulary removed; some procedural timelines made statutory.
- **Not** a pure simplification: three parallel bodies of case law now run (pre-1 July 2024 offences continue under IPC/CrPC/Evidence Act), which in the short run **increases** complexity for ordinary litigants.

### Direct tax: Income-tax Act, 2025

**Verified against the task brief's numbers — they check out, with one nuance:**

| Metric | Income-tax Act, 1961 | **Income-tax Act, 2025** |
|---|---|---|
| Sections | **819** (effective, counting lettered insertions) | **536** |
| Chapters | 23 | **23** |
| Schedules | 14 | **16** |
| Rules | 511 | **333** |
| Forms | 399 | **190** |

- Passed by Parliament in **2025**; **in force from 1 April 2026** (applies from FY 2026-27).
- New **Income-tax Rules, 2026** notified by CBDT on **20 March 2026**.
- Drafting techniques used — these are the real transferable lessons:
  - **Explanations and provisos folded into the main section text** (no more hunting through nested provisos)
  - **Tables and formulas replace narrative prose**
  - Redundant and obsolete provisions deleted outright
  - Cross-references made direct rather than chained
  - "Previous year"/"assessment year" replaced with a single **"tax year"** concept
- **Section 536** is the transitional provision — 22 sub-clauses preserving the old Act for earlier years.
- **Nuance on the brief's "23 chapters":** the 1961 Act also had 23 chapters, so the chapter count did not fall; the compression is in **sections (819→536)** and **rules/forms**.

### Direct Tax Code (DTC)

- The long-discussed **Direct Tax Code** (drafts in 2009, 2010, 2013; DTC task force 2019) has effectively been **absorbed into the Income-tax Act, 2025** rather than enacted under its own name.
- Consensus position in tax commentary: **the DTC as a separate instrument is not expected to be enacted**; its principles are in the 2025 Act.

### GST — "GST 2.0"

- **56th GST Council meeting, 3 September 2025**: abolished the **12% and 28%** slabs; created a **40%** rate for luxury/sin goods. Implemented **22 September 2025**.
- Resulting structure: **0%, 5%, 18%, 40%**, with **5% and 18% as the two standard rates**.
- Migration: **~99% of items** in the old 12% slab moved **down to 5%**; **~90%** of the old 28% slab moved **down to 18%**, ~10% up to 40%.
- All medicines at a concessional **5%** (or nil).
- This is the clearest example in Indian law of simplification that a **consumer** can actually perceive.

### "One Nation One" reforms (consolidation branding)

One Nation One Tax (GST), One Nation One Ration Card, One Nation One Grid, One Nation One Election (constitutional amendment bills referred to a Joint Parliamentary Committee), One Nation One Subscription (research journals), One Nation One Health Card (ABHA). These are **administrative unification** rather than legal simplification, but they are politically framed as the same project.

### Model Tenancy Act, 2021

- Approved by the Union Cabinet in **June 2021** and circulated to States/UTs for adoption. It is a **model law with no direct force** — it binds nobody unless a State enacts or adapts it.
- **Adoption is very poor: only 4 States** are recorded as having tenancy laws in line with it — **Tamil Nadu (2017)** and **Andhra Pradesh (2018)**, both of which pre-date the Model Act, and **Assam** and **Uttar Pradesh (both 2021)**.
- Fact-check note: recurring viral claims of a "new central Rent Agreement Act 2025" are **false** — the Centre has notified no such law.
- **Lesson for the framework in B.7:** model laws without adoption incentives do not reach citizens. Tenancy is one of the highest-volume citizen legal interactions in India and remains governed by a patchwork of colonial-era state Rent Control Acts.

## B.4 Plain-language law and access in Indian languages

### Plain drafting: the state of play

**There is no Indian equivalent of a Plain Language Act, and no binding plain-drafting standard for central legislation.** What exists is:

- The **Legislative Department, Ministry of Law and Justice** drafts all central bills and maintains internal drafting conventions and a Manual on the drafting of legislation, but these are **internal and not citizen-facing standards**, and there is **no statutory obligation** to draft in plain language or to publish a plain summary. `[UNVERIFIED — no publicly available, current "plain language" drafting directive from the Legislative Department was located.]`
- The most substantial plain-drafting work in India is **civil society, not government**:
  - **Vidhi Centre for Legal Policy's SARAL initiative** — "**SARAL**" = **S**imple, **A**ccessible, **R**ational and **A**ctionable **L**aws.
    - **Manual on Plain Language Drafting (2017)** — the first Indian drafting manual of its kind
    - **The SARAL Manual (March 2023)** — the expanded successor, "a plain language drafting manual for better laws", aimed at eliminating legalese from Indian statutes
    - **The SARAL Private Instruments Manual (2025)** — extends the method to contracts, notices and private legal documents
    - **The SARAL Survey** — India's first public-facing survey gathering evidence on how legalese harms comprehension
    - **SARAL Saathi** companion handbooks; dedicated portal at saral.vidhilegalpolicy.in
    - Vidhi's own position is notably nuanced: plain drafting is **not** just word-substitution, it is a communication-design responsibility (see their blog *"Why we must move beyond simplistic approaches to plain drafting"*).
- **What good practice already looks like inside government:** the **Income-tax Act, 2025** drafting method (tables and formulas instead of prose, provisos folded into sections, direct cross-references) is effectively plain-drafting applied to one statute. It is the strongest domestic proof of concept and is **not yet a general rule**.

### Translation into the 22 scheduled languages

- The Constitution's **Eighth Schedule** lists **22 languages**: Assamese, Bengali, Bodo, Dogri, Gujarati, Hindi, Kannada, Kashmiri, Konkani, Maithili, Malayalam, Manipuri, Marathi, Nepali, Odia, Punjabi, Sanskrit, Santali, Sindhi, Tamil, Telugu, Urdu.
- **India Code** (indiacode.gov.in), the Legislative Department's repository of central, state and UT enactments plus subordinate legislation, was **relaunched on 13 August 2026**. It held **836 Central Acts** as at **28 August 2026**. Hindi versions of central Acts are published; **coverage in the other 20 non-English languages is partial at best.** `[UNVERIFIED — no published per-language coverage statistics for India Code were located. This is a real and important data gap.]`
- **Judgments are far ahead of statutes on translation.** Supreme Court e-SCR, using **SUVAS (Supreme Court Vidhik Anuvaad Software**, launched November 2019, built with MeitY support):
  - As at **March 2025 (reported 28 March 2025)**: **36,344 judgments translated into Hindi** and **47,439 into other vernacular languages**, uploaded to the e-SCR portal with High Court assistance.
  - An earlier Ministry of Law figure: 36,271 into Hindi and 17,142 into 16 other regional languages.
  - Coverage spans Hindi plus ~16 languages including Punjabi, Kannada, Tamil, Gujarati, Marathi, Telugu, Malayalam, Odia, Bengali, Nepali, Urdu, Assamese, Garo, Khasi, Konkani.
  - Republic Day 2023 marked the first bulk release (1,268 translated judgments); by 2023 SUVAS had translated **over 31,000 of ~36,000** SC judgments.
- **Bhashini** (National Language Translation Mission) provides real-time AI translation across the 22 scheduled languages and tribal languages, and is the natural infrastructure layer for statute translation.
- **AI4Bharat's IndicTrans2** is the leading open-source NMT model covering all 22 scheduled languages — freely available, and directly usable for statute translation at scale.
- **Lok Sabha proceedings** are being made available in all 22 Eighth Schedule languages (recent additions: Kashmiri, Konkani, Santali), with translation work assigned to the **Centre for Human-Centric AI at IIT Madras**.

**The asymmetry is the story:** India has translated ~84,000 *judgments* but has no comparable programme for the *statutes and rules* that citizens actually have to comply with.

### Law Commission work on simplification

- **LESS study** and **Reports 248-251** (2014-15) — the obsolete-laws series (see B.1).
- The **23rd Law Commission (Sept 2024 - Aug 2027)** has an express mandate to identify obsolete laws and to **audit laws affecting the poor** — the closest thing to a standing "law for the common man" review function in government.
- `[UNVERIFIED — no 2024-2026 Law Commission report specifically on plain-language drafting or statute simplification was located. The 23rd Commission's report output as at Sept 2026 could not be confirmed.]`

### Parliamentary scrutiny of drafting quality

- The **Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice** is the standing forum that examines legislative quality; the **Committee on Subordinate Legislation** in each House scrutinises rules and notifications.
- The **Jan Vishwas 2025 Select Committee (Tejasvi Surya, 49 sittings)** is a live example of committee scrutiny materially improving a bill — it caused the 2025 Bill (16 Acts) to be withdrawn and replaced with a far more comprehensive 2026 Bill (79 Acts).
- **Pre-Legislative Consultation Policy, 2014** requires draft bills to be published for 30 days of public comment; compliance is widely reported as patchy. `[UNVERIFIED — no current compliance audit located.]`

### The "Bharatiya" renaming debate

Renaming statutes in Hindi/Sanskrit (Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam; Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam; Vayuyan Vidheyak) provoked a sustained accessibility objection:

- **Article 348** requires the authoritative text of Bills and Acts of Parliament, and proceedings in the Supreme Court and High Courts, to be **in English**. Critics argue Hindi/Sanskrit titles on English-text statutes sit uneasily with **Article 348(1)(b)**.
- The **Madras Bar Association** passed a unanimous resolution against the renaming; a **writ petition in the Madras High Court** sought a declaration that the Hindi names are unconstitutional, noting only **9 States and 2 UTs** have Hindi as an official language.
- **Tamil Nadu CM M.K. Stalin** called it "linguistic imperialism"; DMK's T.R. Baalu and Congress's P. Chidambaram raised similar objections; commentary described a "north-south divide over Hindi names".
- **Accessibility point that cuts both ways:** a name in an Indian language is more accessible to a Hindi speaker and *less* accessible to a Tamil, Malayalam, Bengali or Assamese speaker than the previous English name. Renaming is not the same as translation — the *contents* were not made available in 22 languages.

### Legal literacy programmes

- **Hamara Samvidhan Hamara Samman** — launched **24 January 2024** by the Vice-President at the Dr B.R. Ambedkar International Centre, for the **75th year of the Republic**. Delivered under the Department of Justice's **DISHA** scheme. Components: Panch Pran pledge, legal literacy workshops, Tele-Law and Nyaya Bandhu integration.
  - **Portal launched 16 July 2024** as a repository on constitutional rights, legal literacy and fundamental duties.
  - Reported reach: **over 1 crore citizens through 13,700+ events** across 2024-25.
  - Transitioned on **24 January 2025** into its successor phase, **"Hamara Samvidhan Hamara Swabhiman"**.
- **NALSA / DISHA legal aid and awareness (latest official figures):**
  - **Over 16.6 lakh people** received free legal aid and advice in **FY 2025-26** (Law Ministry to Lok Sabha, March 2026).
  - **Tele-Law**: **over 1.12 crore pre-litigation advice sessions** delivered, through a network of **~2.5 lakh Common Service Centres** at Gram Panchayat level across **777 districts**.
  - **Nyaya Bandhu** (pro bono lawyer app): **10,263 advocates registered** (as at 28 February); ~**14,888 women beneficiaries** registered to 30 June 2025.
  - **Legal Literacy and Legal Awareness Programme**: **over 1.21 crore beneficiaries** reached.
  - NALSA also publishes **"Know Your Rights"** booklets and runs Lok Adalats and Legal Services Clinics; village-level **Para Legal Volunteers (PLVs)** are its frontline. `[UNVERIFIED — current PLV headcount not located.]`
- **NCERT / NEP 2020:** NEP 2020 lists **legal literacy** explicitly among the "critical life skills" under Education for All (alongside financial, digital and health literacy), and NCERT's **Cell for National Centre for Literacy** carries that mandate. The **National Curriculum Framework for School Education (2023)** grounds curricular values in the Constitution. **However, there is no dedicated legal-literacy subject in the school curriculum** — it sits inside civics/social science and adult-education streams. This is a significant gap: legal literacy is named as a goal but is not a taught, examined subject.

### The gaps, stated plainly

1. **No official plain-language layer.** India Code gives you the *text* of 836 Acts and nothing else. Nyaaya gives you plain language for a *subset*. Nobody offers plain-language coverage of the whole operative statute book.
2. **Statutes lag judgments on translation by an order of magnitude.** ~84,000 judgments translated; no published count of Acts available in all 22 languages.
3. **Subordinate legislation is invisible.** The rules, notifications, circulars and standing orders that actually govern a citizen (EPF scheme rules, e-waste rules, GST notifications) are the least summarised, least translated and least searchable layer.
4. **State law is a black hole.** Over 5,000 state Acts and vastly more state rules, with wildly uneven digitisation and near-zero plain-language coverage. Tenancy, land, police, excise, shops-and-establishments — the laws most citizens meet — are all state law.
5. **No "what changed" service.** When the EPF Scheme 1952 became the EPF Scheme 2026, or the IPC became the BNS, no official plain-language diff was published for citizens.
6. **Funding fragility.** The best plain-law asset in the country (Nyaaya) is philanthropically funded and has no statutory backing or sustained public funding.
7. **Discovery is broken.** A citizen searching "can I withdraw my PF" reaches SEO-optimised commercial content (bank blogs, fintech pages) long before reaching EPFO, India Code or Nyaaya.
8. **No feedback loop from confusion to drafting.** Nothing routes the questions citizens actually ask back to the Legislative Department to fix the drafting.

## B.6 International comparisons — models India could borrow

### United States

- **Statutes:** federal law is consolidated into the **United States Code**, organised into **~54 titles** (50 originally; several added/reorganised). Individual public laws number in the tens of thousands historically; the Code is the consolidated, citable version. `[UNVERIFIED — no reliable single count of "federal laws in force" exists; the Code-title structure is the meaningful unit.]`
- **Regulations:** the **Code of Federal Regulations** has **50 titles**, ~**200-243 bound volumes**, and stood at **188,343 pages at year-end 2021** (National Archives has not logged 2022-24). Annual *Federal Register* rule counts are tracked by the Competitive Enterprise Institute's "Ten Thousand Commandments".
- **Plain Writing Act of 2010** — requires federal agencies to use "clear Government communication that the public can understand and use"; mandates agency plain-writing officers, training, annual compliance reports and a plain-language section on each agency website. **Applies to agency documents, not to statutes themselves.**
- **Model value for India:** the *mechanism* — a named officer, a published compliance report, and a website section — is cheap and auditable.

### United Kingdom

- **Statute Law (Repeals) Acts** — **19 such Acts** to date, between them **repealing over 3,000 Acts in their entirety** and partially repealing thousands more. Examples: **1995 Act repealed 481 enactments**; **2004 Act repealed 68 Acts wholly and parts of 400 others**; **2008 Act repealed 250+ Acts in full and 50+ in part**.
- **Consolidation Acts** — the **Law Commission was responsible for 220 consolidation Acts between 1965 and 2006**, plus three since: **Charities Act 2011**, **Co-operative and Community Benefit Societies Act 2014**, **Sentencing Act 2020**.
- The key institutional design: a **special parliamentary procedure for Law Commission bills** (consolidation and repeals bills get expedited, low-controversy passage), so tidying the statute book does not compete for scarce floor time.
- **legislation.gov.uk** publishes **revised, as-amended** text — the "point in time" feature lets a user see the law as it stood on any date.
- **Model value for India:** (a) the fast-track procedure for repeal/consolidation bills; (b) **as-amended consolidated text**, which India Code does not reliably provide.

### New Zealand

- **Plain Language Act 2022** (No. 54), **royal assent 21 October 2022**. Requires **public service agencies and Crown agents** to use plain language in documents to the public. Defines plain language as language **"appropriate to the intended audience"** and **"clear, concise, and well organised"**. Requires each agency to appoint a **Plain Language Officer** and report to the Public Service Commission. Closely modelled on the US Plain Writing Act 2010.
- New Zealand also runs **revision Bills** (Legislation Act) — a standing mechanism to re-enact old statutes in modern language **without changing their effect**.
- **Model value for India: the revision-bill mechanism is the single most transferable idea** — it allows modernising the *language* of an Act through a light-touch parliamentary process precisely because substance is unchanged.

### Comparative table

| | India | US | UK | NZ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Consolidated as-amended official text | Partial (India Code) | Yes (US Code) | Yes (legislation.gov.uk) | Yes |
| Statutory plain-language duty | **None** | Plain Writing Act 2010 (agency docs) | None statutory; drafting guidance | **Plain Language Act 2022** |
| Standing repeals mechanism | Ad hoc Repealing & Amending Acts | Ad hoc | **Statute Law (Repeals) Acts, 19 to date** | Revision Bills |
| Standing consolidation mechanism | None | None | **Law Commission consolidation, 220+ Acts** | Revision Bills |
| Official multilingual statute text | Hindi + English (partial) | English | English/Welsh | English/Māori |

## B.7 A 10-point framework: bringing all law to the common man

*Research synthesis. Each point states the proposal, the evidence base from the sections above, and the nearest existing hook so it does not start from zero.*

### 1. One statutory citizen-law portal — "Bharat Kanoon" — built on India Code

**Proposal.** Merge into a single official destination: authoritative as-amended statutory text (India Code), a plain-language summary layer for every Act, subordinate legislation attached to its parent Act, state law, and an "as at date" time-machine view. One URL, one search box, one identity for Indian law.
**Evidence.** India Code (relaunched 13 Aug 2026, 836 Central Acts) holds text but no explanation; PRS explains Bills but not operative law; Nyaaya explains a subset. No single place exists.
**Model.** legislation.gov.uk point-in-time text.
**Hook.** The August 2026 India Code relaunch is the natural moment to add the layer rather than build a new site.

### 2. Plain-language summaries of the top 50 citizen-facing Acts, in all 22 scheduled languages

**Proposal.** Pick the 50 Acts an ordinary person actually collides with — Social Security Code and EPF Scheme 2026, BNS/BNSS/BSA, Consumer Protection Act 2019, Motor Vehicles Act, RTI Act, Income-tax Act 2025, RERA, DV Act, POSH, SC/ST (PoA) Act, Sale of Goods/contract basics, state Rent Control, Shops & Establishments, Land Revenue Codes, Registration Act, Succession Acts. Commission a 1,500-word plain summary of each, then translate into all 22 Eighth Schedule languages using **Bhashini / IndicTrans2** with human legal review.
**Evidence.** ~84,000 *judgments* are translated via SUVAS, but there is no comparable statute programme; India Code language coverage beyond Hindi is unverified and likely thin.
**Cost logic.** 50 Acts × 22 languages = 1,100 documents — trivially achievable with existing NMT infrastructure plus review.

### 3. One-page "rights cards" — the offline artefact

**Proposal.** For each of ~30 life events (job loss, PF withdrawal, arrest, FIR refusal, eviction, dowry harassment, workplace sexual harassment, consumer defect, wage non-payment, land mutation, RTI filing, domestic violence), a single A4 card: what the law gives you, the three steps to claim it, the section numbers, and the phone number/portal that helps. Printed and distributed through NALSA legal services clinics, CSCs, panchayats, schools and post offices; QR-linked to the full summary.
**Evidence.** NALSA already publishes "Know Your Rights" booklets and reaches 1.21 crore people through legal awareness; **Tele-Law reaches 2.5 lakh CSCs across 777 districts** — the distribution rail already exists and is India's real comparative advantage.
**Design principle.** The card is for the person with a feature phone and no data plan — the population every digital-first plan misses.

### 4. Legal literacy as a taught, examined school subject

**Proposal.** A defined legal-literacy strand in Classes 6-12 under the National Curriculum Framework: rights and duties, how to file an FIR and an RTI, consumer remedies, contracts you sign as an adult (rent, employment, loan), what a court actually does. Teacher training through SCERTs; NALSA legal literacy clubs formalised in schools.
**Evidence.** NEP 2020 **already names legal literacy** as a critical life skill, and NCF 2023 grounds values in the Constitution — but there is **no dedicated taught subject**. The gap is between policy statement and curriculum.
**Hook.** *Hamara Samvidhan Hamara Swabhiman* already puts constitutional literacy into schools at campaign scale (1 crore+ citizens, 13,700+ events); convert campaign into curriculum.

### 5. A mandatory plain-language summary attached to every Bill and every rule

**Proposal.** Amend the Rules of Procedure of both Houses (and the Pre-Legislative Consultation Policy) to require every Bill, and every set of statutory rules, to be accompanied by (a) a **one-page plain-language Explanatory Statement** in prescribed format and (b) translations into the 22 scheduled languages before the vote. Extend to Ordinances and to subordinate legislation laid before Parliament.
**Evidence.** PRS already produces exactly this artefact for free, proving it is feasible in a few hundred words; but it is unofficial and does not travel with the Bill. Article 348's English-authoritative-text rule is untouched — the summary is explanatory, not authoritative, which neatly sidesteps the "Bharatiya" naming controversy.
**Model.** US Plain Writing Act mechanics: named officer, prescribed format, published annual compliance report.

### 6. Sunset clauses and a statutory decennial review duty

**Proposal.** Default sunset (10-15 years) on regulatory statutes and on all subordinate legislation, with active re-enactment required; plus a statutory duty on every Ministry to review its Acts and rules every ten years and publish the review.
**Evidence.** The Ramanujam Committee had to find **1,741** dead Acts in 2014 because nothing ever expired; **1,562 Acts** have since had to be repealed one tranche at a time, and the job is still not finished (71 more in December 2025, 80 in Maharashtra in March 2026). Sunsetting converts a recurring political clean-up into an automatic process.
**Model.** UK Statute Law (Repeals) Acts — 19 of them, 3,000+ Acts repealed — show that even a standing mechanism needs a forcing function.

### 7. A standing Consolidation and Revision Commission with a fast-track parliamentary route

**Proposal.** Give the Law Commission (or a dedicated standing body) a permanent consolidation mandate plus a **special expedited procedure** in both Houses for two classes of bill: (a) **repeals bills**, (b) **revision bills** that re-enact an existing Act in modern plain language **without changing its effect**, certified as such by the Law Officer.
**Evidence.** The **UK Law Commission produced 220 consolidation Acts (1965-2006)** because it had a procedure that did not compete for floor time; **New Zealand's revision Bills** do the same for language. India has consolidated brilliantly when it tried (29 labour laws → 4 Codes; IPC 511 → BNS 358 sections; Income-tax 819 → 536 sections) but only through large, politically expensive set-piece reforms.
**Hook.** The **23rd Law Commission (Sept 2024-Aug 2027)** already has an obsolete-laws and "audit laws affecting the poor" mandate. Add revision powers to it.

### 8. Extend Jan Vishwas from ease-of-*business* to ease-of-*living*

**Proposal.** A "Jan Suvidha" companion track: a systematic decriminalisation and simplification sweep of the provisions that criminalise or entangle **ordinary citizens** rather than businesses — minor municipal and police offences, small-cause procedures, documentary requirements, affidavit and attestation demands, tenancy and land-mutation processes. Pair it with adoption incentives for the **Model Tenancy Act** (only 4 states in 5 years).
**Evidence.** The 2026 Jan Vishwas Act decriminalised **717 provisions across 79 Acts** — but only **67 of 784** amended provisions were tagged "ease of living". The compliance-reduction scorecard (**47,000+ compliances** reduced) is measured almost entirely in business terms. The citizen has no equivalent scorecard.
**Also:** publish a **citizen compliance burden index** alongside the business one.

### 9. AI legal assistants with mandatory citation, built on official corpora

**Proposal.** A publicly funded, open, multilingual legal question-answering assistant that (a) answers in the user's language via Bhashini/IndicTrans2, (b) **cites the Act, section and date of the version relied on**, (c) refuses rather than guesses, (d) says explicitly when the question needs a lawyer and routes to **Tele-Law / Nyaya Bandhu**, and (e) logs the questions asked.
**Evidence.** The corpora already exist and are open: **India Code** (836 Central Acts + state Acts + subordinate legislation), **Indian Kanoon** (judgments), **e-SCR** (~84,000 translated judgments), **Justice Hub** (CivicDataLab open datasets), **Nyaaya** (plain-language explainers), **IndicTrans2** (open NMT for all 22 languages).
**Guardrail.** Uncited generative legal advice is worse than no advice. The citation requirement is the whole design — and the query log becomes the feedback loop for point 10.
**Risk to name explicitly:** search results for citizen legal questions are already dominated by SEO-driven commercial content; an official, cited assistant is a corrective, not a novelty.

### 10. Community paralegals as the last mile — and a drafting feedback loop

**Proposal.** Professionalise and scale **Para Legal Volunteers** into a paid, certified community paralegal cadre attached to Legal Services Authorities and CSCs, with a defined scope of practice (form-filling, entitlement claims, first-instance grievance, referral). Then **close the loop**: aggregate the questions coming through PLVs, Tele-Law, and the AI assistant into a quarterly report to the Legislative Department identifying the specific provisions citizens cannot understand — and require a drafting response.
**Evidence.** **16.6 lakh people received free legal aid in FY 2025-26**; **1.12 crore Tele-Law advice sessions**; **10,263 Nyaya Bandhu advocates**. The human infrastructure is the largest of its kind anywhere and is currently used only to dispense advice, never to diagnose the statute book.
**Why this is the keystone.** Points 1-9 push law outward to citizens. Point 10 is the only one that pushes citizens' confusion back into how law is written. Without it, every simplification effort is a guess about what people find hard.

### Sequencing note

Points **2, 3 and 5** are achievable within 12-18 months on existing budgets and infrastructure. Points **1, 9 and 10** are 2-3 year builds. Points **6 and 7** need parliamentary procedure changes and are the highest-leverage but slowest. Point **4** runs on the education cycle. Point **8** needs a political decision to reframe deregulation around citizens rather than firms.
