# How Law Works in India — The Machinery Explained Simply

*A plain-language guide for ordinary citizens. Current as of September 2026.*

Most Indians meet "the law" only when something goes wrong — a property dispute, a cheque bounces, a police complaint, a consumer grievance, a tenancy fight. At that point everyone throws around words like Act, Rule, Notification, FIR, Tribunal, Bail — and it all sounds like a foreign language. This guide explains the machinery in plain English, with examples, so you know what each piece does, who runs it, and how to use it.

---

## 1. Types of law — and the vocabulary that confuses everyone

Indian law isn't one thing. It is a stack of documents, each with a different source of power, and each ranking above or below the others. Here is the vocabulary, from the top down.

| Term | What it actually is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Constitution** | The supreme law. Every other law must obey it. Courts can strike down anything that conflicts with it. | The Constitution of India, 1950 |
| **Act / Statute** | A law passed by a legislature (Parliament or a State Assembly). Starts life as a "Bill". | The Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 |
| **Code** | A large Act that consolidates an entire area of law into one systematic document. | Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (crimes); Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (criminal procedure) |
| **Bill** | A draft law before it is passed. Not law yet — has no legal force. | The Digital Personal Data Protection Bill (before it became an Act in 2023) |
| **Ordinance** | A law issued directly by the President (or a Governor, for a state) when Parliament/Assembly is not in session. Has full force of law immediately, but is temporary. | Various ordinances promulgated between Parliament sessions |
| **Rules** | Detailed operating instructions made by the government (usually a ministry) *under the authority an Act gives it*. This is where most day-to-day compliance actually lives. | Income-tax Rules, 1962 made under the Income-tax Act |
| **Regulations** | Similar to Rules, but usually made by a regulator (RBI, SEBI, etc.) for its own sector, under powers the parent Act gives it. | SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015 |
| **Notifications** | Official announcements in the Gazette of India that bring a law into force, appoint a date, fix a rate, or declare something (e.g., a "notified area"). This is how the government pulls a lever the Act gave it. | A notification fixing GST rates on an item |
| **Orders** | Directions or decisions issued under a law for specific situations, or executive orders. | A government Order suspending internet in a district under law |
| **Bye-laws** | Rules made by a local or specialised body (a municipal corporation, a housing society, a university) under powers a statute gives that body. | A municipal corporation's building bye-laws; a housing society's bye-laws under the Cooperative Societies Act |
| **Circulars** | Internal clarifications or instructions issued by a department/regulator explaining how it will interpret or apply a law or rule. Persuasive, and binding on the issuing department's own officers, but cannot override the Act. | CBDT/CBIC circulars clarifying tax provisions |
| **Schemes** | Government programmes (often with budgetary support) created to implement a policy — may or may not rest on a specific Act. | Ayushman Bharat, PM-KISAN |
| **Case law / Precedent** | Judgments of the Supreme Court and High Courts. Under Article 141, Supreme Court judgments bind all courts in India. This "law declared by courts" fills gaps and interprets statutes. | The *Kesavananda Bharati* judgment on the "basic structure" of the Constitution |
| **Customary law** | Long-standing local or community custom that courts recognise as having the force of law where no statute covers the point, especially in personal and tribal matters. | Certain tribal inheritance customs recognised under the Fifth/Sixth Schedule areas |
| **Personal law** | Law that applies based on a person's religion for matters like marriage, divorce, succession, and adoption. | Hindu Marriage Act, 1955; Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937; Indian Christian Marriage Act, 1872 |

### The hierarchy — which beats which

1. **The Constitution** — beats everything.
2. **Acts/Statutes passed by Parliament or State Legislatures** (including Codes) — must conform to the Constitution.
3. **Ordinances** — equal in force to an Act while they last, but temporary and must eventually be replaced by an Act or they lapse.
4. **Rules and Regulations** ("subordinate" or "delegated" legislation) — made by the executive (a ministry or regulator) *only because* the parent Act authorises it. If a Rule goes beyond what the Act allows, courts can strike it down as "ultra vires" (beyond power).
5. **Notifications, Orders, Circulars** — used to operationalise Rules/Acts (fix dates, rates, procedures). Circulars cannot override Rules or the Act.
6. **Bye-laws** — narrowest, made by local bodies under a specific statutory permission.

**Why Rules matter as much as the Act itself:** An Act typically states the broad principle ("employers must pay minimum wages"); the Rules made under it specify the actual amounts, forms, timelines, and procedures that affect you day to day. For example, the Act might say GST is payable on goods and services, but the *rates* on your grocery bill come from a **Notification** under GST Rules — Parliament does not vote on each rate. This is why so much of real-world law is Rules and Notifications, not the headline Act you read about in the news. It also means that ministries can change your practical experience of a law substantially — extending deadlines, changing forms, adding compliance steps — without going back to Parliament, as long as they stay within the powers the Act gave them.

**Case law's role:** Where a statute is silent, ambiguous, or arguably unconstitutional, courts step in. Their interpretation becomes part of "the law" going forward — this is why lawyers cite old judgments as authority, not just the bare text of an Act.

## 2. How a law is actually made

### The Central route (Parliament)

1. **Drafting**: A ministry drafts a Bill, usually with the Legislative Department's help.
2. **Pre-legislative consultation**: Under the **Pre-Legislative Consultation Policy, 2014**, ministries are supposed to place the draft Bill in the public domain — with an explanation of why it's needed, its likely costs/benefits, and who it affects — for **at least 30 days**, so citizens and experts can send comments (often via **MyGov.in** or the ministry's own website), and the ministry is expected to publish the comments it received. **In practice this is patchy**: studies by PRS Legislative Research and others have found a large share of Bills introduced in Parliament skip this consultation altogether, and many that are put up for comment don't get the full 30 days. So don't assume every Bill you hear about was open for public comment — check the ministry's website for the specific Bill.
3. **Introduction**: The Bill is introduced in either the **Lok Sabha** (House of the People, directly elected) or the **Rajya Sabha** (Council of States, representing states), except **Money Bills**, which under Article 110 (dealing only with taxation, government borrowing, or spending from the Consolidated Fund) can *only* be introduced in the Lok Sabha, and the Rajya Sabha can merely recommend changes — the Lok Sabha can accept or reject those recommendations, and if the Rajya Sabha does nothing for 14 days, the Bill is deemed passed anyway. This makes Money Bills much faster to push through, which is also why the *classification* of a Bill as a Money Bill is sometimes politically contested.
4. **Committee stage**: Many Bills (though not all — this is discretionary and inconsistent) are referred to a **Standing Committee** (subject-specific, e.g. Home Affairs, Finance) or a **Select/Joint Committee**, which examines the Bill in detail, takes expert and public testimony, and reports back with recommendations. This is usually where the most substantive scrutiny happens, away from the cameras.
5. **Passage**: The Bill must be passed by **both** Houses (Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha) in identical form. If they disagree on a non-Money Bill, a rare **joint sitting** (Article 108) can resolve it.
6. **President's assent**: The passed Bill goes to the **President of India**, who can assent, withhold assent, or (for non-Money Bills) return it once for reconsideration. Once assented, it becomes an **Act**.
7. **Gazette notification**: The Act is published in the **Gazette of India** (now the **e-Gazette**, egazette.gov.in) — this is the official record of the law's existence.
8. **"Coming into force"**: An Act does not necessarily apply immediately. Many Acts state they take effect on a date the government notifies later (commonly written as "shall come into force on such date as the Central Government may, by notification, appoint"). This is a crucial and confusing feature: an Act can be passed and assented to but sit dormant for months or years until "notified into force" — sometimes only in part, section by section. Example: the three new criminal codes (Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam) received presidential assent on 25 December 2023 but were brought into force only from **1 July 2024**, by notification.
9. **Rules framed**: After the Act is in force, the concerned ministry drafts and notifies the **Rules** needed to operate it. Until the Rules exist, an Act can be legally in force but practically un-implementable.

**How long does this take?** There's no fixed timeline. A routine amendment Bill can pass in one session (a few weeks); a major, contested Bill can take years of drafting, committee review, and political negotiation, and then further months or years before the Rules are notified and it's actually usable. As a rough rule of thumb: expect months between "Bill introduced" and "Act notified," and further months between "Act notified" and "Rules ready," unless the government treats it as urgent.

### The Ordinance route (the fast lane)

Under **Article 123** (Centre) and **Article 213** (states), when Parliament or a State Assembly is *not in session* and the government considers immediate action necessary, the **President** (or **Governor**) can promulgate an **Ordinance**. It has the same force as an Act from the moment it's issued — but it is temporary: it **lapses six weeks after Parliament reassembles**, unless Parliament passes it as a regular Act before then, or Parliament passes a resolution disapproving it earlier. Governments sometimes re-issue ordinances back-to-back when a Bill fails to get taken up, a practice the courts have criticised as improper use of the power.

### State laws

State Assemblies follow the same basic sequence (Bill → Assembly → Governor's assent → state Gazette notification → Rules) for subjects in the **State List** and, alongside Parliament, the **Concurrent List** (see Section 8). A state Bill on some subjects (e.g. those affecting the powers of High Courts, or those reserved for the Governor's discretion) can be reserved for the **President's** assent instead of the Governor's.

### How citizens can participate

- **MyGov.in**: The government's crowdsourcing platform for draft policies, Bills and Rules.
- **Ministry websites**: Under the Pre-Legislative Consultation Policy, draft Bills and draft Rules are supposed to be posted for comment (search the specific ministry's site, as there's no single consolidated portal for this).
- **Draft Rules and Regulations**: Regulators like SEBI, RBI, and TRAI routinely publish draft regulations/discussion papers for public comment before finalising them — a citizen or a company can respond directly.
- **Parliamentary Committees**: Standing Committees sometimes invite public submissions and expert testimony — advocacy groups and citizens can seek to depose before them.
- **RTI**: The Right to Information Act, 2005 lets any citizen ask a government department for information related to a law's drafting, implementation data, or reasons for delay.

## 3. Who enforces the law

Enforcement is split across many agencies because policing is largely a **state subject** while several specialised crimes need central, cross-border, or technical machinery.

- **State Police**: The default enforcer for most crimes (theft, assault, most murders, local law and order) — organised, controlled, and funded by each **state government** (Police is Entry 2 of the State List). This is why policing quality, style, and even which acts are aggressively enforced (e.g., liquor laws) vary sharply by state.
- **CBI (Central Bureau of Investigation)**: India's premier federal investigation agency, handling major corruption, high-profile fraud, and cases of national or inter-state significance, or cases a High Court/Supreme Court specifically hands it. Needs either a state's consent (general or case-specific) or a court order to operate within that state, because policing is a state subject.
- **ED (Enforcement Directorate)**: Under the Department of Revenue, Ministry of Finance. Investigates **money laundering** (Prevention of Money Laundering Act) and **foreign exchange violations** (FEMA) — essentially, "follow the money" cases, often tied to other predicate crimes.
- **NIA (National Investigation Agency)**: A specialised central agency for **terrorism and threats to national security**, created after the 2008 Mumbai attacks. Can investigate scheduled offences anywhere in India without needing separate state consent for each case.
- **Income Tax Department**: Enforces the Income-tax Act — assessments, search-and-seizure (raids), and prosecution for tax evasion.
- **GST officers / DGGI**: The Directorate General of GST Intelligence and state/central GST officers enforce GST law, investigate fake invoicing and tax evasion.
- **Municipal bodies**: Enforce local laws — building bye-laws, sanitation, trade licences, property tax, encroachment — through Municipal Corporations/Councils under state Municipal Acts.
- **Sector regulators**, each enforcing rules in their own domain:
  - **RBI** (Reserve Bank of India) — banks, NBFCs, payment systems.
  - **SEBI** (Securities and Exchange Board of India) — stock markets, listed companies, mutual funds.
  - **IRDAI** (Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority) — insurance companies and agents.
  - **TRAI** (Telecom Regulatory Authority of India) — telecom and broadcasting.
  - **CCI** (Competition Commission of India) — anti-competitive practices, mergers.
  - **FSSAI** (Food Safety and Standards Authority of India) — food safety and labelling.
  - **CERC** (Central Electricity Regulatory Commission) — electricity tariffs and inter-state power.
- **Lokpal / Lokayukta**: Anti-corruption ombudsmen — the **Lokpal** at the Centre and **Lokayuktas** in states investigate corruption complaints against public servants (including, under conditions, the Prime Minister).
- **Human Rights Commissions**: The **National Human Rights Commission (NHRC)** and **State Human Rights Commissions (SHRCs)** investigate human rights violations, especially custodial deaths, torture, and abuse by state agencies — but their orders are recommendatory, not directly enforceable like a court's.
- **Specialised statutory commissions**: **National/State Commission for Women**, **National/State Commission for Protection of Child Rights (NCPCR)**, **National Commission for Scheduled Castes**, **National Commission for Scheduled Tribes**, **National Commission for Backward Classes**, and **National Commission for Minorities** — each investigates complaints and recommends action relating to its constituency, and can summon witnesses like a civil court for inquiries, but generally cannot itself punish; it refers matters onward to police/courts/government.

## 4. The court system

India has a single, **integrated judiciary** (unlike the US's separate federal/state court systems) — one hierarchy interprets both central and state laws.

### The ladder

- **Supreme Court of India** (New Delhi): The apex court. Its sanctioned strength stood at **34 judges (including the Chief Justice of India)** for years — but in **May 2026**, Parliament increased this to **38 judges** (33 → 37 puisne judges, plus the CJI) via the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2026, promulgated first as an ordinance, to help tackle the backlog. Its decisions bind every court in the country (Article 141), and it is also the final court of appeal and the guardian of the Constitution (writ jurisdiction under Article 32).
- **25 High Courts**: One or more states/UTs are covered by each High Court (Allahabad HC alone has over 160 sanctioned judge posts — the largest; Sikkim HC has as few as 3 — the smallest). High Courts have both original jurisdiction (for some matters, like certain writ petitions) and appellate jurisdiction over subordinate courts and tribunals in their territory.
- **District & Sessions Courts**: The main trial courts at the district level — "District Court" for civil matters, "Sessions Court" for serious criminal trials. Headed by a District & Sessions Judge.
- **Magistrate Courts**: Below the Sessions Court — Judicial Magistrates (First Class/Second Class) try less serious criminal cases and handle initial stages (remand, bail) of all criminal cases; Executive Magistrates handle certain administrative/law-and-order functions.
- **Gram Nyayalayas**: Village-level courts created under the Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008 to deliver quick, local, informal justice for petty civil and criminal matters. As of **31 October 2025**, only **488 have been notified** across 15 states/UTs and just **333 are actually operational** in 11 states — a scheme that has struggled with implementation despite the law being 17 years old.
- **Lok Adalats** ("People's Courts"): Not regular courts but statutory forums under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987, where cases are settled by **mutual compromise/conciliation**, not adjudicated — awards are final and non-appealable, and court fees already paid are refunded. **National Lok Adalats** are held on fixed dates across the country (four times in 2026 — the 2nd Saturday of March, May, September and December); the first National Lok Adalat of 2025 alone disposed of over **3 crore cases**, settling over Rs 18,000 crore in disputes.
- **Tribunals**: Specialised quasi-judicial bodies for technical areas, meant to be faster than regular courts:
  - **NCLT** (National Company Law Tribunal) — company law, insolvency (IBC).
  - **NGT** (National Green Tribunal) — environmental disputes.
  - **CAT** (Central Administrative Tribunal) — central government service disputes.
  - **ITAT** (Income Tax Appellate Tribunal) — tax appeals.
  - **Consumer Commissions** (District, State, National — under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019): handle consumer complaints, with jurisdiction fixed by the value of goods/services paid — as of 2026, **up to ₹50 lakh** at District level, **₹50 lakh to ₹2 crore** at State level, and **above ₹2 crore** at the National Commission (this counts only the amount paid, excluding claimed compensation/interest).
- **Family Courts**: Set up under the Family Courts Act, 1984 in many cities to handle matrimonial and family disputes (divorce, maintenance, custody) with an emphasis on conciliation.
- **Fast-track and POCSO Courts**: Special courts designated to hear specific categories of cases on priority — fast-track courts for backlog-reduction generally, and dedicated **POCSO courts** for cases under the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012, meant to complete trials within strict time limits and shield child witnesses.

### Civil vs criminal track (in plain text)

**Criminal track** (the state prosecutes an accused for an offence against society):
```
Offence occurs → Complaint/FIR → Police investigation →
Arrest/Bail decision → Chargesheet filed in Magistrate Court →
Committal to Sessions Court (if serious) → Trial (evidence, arguments) →
Judgment (acquittal or conviction+sentence) → Appeal (Sessions→High Court→Supreme Court)
```

**Civil track** (one private party sues another over rights/money/property):
```
Dispute arises → Plaint filed in civil court → Court issues Summons to defendant →
Defendant files Written Statement → Framing of issues → Evidence & cross-examination →
Final arguments → Judgment & Decree → Execution (if the losing side doesn't comply) →
Appeal (District Court/High Court, then Supreme Court)
```

### Pendency — the scale of the problem

According to the **National Judicial Data Grid (NJDG)** and government data reported in 2026, India's courts collectively have around **4.76 crore (47.6 million) pending cases** — including over **63 lakh** in the High Courts, roughly **92,000+** at the Supreme Court, and the bulk (well over 4 crore) in the district/subordinate courts, with Uttar Pradesh alone accounting for over **1.13 crore** pending district-court cases. This backlog is the single most-cited reason both for judicial reforms (more judges, more courts, tribunals) and for the government's push toward Lok Adalats, mediation, and other out-of-court settlement routes described below.

## 5. The journey of a case

### Criminal case (under the new BNSS, in force since 1 July 2024, replacing the CrPC)

1. **Complaint**: A person reports a crime to the police, or complains directly to a Magistrate.
2. **FIR (First Information Report)**: For a **cognizable offence** (a serious crime — e.g. murder, rape, robbery — where police can arrest without a warrant and must investigate on their own), the police are legally bound to register an FIR. For a **non-cognizable offence** (a less serious crime — e.g. simple hurt, defamation — where police need a Magistrate's permission to investigate and cannot arrest without a warrant), the police record it as an NCR/complaint and the person must generally approach a Magistrate directly.
3. **Investigation**: Police gather evidence, question witnesses, and may arrest the accused. Under BNSS, e-FIR filing and use of forensic/video evidence (e.g., through the **eSakshya** app for recording crime-scene video) is now built into the process for many offences.
4. **Arrest and bail**: For a **bailable offence**, the accused has a right to be released on bail as a matter of course. For a **non-bailable offence**, bail is at the court's discretion, weighing factors like flight risk, tampering with evidence, and offence severity.
5. **Chargesheet**: The police must file their final investigation report (chargesheet) with the Magistrate. BNSS builds in statutory outer time limits — commonly cited as **60 or 90 days** depending on the offence's severity, after which, if no chargesheet is filed, the accused becomes entitled to "default bail." A Magistrate is required to decide on taking cognizance of the chargesheet within a set period (commonly cited as **14 days**) under BNSS's new timeline provisions.
6. **Trial**: If the Magistrate takes cognizance and (for serious offences) commits the case to the Sessions Court, a full trial follows — charges are framed, prosecution and defence lead evidence, witnesses are cross-examined, and both sides argue.
7. **Judgment**: The court either acquits or convicts and sentences the accused.
8. **Appeal**: A convicted person (or, in some cases, the state against an acquittal) can appeal — usually Sessions Court → High Court → Supreme Court.

*(Note: exact day-counts under BNSS vary by section and offence type — always check the specific provision or consult a lawyer/legal aid clinic for a live case; the numbers above are the commonly cited general timelines, not a substitute for the bare Act text.)*

### Civil case

1. **Plaint**: The plaintiff (person suing) files a plaint — a formal written statement of the claim — in the appropriate court (jurisdiction depends on the amount claimed and the property/cause of action's location).
2. **Summons**: The court issues summons, formally notifying the defendant and requiring a response.
3. **Written Statement**: The defendant files a written statement responding to each claim.
4. **Framing of issues, evidence**: The court frames the specific questions ("issues") to be decided, then both sides present evidence and witnesses, with cross-examination.
5. **Judgment**: The court delivers a judgment and a decree (the formal, enforceable order).
6. **Execution**: If the losing party doesn't voluntarily comply, the winning party files an execution petition to have the decree enforced (e.g., attaching property, recovering money).
7. **Appeal**: Either side can appeal to a higher court within limitation, typically District Court → High Court → Supreme Court, depending on where the suit started.

Civil cases are typically far slower than the flowchart suggests in practice — years, not months, are common for contested cases, especially where appeals are pursued at every stage.

### Alternative routes (faster and often cheaper than a full trial)

- **Mediation**: A neutral third party helps parties reach a voluntary settlement. The **Mediation Act, 2023** (most provisions in force since **9 October 2023**) gives mediated settlement agreements the same enforceability as a court decree, and for certain civil/commercial disputes even makes a pre-litigation mediation attempt mandatory before going to court.
- **Arbitration**: A private, binding process where an arbitrator (not a judge) decides the dispute, governed by the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 — common in commercial contracts.
- **Lok Adalat**: As described above — quick, informal, compromise-based, and non-appealable (see Section 4).
- **Consumer Commission**: A faster, less formal, lawyer-optional route for consumer disputes about goods/services, with pecuniary limits noted in Section 4.
- **Online Dispute Resolution (ODR)**: A growing set of platforms (often run with fintech/e-commerce companies, banks, and some court e-filing systems) that let parties resolve smaller disputes — loan defaults, e-commerce complaints, cheque bounce — through online mediation/arbitration without physically visiting a forum.

## 6. Digital access to justice

India has built a significant digital layer over the traditional court system:

- **eCourts / eCourts Services Portal**: Lets anyone check case status, cause lists, orders, and judgments online for most courts across the country.
- **NJDG (National Judicial Data Grid)**: A public dashboard of pendency and disposal data for every court, from the Supreme Court down to district courts — the source of the "4.76 crore pending cases" figure above.
- **e-Filing**: Lawyers and, increasingly, litigants can file cases and documents electronically instead of physically at the court registry.
- **Virtual hearings**: Video-conferencing hearings, which expanded hugely during COVID-19 and remain available (and sometimes mandatory) in many courts and tribunals.
- **eSakshya**: A mobile app under the new criminal law framework for police to record and upload crime-scene video/audio evidence in a chain-of-custody-preserving way.
- **e-FIR**: Online filing of first information reports for certain categories of offences (notably vehicle theft and lost property in several states), reducing the need to visit a police station in person.
- **NALSA (National Legal Services Authority)**: The apex body running the whole free-legal-aid ecosystem, down to State Legal Services Authorities (SLSAs) and District Legal Services Authorities (DLSAs) — see Section 7.
- **Tele-Law**: A Department of Justice programme (since 2017) connecting citizens — especially in rural/remote areas — to panel lawyers via video conference from Common Service Centres, mainly for pre-litigation advice.
- **Nyaya Setu**: A newer (launched around 1 January 2026) free, AI-powered legal-assistance chatbot on WhatsApp from the Ministry of Law and Justice, offering 24/7 guidance in multiple Indian languages on family, property, criminal, and civil questions, and acting as a gateway into Tele-Law and the Nyaya Bandhu pro-bono lawyer network for cases that need a human lawyer.
- **DigiLocker**: A government platform to store and share verified digital documents (Aadhaar, PAN, driving licence, educational certificates, etc.) — useful for producing documents in legal and administrative proceedings without physical copies.
- **India Code**: The official government portal/app (indiacode.nic.in) hosting the authoritative, updated text of central Acts and, increasingly, state Acts — the closest thing to a single source of truth for statute text.
- **eGazette**: The official portal (egazette.gov.in) for the Gazette of India, where every central notification, Rule, and Act is legally published.
- **SUVAS / e-SCR**: The Supreme Court's AI-assisted translation initiative (Supreme Court Vidhik Anuvaad Software) that translates judgments from English into regional languages, published on the **e-SCR** portal. As of around March 2025, over **36,000 judgments had been translated into Hindi** and roughly **47,000 more into other regional languages**, with the system now covering **16+ Indian languages** (having grown from an initial 9); separate reporting has referenced translation into "18 vernacular languages" as a Supreme Court–High Court collaboration goal. **Note: exact current figures change frequently as translation continues — treat the counts above as approximate, sourced to reports from 2024-25, not a real-time count.**

## 7. Legal aid — who gets it free, and how

Under Article 39A of the Constitution (equal justice and free legal aid) and the **Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987**, India runs a nationwide free legal aid system through **NALSA** (national), **State Legal Services Authorities**, **District Legal Services Authorities**, and **Taluk Legal Services Committees**.

**Section 12 of the Act** entitles the following categories to free legal services (subject to the authority being satisfied there's a genuine case to pursue or defend):
- Members of **Scheduled Castes or Scheduled Tribes**
- **Women and children** (a woman is eligible regardless of her income; a child is eligible until the age of majority, 18 years)
- Persons with disabilities
- Victims of **trafficking or forced labour** (beggars, per the Act's wording)
- Industrial workmen
- Persons in **custody**, including in a protective home, juvenile home, psychiatric hospital, or similar institution
- Victims of a **mass disaster, ethnic violence, caste atrocity, flood, drought, earthquake, or industrial disaster**
- Persons with an **annual income below a limit set by the respective State Government** for cases in any court other than the Supreme Court, and **below ₹5 lakh** for cases before the Supreme Court itself
- Persons in cases related to a **Union Territory calamity** or in specified situations

**How to apply**: Approach the local **District Legal Services Authority (DLSA)** (usually located in the district court complex), a **Taluk Legal Services Committee**, or a legal aid clinic, with proof of your eligible category and a short statement of the case; a panel lawyer is then assigned free of charge. You can also dial the toll-free **NALSA helpline 15100** — an IVR-based, multi-lingual service available 24/7 that can connect you to your DLSA, offer legal advice, arrange a panel advocate, help with mediation, or let you check the status of an existing legal-aid application. There is no fee for applying.

**Lok Adalat statistics** (context on scale): the first National Lok Adalat of 2025 alone disposed of over **3.09 crore cases** (about 2.58 crore pre-litigation and 50.8 lakh pending cases) at a **79% disposal rate**, settling disputes worth over **₹18,212 crore**; across 2023–2025, twelve National Lok Adalats together disposed of roughly **26 crore cases**. National Lok Adalats are scheduled on fixed Saturdays four times in 2026 (March, May, September, December).

## 8. Federal division: why the same "offence" plays out differently by state

India's Constitution divides law-making power via the **Seventh Schedule** into three lists:

- **Union List** (~100 subjects): Parliament's exclusive domain — defence, foreign affairs, currency, banking, railways, atomic energy, etc.
- **State List** (~61 subjects): State Legislatures' exclusive domain — **police**, **public order**, **land**, agriculture, **liquor** ("intoxicating liquors" — production, possession, sale, transport, taxation), local government, public health.
- **Concurrent List** (~52 subjects): Both Parliament and State Legislatures can legislate — criminal law and procedure, marriage and divorce, contracts, education, forests, economic and social planning. Where both a central and a state law conflict on a Concurrent List subject, the central law generally prevails (Article 254), unless the state law had received Presidential assent.

**Why this matters practically**: because **police, land, and liquor are State List subjects**, the same act — say, drinking in public, or a land transaction — can be legal, restricted, or entirely banned depending on which state you're in, and enforced with very different intensity, because each state runs its own police force and its own excise/land administration.

**Well-known state-specific laws citizens actually run into**:
- **Rent control**: e.g., the Maharashtra Rent Control Act, 1999 — rent, eviction, and tenancy rules differ significantly state to state.
- **Shops and establishments**: e.g., the Karnataka Shops and Commercial Establishments Act — working hours, weekly holidays, and registration requirements for any shop/office vary by state.
- **Land reforms**: e.g., the Tamil Nadu Land Reforms (Fixation of Ceiling on Land) Act — ceiling limits and tenancy protections differ by state.
- **State police acts**: e.g., the Delhi Police Act, 1978 — police powers, discipline and organisation are set by each state's own Police Act (or in some states, older colonial-era Police Acts still apply).
- **Prohibition/liquor laws**: several states (e.g., Gujarat, Bihar) have full or partial prohibition; others regulate but permit sale — a completely legal drink in one state can be a criminal offence in another.
- **Anti-conversion (\"Freedom of Religion\") laws**: several states (e.g., Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat, Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh) have enacted laws restricting religious conversion by "force, fraud, or allurement" — with varying definitions and penalties; no equivalent central law exists.
- **Cow protection/slaughter laws**: most states have their own cattle-slaughter/cow-protection Acts with widely varying scope and penalties, since agriculture/animal husbandry sit with states.
- **Stamp duty**: charged on property and other transactions under the (central) Indian Stamp Act, 1899, but **rates are fixed and amended by each state**, so registering the same value of property costs very different stamp duty in Maharashtra versus, say, Telangana.
- **RTE fee reimbursement**: the (central) Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 mandates 25% reservation for disadvantaged children in private schools, but the **fee-reimbursement formula and implementation rules are set by each state**, producing very different real-world experiences for parents.
- **Right to Public Services Acts**: roughly **20 states** have enacted their own "right to service" laws guaranteeing citizens specific government services within a fixed time limit, with penalties on defaulting officials — e.g., **Karnataka's Sakala** (Karnataka Sakala Services Act, 2011 — over 1,249 notified services as of October 2024), **Uttar Pradesh's Janhit Guarantee Adhiniyam**, **Bihar's, Rajasthan's, Punjab's, Delhi's, Odisha's, Jharkhand's, Himachal's, Uttarakhand's, Chhattisgarh's, and J&K's** equivalents (most enacted around 2011). There is no single central Right to Public Services law — each state's Act, timelines, and covered services differ.

## 9. The language of law — and why it's so hard to read

**Which language is a law "official" in?** Under Article 348, the **authoritative text** of central Acts, Ordinances, Bills, and the Constitution is the **English** version, even though Hindi translations (and, informally, other regional-language translations) are also published for convenience. State legislatures can conduct business and pass some laws in the state's official regional language, but the English text generally remains authoritative in courts unless the President has specifically directed otherwise for a particular state law's Hindi text.

**Why ordinary citizens find law hard to understand:**
- Many Acts are old, drafted in dense **colonial-era legal English** (long sentences, archaic phrasing like "notwithstanding anything contained herein," heavy cross-referencing between sections).
- The real operative detail often sits in **Rules, Regulations, Notifications, and Circulars** scattered across ministry websites and Gazette issues — not conveniently collected in one place with the parent Act.
- **Amendments** are frequently issued as stand-alone amending Acts that a reader must manually cross-apply to the original text — unless they're using an updated, consolidated version (like India Code aims to provide).
- Law students and lawyers are trained in this idiom, creating an expertise barrier that makes plain law feel inaccessible without professional help.

**What's being done about it:**
- **Plain language drafting**: **Vidhi Centre for Legal Policy** published a **Manual on Plain Language Drafting** (2017) and its updated **"SARAL Manual"** (2023), aimed at giving drafters (including the government's own **Legislative Department**) an actionable guide to eliminate legalese from new Indian laws; the Legislative Department itself states it is committed to "simple, plain, precise and unambiguous" drafting going forward.
- **Law Commission of India**: Has periodically recommended **consolidation** (merging overlapping/duplicate laws into single codes) and **repeal of obsolete laws** — a push that has, over the years, resulted in the repeal of hundreds of outdated Acts.
- **Codification**: The 2023–24 replacement of the IPC/CrPC/Evidence Act with the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita/Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita/Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam is itself framed partly as a modernisation and consolidation exercise (though its plain-language success is debated).
- **PRS Legislative Research**: An independent nonprofit that publishes accessible, non-partisan explainers ("Bill Tracks," legislative briefs) on pending and passed Bills, widely used by journalists, MPs, and citizens to understand what a Bill actually does.
- **Nyaaya (Vidhi Centre for Legal Policy)**: A public-facing platform (nyaaya.org), started in 2016, that translates central and state laws affecting citizens' everyday rights into simple, layperson-friendly explainers.
- **NALSA legal literacy clubs**: NALSA runs legal literacy and legal awareness clubs, particularly in schools and colleges, to build early familiarity with rights and remedies.
- **Legal literacy under NEP**: The National Education Policy 2020 framework has encouraged integrating basic legal literacy/civic and constitutional awareness into school curricula, though implementation varies by state and board.
- **"Constitution at 75" and similar public-outreach initiatives**: Around the Constitution's 75th anniversary (2024–25), the government and judiciary ran outreach campaigns (exhibitions, translated materials, digital content) aimed at improving public understanding of constitutional rights and institutions.
- **Single-portal efforts**: **India Code** (for consolidated statute text) and **eGazette** (for notifications) are steps toward a "one place to check the law" ideal, though Rules, Circulars, and state-specific amendments are still not fully unified in one search-friendly system — a gap experts continue to flag as needing a genuinely single, updated, plain-language legal portal for citizens.
