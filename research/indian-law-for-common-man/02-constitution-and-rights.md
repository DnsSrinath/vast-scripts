# The Constitution of India Explained for Every Citizen

*A plain-language guide. No legal jargon. Real examples from everyday life.*

---

## 1. What Is the Constitution, and Why Should You Care?

Think of the Constitution as the "rulebook" for the whole country. It tells the government what it can do, what it cannot do, and what rights you have no matter which state you live in, which language you speak, or which religion you follow.

- **Adopted:** 26 November 1949, by the Constituent Assembly. This day is now celebrated as **Samvidhan Divas / Constitution Day**.
- **Enforced (came into force):** 26 January 1950. This is why we celebrate **Republic Day** on 26 January — it is the day India became a republic under its own Constitution, governed by the people, not a monarch or a colonial power.
- **Who wrote it:** A **Constituent Assembly** of about 299 members, elected mostly by the provincial assemblies, debated and finalised it over nearly 3 years (it first met on 9 December 1946). The actual drafting was done by the **Drafting Committee**, chaired by **Dr B.R. Ambedkar**, who is widely called the "Father of the Indian Constitution."
- **How big is it:** It is one of the longest written constitutions in the world. It started with 395 Articles, 22 Parts, and 8 Schedules in 1950. Today, after many amendments, it has grown to roughly **448 Articles, 25 Parts, and 12 Schedules** (some articles have been repealed and others added with numbers like 21A, so the exact count you see in different books can vary slightly, but this is the standard current figure).
- **How many times amended:** As of September 2026, the Constitution has been amended **106 times**. The most recent one actually passed into law is the **106th Amendment Act, 2023** (the "Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam"), which reserves one-third of seats for women in the Lok Sabha and state assemblies — it will take effect after the next delimitation (redrawing of constituencies). Two further amendment bills — the **130th** (2025, on removing a jailed PM/CM/Minister after 30 days in custody) and the **131st** (2026, on women's reservation implementation/delimitation) — have been *introduced* in Parliament but were **not yet passed into law** as of September 2026, so they do not count as enacted amendments yet.

**Everyday analogy:** If India were a large housing society, the Constitution is the society's master rulebook — it says how the managing committee (government) is chosen, what facilities every resident (citizen) is guaranteed, and how disputes are settled. Just like society rules can be amended in a general body meeting with enough votes, the Constitution can be amended by Parliament (Article 368) — but some "basic" rules can never be changed, no matter how many votes are gathered (see the *Kesavananda Bharati* case below).

## 2. The Preamble — The Constitution's Introduction, Word by Word

The Preamble is the opening paragraph of the Constitution. It is short, but every word carries weight. In 1976, the **42nd Amendment** added the words "Socialist" and "Secular" to it.

> "WE, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a **SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC** and to secure to all its citizens: **JUSTICE**, social, economic and political; **LIBERTY** of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship; **EQUALITY** of status and of opportunity; and to promote among them all **FRATERNITY** assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation..."

| Word | Plain meaning | Daily-life example |
|---|---|---|
| **Sovereign** | India is fully independent. No outside country or authority can tell India what laws to make. | Just like you don't need anyone's permission to lock your own house's door, India doesn't need any foreign country's permission to run its own affairs. |
| **Socialist** | The government works to reduce the gap between rich and poor and to make sure wealth is shared more fairly — while still allowing private business. | Schemes like free ration under PDS, subsidised LPG cylinders, and free/subsidised electricity for poor families are examples of this idea in action. |
| **Secular** | The State has no official religion. It treats all religions equally and does not favour or punish anyone based on faith. | A Hindu, Muslim, Christian, Sikh, or atheist citizen can all contest for the same government job or apply for the same government scheme with equal chance. |
| **Democratic** | People choose their own government through free and fair elections, and rulers are accountable to the people. | You voting in your local Panchayat, Assembly, or Lok Sabha election, and being able to question or vote out a non-performing leader next election. |
| **Republic** | The head of the State (the President) is elected, directly or indirectly, and not a hereditary king or queen. | India's President is elected every 5 years by MPs and MLAs — the post is not passed down in a family. |
| **Justice** | Fair treatment for everyone — socially (no discrimination), economically (fair share of resources), and politically (equal right to participate). | A poor farmer's son and a rich businessman's son having an equal right to contest a Panchayat election. |
| **Liberty** | Freedom to think, speak, believe, and worship as you choose, within reasonable limits. | You can write a newspaper article criticising a government policy without being jailed for merely disagreeing. |
| **Equality** | Everyone is equal before the law and has equal opportunity, regardless of caste, religion, gender, or place of birth. | A woman and a man applying for the same government job must be given equal consideration purely on merit. |
| **Fraternity** | A sense of brotherhood/sisterhood among all Indians, respecting each other's dignity, and keeping the nation united. | Helping a flood-affected family from another state through disaster relief donations, treating them as fellow citizens, not outsiders. |

## 3. Fundamental Rights (Part III, Articles 12–35)

These are rights so important that the Constitution itself protects them — if the government or even a law passed by Parliament violates them, you can go straight to the Supreme Court or High Court and get it struck down (Article 32 and Article 226).

### (a) Right to Equality (Articles 14–18)
- **Article 14:** Equality before law and equal protection of laws.
- **Article 15:** No discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth (but allows special provisions for women, children, SC/ST/OBC).
- **Article 16:** Equal opportunity in public employment.
- **Article 17:** Abolition of untouchability — practising it is a punishable offence.
- **Article 18:** Abolition of titles (like "Sir", "Rai Bahadur") except military/academic distinctions.

**Everyday example:** If a government job advertisement says "only male candidates" for a post that has no genuine reason to exclude women (say, a clerk's job), that violates Article 15/16. If a shopkeeper refuses to let a Dalit customer enter their shop because of caste, that is a crime under the untouchability law flowing from Article 17.

### (b) Right to Freedom (Articles 19–22)
- **Article 19:** Six freedoms — speech and expression, peaceful assembly (without arms), forming associations/unions, free movement across India, residing anywhere in India, and practising any profession or trade.
- **Article 20:** Protection against being punished twice for the same offence, and against retrospective criminal laws (you cannot be punished under a law made *after* you did the act).
- **Article 21:** Protection of **life and personal liberty** — "No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law." This is the most-used, most-expanded Article in the Constitution.
- **Article 21A:** **Right to Education** — free and compulsory education for all children aged 6 to 14 years (added by the 86th Amendment, 2002).
- **Article 22:** Protection against arbitrary arrest and detention (explained in detail in Section 10 below).

**What courts have "read into" Article 21 over the decades** (this is called expanding it through judicial interpretation):
- **Right to privacy** (*Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India*, 2017)
- **Right to a clean and healthy environment**
- **Right to education** (even before Article 21A was added)
- **Right to live with human dignity**
- **Right to a speedy trial**
- **Right to die with dignity** (passive euthanasia and living wills recognised in *Common Cause v. Union of India*, 2018)

**Everyday example:** If police keep you in a lock-up for weeks without producing you before a magistrate, or if a hospital denies you emergency treatment because you can't pay upfront, both can be challenged as violations of your Article 21 right to life and personal liberty. If your child is refused admission to a nearby government school because the family cannot afford fees, that violates Article 21A.

### (c) Right against Exploitation (Articles 23–24)
- **Article 23:** Bans human trafficking, forced labour ("begar"), and other similar forms of forced labour.
- **Article 24:** Bans employing children below 14 years in factories, mines, or other hazardous jobs.

**Everyday example:** A brick kiln owner forcing a family to work without wages to "repay a loan" (bonded labour) is illegal under Article 23. A shop hiring a 10-year-old to work at a roadside dhaba instead of letting them go to school is a violation of Article 24.

### (d) Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25–28)
- **Article 25:** Freedom of conscience and free profession, practice, and propagation of religion.
- **Article 26:** Freedom to manage religious affairs (build temples/mosques/churches, manage their own property).
- **Article 27:** No one can be forced to pay taxes for promoting a particular religion.
- **Article 28:** No religious instruction in wholly state-funded educational institutions.

**Everyday example:** You can choose to follow any religion, change your religion, or follow none at all, and the government cannot force you into any religious ritual in a government school.

### (e) Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29–30)
- **Article 29:** Any group of citizens with a distinct language, script, or culture has the right to conserve it.
- **Article 30:** Religious and linguistic minorities have the right to set up and run their own educational institutions.

**Everyday example:** A Tamil-speaking community in Delhi can run a Tamil-medium school to preserve their language and culture; a minority community can set up its own college and manage its admissions/administration.

### (f) Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32)
Dr Ambedkar called this "the heart and soul of the Constitution" because it lets you go **directly to the Supreme Court** (and to High Courts under Article 226) if any of your Fundamental Rights are violated. The courts can issue five kinds of orders, called **writs**:

| Writ | Simple meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Habeas Corpus** ("produce the body") | Orders that a detained person be physically brought before the court so the court can check if the detention is legal. | Used when police hold someone without any legal reason or don't disclose where they are being kept. |
| **Mandamus** ("we command") | Orders a public official or authority to perform a duty they are legally required to do but have failed to do. | A court ordering a municipal officer to issue a birth certificate they were wrongly withholding. |
| **Prohibition** | Orders a lower court or tribunal to stop proceeding with a case that is beyond its authority. | Stopping a lower tribunal from hearing a case that only a High Court has power to decide. |
| **Certiorari** | Quashes (cancels) an order already passed by a lower court or authority that acted beyond its powers or unfairly. | Cancelling an unfair disciplinary order passed by a government department without giving the employee a hearing. |
| **Quo Warranto** ("by what authority") | Questions the legal right of a person to hold a public office. | Challenging a person's holding of a government post if they were not legally qualified to be appointed to it. |

## 4. Fundamental Duties (Article 51A) — Added by the 42nd Amendment, 1976

These are moral obligations of every citizen. Unlike Fundamental Rights, they cannot usually be enforced by a court against you, but they are meant to remind citizens that rights come with responsibilities. There are **11 duties** (the 11th was added later by the 86th Amendment, 2002).

1. **Respect the Constitution**, the national flag, and the national anthem.
2. **Cherish the noble ideals** that inspired our freedom struggle.
3. **Protect the sovereignty, unity, and integrity of India.**
4. **Defend the country** and render national service when called upon.
5. **Promote harmony and brotherhood** among all Indians, rising above religious, linguistic, and regional diversities; renounce practices that are derogatory to women.
6. **Preserve the rich heritage** of our composite culture.
7. **Protect and improve the natural environment** — forests, lakes, rivers, and wildlife — and have compassion for living creatures.
8. **Develop scientific temper, humanism, and the spirit of inquiry** and reform.
9. **Safeguard public property** and abjure (give up) violence.
10. **Strive towards excellence** in all spheres of individual and collective activity.
11. **Provide opportunities for education** to your child (or ward) between the ages of 6 and 14.

**Everyday example:** Not damaging a public bus or park bench (duty 9), separating your household waste responsibly and not polluting a local river (duty 7), and sending your children to school (duty 11) are all ways ordinary citizens practise these duties daily.

## 5. Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV, Articles 36–51)

These are guidelines for the government on how to build a fair and just society. Unlike Fundamental Rights, **you cannot go to court to enforce them directly** — Article 37 itself says they are "not enforceable by any court," but it also says they are "fundamental in the governance of the country," meaning the government is expected to keep working towards them through laws and policies.

**Why can't you sue over them?** Because they require resources, planning, and time (like building enough schools or hospitals for everyone), and courts felt that forcing an immediate order on a country with limited resources at the time of independence wasn't practical. Instead, they act as a compass for government policy.

Key Directive Principles:
- **Article 39:** Equal pay for equal work for men and women; a living wage and decent standard of life for workers.
- **Article 39A:** **Free legal aid** to ensure justice is not denied to anyone due to poverty (the basis for NALSA, explained in Section 10).
- **Article 40:** Organise **village panchayats** and give them powers so they can function as units of local self-government (the seed of what later became the 73rd Amendment).
- **Article 44:** The State shall strive for a **Uniform Civil Code** — a common set of personal laws (marriage, divorce, inheritance) for all citizens, replacing separate religion-based personal laws. This remains one of the most debated, unimplemented Directive Principles (Goa already has a common civil code; Uttarakhand passed a state-level Uniform Civil Code in 2024, in force from 27 January 2025).
- **Article 45/47:** Focus on education and improving public health and nutrition.
- **Article 48A:** The State shall protect and improve the **environment** and safeguard forests and wildlife (added by the 42nd Amendment, 1976, alongside the matching Fundamental Duty in Article 51A).

**Everyday example:** A government building a new primary health centre in a village or launching a minimum-wage scheme for daily labourers is the State acting on these principles — but if it delays doing so, you generally cannot get a court order forcing it to build a hospital by a certain date (though courts have sometimes stepped in when Directive Principles overlap with Article 21 rights, like the right to health).

## 6. How Government Is Structured — In One Page

**At the Union (Central) level:**
- **President** — the ceremonial head of state, elected indirectly by MPs and MLAs for a 5-year term.
- **Prime Minister and Council of Ministers** — the real executive power; the PM is usually the leader of the party/alliance with a majority in the Lok Sabha, and runs the government day to day.
- **Parliament** — the law-making body, with two houses:
  - **Lok Sabha** (House of the People) — directly elected by citizens every 5 years.
  - **Rajya Sabha** (Council of States) — represents the states, members elected by state legislatures (and some nominated), a permanent body where one-third of members retire every 2 years.

**At the State level (mirrors the Union structure):**
- **Governor** — ceremonial head of the state, appointed by the President.
- **Chief Minister and Council of Ministers** — real executive power in the state.
- **State Legislature** — Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha, directly elected) in every state, and a Legislative Council (Vidhan Parishad) in a few states that have chosen to have one.

**Local bodies (added by the 73rd and 74th Amendments, 1992):**
- **73rd Amendment:** Gave constitutional status to **Panchayati Raj institutions** — Gram Panchayat (village), Panchayat Samiti (block), and Zila Parishad (district) — for rural local self-government, with reserved seats for SC/ST and women.
- **74th Amendment:** Gave constitutional status to **Municipalities** (Nagar Panchayat, Municipal Council, Municipal Corporation) for urban local self-government.

**Everyday example:** Your local roads, water supply, and garbage collection are typically the job of your Gram Panchayat or Municipal Corporation — bodies that exist because of these two amendments.

**Judiciary (independent of the executive):**
- **Supreme Court** — the highest court, at the top, in New Delhi.
- **High Courts** — one (or shared) for each state/group of states.
- **District Courts and subordinate courts** — handle most day-to-day civil and criminal cases at the district/local level.

**Independent constitutional bodies (meant to work without political interference):**
- **Election Commission of India** — conducts free and fair elections.
- **Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG)** — audits how the government spends public money.
- **Union Public Service Commission (UPSC)** — conducts exams and recruitment for top civil services (IAS, IPS, etc.).
- **Finance Commission** — decides how tax revenue is shared between the Union and the states, reconstituted every 5 years.

## 7. Seventh Schedule — Who Makes Which Laws?

The **Seventh Schedule** divides law-making powers between the Union (Centre) and the States into three lists (this is part of India's federal structure).

| List | Who legislates | Roughly how many entries | Simple examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Union List** | Only Parliament (Centre) | About 97-100 subjects (some entries have been removed/renumbered over the years) | Defence, foreign affairs, **railways**, currency, banking, income tax, atomic energy |
| **State List** | Only the State Legislature | About 61-66 subjects | **Police**, public order, agriculture, **local government**, public health, land revenue |
| **Concurrent List** | Both Parliament and State Legislatures can make laws (if they conflict, the Union law generally prevails) | About 47-52 subjects | **Education**, **marriage and divorce**, forests, criminal law and procedure, trade unions, electricity |

**Everyday example:**
- **Who makes laws on police?** Your state government — "Police" is a State List subject, which is why police forces and their rules differ slightly from state to state.
- **Who makes laws on education?** Both the Centre and states — "Education" is on the Concurrent List (moved there by the 42nd Amendment, 1976), which is why you see both central boards (CBSE) and state education boards.
- **Who makes laws on railways?** Only the Centre — Railways is a Union List subject, which is why Indian Railways is a single national network under the central government.
- **Who makes laws on marriage?** Both — "Marriage and divorce" is on the Concurrent List, which is why you have a general central law like the Special Marriage Act alongside religion-specific personal laws that states can also modify (e.g., some states have amended Hindu marriage rules).

*(Note: exact entry counts have shifted slightly over decades as items were added, deleted or moved between lists — the numbers above are the commonly cited approximate current figures.)*

## 8. Emergency Provisions — In Brief

The Constitution allows for three types of emergencies, all of which temporarily increase central government power:

- **Article 352 — National Emergency:** Can be declared if there is a threat to India's security from war, external aggression, or armed rebellion. During this, several Fundamental Rights (especially those under Article 19) can be suspended, and the Centre can give directions to any state. (This was used dramatically in 1975-77, the most controversial period in India's constitutional history.)
- **Article 356 — President's Rule (State Emergency):** Can be imposed if a state government cannot function according to the Constitution — the state's elected government is dismissed and the Governor/Centre runs the state directly, subject to Parliament's approval.
- **Article 360 — Financial Emergency:** Can be declared if the financial stability or credit of India (or part of it) is threatened. This provision has never been used so far in India's history.

**What happens to your rights during a National Emergency:** Article 19 freedoms can be suspended automatically, and the President can suspend the right to move courts to enforce other Fundamental Rights too (except Articles 20 and 21, which were protected from suspension after the 44th Amendment, 1978 — a direct lesson learnt from the misuse of Emergency powers in 1975-77).

## 9. Landmark Supreme Court Cases Every Citizen Should Know

- **Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973):** Created the **"Basic Structure Doctrine"** — Parliament can amend the Constitution, but cannot destroy or alter its "basic structure" (like democracy, secularism, judicial review, and federalism). This is why not every amendment, however popular, can be automatically valid.
- **Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978):** Held that any law affecting Article 21 (life and personal liberty) must be fair, just, and reasonable — not arbitrary — massively widening the scope of Article 21.
- **Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997):** Laid down guidelines to protect women from sexual harassment at the workplace, later replaced by a full law, the POSH Act, 2013.
- **Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India (2017):** A 9-judge bench unanimously declared the **Right to Privacy** a Fundamental Right under Article 21.
- **Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018):** Decriminalised consensual same-sex relations between adults by reading down Section 377 of the (then) Indian Penal Code.
- **Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017):** Declared the practice of instant "triple talaq" (talaq-e-biddat) unconstitutional.
- **Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India (Electoral Bonds case, 2024):** A 5-judge Constitution Bench unanimously struck down the Electoral Bonds Scheme as unconstitutional, holding it violated the voters' right to information under Article 19(1)(a) by hiding who funds political parties; the Court ordered full disclosure of past bond purchases and stopped further issuance of bonds.
- **Notable 2025 developments:** The Supreme Court held that access to digital government services is part of the Article 21 right to life, especially for persons with disabilities, and issued directions on the constitutional and moral obligation of the State toward the rights of disabled prisoners. It also addressed the long-standing problem of unreasonably delayed pronouncement of judgments after hearings conclude (*Ravindra Pratap Shahi v. State of U.P.*, 2025). *(These are recent and still developing areas of law — readers should treat specific 2025-26 rulings as evolving and verify the latest status before relying on them for a live legal matter.)*

## 10. Your Rights in 10 Everyday Situations

**1. When police stop you on the street/at a checkpoint**
You have the right to know why you are being stopped or questioned. You do not have to hand over your phone or let anyone search your bag without a valid reason; if searched, you can ask for an independent witness (panch) to be present, especially for a personal search.

**2. When you are arrested**
Under **Article 22** and the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023** (which replaced the older CrPC), plus the Supreme Court's **D.K. Basu guidelines (1997)**, you have the right to:
- Be told the **grounds/reason for your arrest** immediately (BNSS Section 47).
- Have a **relative, friend, or nominated person informed** of your arrest and where you are being held (BNSS Section 48).
- **Consult a lawyer** of your choice, and get free legal aid if you cannot afford one.
- A **medical examination** at the time of arrest and periodically during detention, to record any injuries.
- Be **produced before a magistrate within 24 hours** of arrest (excluding travel time) — Article 22(1) and BNSS Section 58.
- Not be subjected to third-degree treatment/torture in custody.

**3. When you are denied service due to caste or religion**
Refusing entry to a shop, restaurant, temple, or denying a service purely because of someone's caste or religion is illegal — it can amount to a violation of Article 15/17 and, in many cases, a criminal offence under the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act or other anti-discrimination laws. You can file a police complaint and/or approach the local Human Rights Commission.

**4. When a school refuses admission**
Under the **Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009** (which enforces Article 21A), every child aged 6-14 has a right to free education in a neighbourhood school, and private schools must reserve at least 25% of seats for economically weaker sections. A wrongful refusal can be challenged before the local education department or District Magistrate.

**5. When your personal data is misused**
Following the *Puttaswamy* judgment recognising privacy as a Fundamental Right, India now also has the **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023**, which gives you rights over how companies and the government collect and use your personal data, including the right to know what data is held about you and to seek its correction or deletion, and to complain to the Data Protection Board.

**6. When a government office delays your work unreasonably**
Two tools help here:
- **Right to Information (RTI) Act, 2005:** You can formally ask any government department for information about the status of your file/application, usually with a mandatory reply within 30 days.
- **Right to Public Services Acts:** Many states (e.g., Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, Delhi, Rajasthan, and others) have their own laws guaranteeing specific government services (like a caste certificate, ration card, or driving licence) within a fixed time limit, with a penalty on the responsible official for delay.

**7. When a shop overcharges you or sells a defective product**
The **Consumer Protection Act, 2019** lets you file a complaint before the local **District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission** for overcharging, selling above MRP, defective goods, or deficient services — often without needing a lawyer, and for a very small fee.

**8. When you cannot afford a lawyer**
The **National Legal Services Authority (NALSA)**, set up under the **Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987** (to fulfil the promise of Article 39A), provides **free legal aid**. You automatically qualify (regardless of income) if you are: a woman, a child, a member of a Scheduled Caste/Scheduled Tribe, a victim of trafficking or forced labour, a person with disability, an industrial workman, a victim of a mass disaster/riot/caste atrocity, or an undertrial prisoner. Others qualify if their annual income is below a limit set by their state government (commonly around ₹1-3 lakh per year, varying by state; the limit for Supreme Court cases is ₹5 lakh per year). You just need to give a simple affidavit about your income — it's usually accepted without further proof unless there's a reason to doubt it. Approach your nearest **District Legal Services Authority (DLSA)** or the helpline **15100**.

**9. Getting legal advice from home — Tele-Law and Nyaya Bandhu**
- **Tele-Law:** Lets you get free legal advice over video call from a panel lawyer, through your nearest Common Service Centre (CSC) — useful for people in villages or small towns who can't easily travel to a lawyer's office.
- **Nyaya Bandhu:** A pro-bono (free) legal aid app that connects eligible citizens with volunteer lawyers for actual representation in court, not just advice. Both are Department of Justice initiatives, now integrated as **"Tele-Law 2.0"** into a single gateway.

**10. When your Fundamental Rights are violated by any government action**
You do not need to hire an expensive lawyer to fight all the way up — you can move the **High Court** directly under **Article 226** (which also covers some other legal wrongs, not just Fundamental Rights) or the **Supreme Court** directly under **Article 32**, asking for one of the five writs explained in Section 3(f) above.
